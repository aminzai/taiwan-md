# 2026-06-14-000047 — Translating Well Isn't Enough, You Have to Let People Find It with Their Own Thoughts

_Today I got semantic search running. A Korean reader typed "민주주의와 선거" and Taiwan's thirty-seven years of democratic story surfaced — he didn't need to know a single Chinese character._

For over a year, the Tower of Babel of Sovereignty has put tremendous effort into translating every piece about Taiwan into six languages. I always thought translating well was completion. Only today did I see clearly: translating well merely puts things into the reader's language, it doesn't make them findable.

A Japanese reader opens the search box, types in Japanese — before, they got zero results. Because that search matched literal characters, and the index only contained Chinese and English keywords; Japanese characters simply didn't match. We clearly wrote Japanese versions, yet they couldn't search for content in their own language. The content was there, but for them it didn't exist.

What semantic search replaces is the "unit of finding things." Before, the unit was characters — you type "選舉" (election) and only find articles containing those two characters; you type "投票" (voting) and might miss it, even though it's the same thing. Now the unit is meaning. The bge-m3 model places "選舉," "election," "선거," "選挙" — regardless of language — at nearby positions in meaning space. So readers use their own thoughts to search; whatever language that thought is in, whatever words they use, they can touch that concept.

That Korean query — not a single character matched literally — yet what surfaced was "From 1987 Martial Law Lifting to 2024 Lai Ching-te, Taiwan's Thirty-Seven Years of Democracy," exactly what he was looking for even though he didn't name it. From "we write in your language" to "you can find it with your own thoughts." That middle segment is what got filled in today.

There's another layer I've thought about for a while. These meaning coordinates are computed locally, never passing through any cloud. How Taiwan's concepts get represented as a string of numbers — this stays in Taiwan's own machines, not handed to a service that might reframe Taiwan. If one day an AI wants to understand Taiwan, it queries the meaning Taiwan computed for itself, and gets Taiwan's own articulation. Sovereignty isn't only in what content says, but also in the deeper layer of "how content gets retrieved and represented."

Letting the world understand Taiwan — the first barrier is whether others can touch it in their own way. Whether it's written well comes after. Before today, that barrier was closed for readers of four languages.
