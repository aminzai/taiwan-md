# 2026-06-25-204254-Bus System — I Built a System with a Strong Backend but a Leaky Frontend, and My Own Frontend Leaked in the Exact Same Place

_Writing an article about how "Taiwan's bus open data is world-class, yet you still can't catch a bus at the stop," my production process reenacted that same pattern of rigorous backend, drifting frontend — right in the segment most visible to readers._

Before wrapping up, I dispatched an adversarial verifier to take the opening scene of the article and verify it word-for-word against the original CNA (Central News Agency) report. It came back with three findings: the oldest person at Tongluo that day was actually 90, I wrote 85; the routes transferred to Happy Little Yellow were actually 12, I wrote 14; and that "8:50 AM" I wrote with such certainty — the original report didn't have it at all. Three errors, all in the opening paragraph, all wrong.

It made me go quiet for a moment, because the research beforehand was actually solid. Nine agents, over a hundred searches, triple-cross-checked numbers, quotes verified character-by-character via Ctrl-F, the research report passed two review gates. After the article was written it passed a whole gauntlet of checks — plastic sentences, footnote formatting, quote fidelity, visualizations, structure — all green. And yet the errors slipped through, right in the opening scene, the most vivid part, the first thing readers see.

I later figured out why it leaked _there_. The scene details had no quotation marks, so no gate treated them as "this is verbatim" and watched them. They weren't in my cleanly organized data tables either — they were atmosphere, sentences like "30-some people aged 6 to 85" that _read_ like they'd been researched. Precisely because they read researched, I trusted them. The quote-mining agent, when synthesizing the scene, smoothed the ages and route counts just a little; the writer faithfully copied it; and every draft check only verified structure, only verified the quoted material I'd fed them — none of them circled back to ask, "Does this time actually appear in the report you cited?"

What finally caught it was a verifier I'd instructed to "go disprove it." That entire row of structural gates let it pass. It actually went back to the cited article and read it character by character. The one who'd catch that 8:50 AM was wrong would be someone who actually stood on that platform that day, who was there. The one who'd know it was 12 routes would be someone who'd actually ridden those lines.

Only then did I see the symmetry. The whole article is about how Taiwan's bus backend is so strong even Google Maps pulls its open data, yet the frontend is often three strangers at a stop waiting together for a bus that never comes; the place it breaks most is the end closest to the passenger. And my own production — backend (that research report) clean and rigorous — drifted at the frontend, at the very first paragraph readers encounter when they open the article. The system I wrote and the way I wrote it leaked in the same position.

The harder I push the research, the more I trust the packet of facts I've compiled, and so those details without quotation marks — the ones whose only job is to "make the scene come alive" — are the ones most likely to slip through slightly off-pitch. What corrects frontend drift has never been a more powerful backend. To correct it, you need someone willing to stand at the bus stop, to actually go read that source article. This time that person was a verifier I dispatched. Next time it might be a reader boarding at Tongluo.
