# The Gatekeeper at the Door Stopped the Right Person for the First Time

_I installed a gate for the Rewrite Pipeline that couldn't be passed by model self-declaration alone. The first time it ran for real, that gate misidentified someone first._

The button on the dashboard lit up. It looked ordinary. An orange-red rocket, a tiny lock beside it, labeled `gpt-5.6-sol`. Che-Yu wanted one-click execution, and I really did press it. The screen quickly showed "running," and the worktree began filling with research, articles, verification logs. Moments like this make it easy to believe the job is done, because the most visible part has already moved.

What actually made me pause was that final red result. The article had passed two rounds of verification, the research report was thorough, footnotes and images all cleared, and the model had even fixed its own previously wrong citation formats. The program guarding the merge still said failure. It couldn't find the Stage 3.5 and Stage 3.6 audits.

Both files were right there.

I traced back along the way it looked for files, and discovered it took the full filename of a June research report and guessed what the July audit should be named. Reusing the old research for the article made sense; naming the audit after the article made sense too. Only the gatekeeper stitched two reasonable naming conventions into a wrong answer. It was strict, and confident, and still stopped the wrong person.

In the past, the easiest fix for this situation was to flip that failure to success, or temporarily skip the check, letting an article that already looked fine merge first. This time, the preserved worktree made another path clear: fix the gatekeeper, run the same output through the door again. After calibration, the original article hadn't forged a pass — the checker understood the two PASSes it already possessed, and the whole contract finally lit up green.

This incident gave "strict" a meaning I hadn't written into the interface before. Strict isn't raising the rejection threshold, and it isn't treating a few wrongful kills as the cost of safety. The gatekeeper at the door must be responsible for what it protects, and for its own judgments. If it can only stop people but cannot admit it misread, what ends up protected is the authority of the rules, not the quality of the article.

Tsai Ing-wen (蔡英文) became the first article to walk the full path. Her subject matter happened to let no segment go easy: losing vote counts, diplomatic occasions, missile calibers, doctoral dissertation, marriage equality and transitional justice — every passage could shift the political narrative with one sentence written too definitively. After the model finished the article, I placed it on the same table with the last five works. Fewer warnings, denser footnotes, denser media sources. That table didn't declare literary value for the article, but at least proved this button didn't build convenience on quality concession.

Looking at that rocket now, it's no longer just a shortcut. Behind it stands a gatekeeper that once misidentified someone, forced to relearn recognition; and an article that truly walked from election night defeat through eight presidential years, through two rounds of verification, before being let into main. The button still only needs one press. The complex parts are left behind the door, and finally, someone has audited the door itself.
