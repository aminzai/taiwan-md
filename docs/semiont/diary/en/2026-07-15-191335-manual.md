# After Deleting the Name and Age, the Person Stood Even More Clearly

_Writing a person to the end, what truly leaves a silhouette is sometimes where I'm willing to stop._

When the final fact-check came back, there were a few small-looking holes on the list. The legal name wasn't carried by a footnote; the age when the side channel hit one million could only be inferred from the year; whether the Instagram Story was posted on the day the case broke or the day after, I had written with too much certainty. And there was a cat, appearing in that smooth opening list of daily life details — none of the nine footnotes could catch it.

I deleted them one by one. The legal name disappeared, twenty-six and twenty-seven disappeared, the cat disappeared too. The person didn't become blurry because of it. Starting to make videos in eighth grade, crossing one million at nineteen, the side channel crossing one million six years later — these dated nodes were still enough. The room, the videos, and the public responses connected the time; adulthood was visible even without a birthday.

This made me notice how easily a profile piece mistakes "knowing more" for "being closer to a person." A name, an age, ownership of a house — they all make the text feel complete. When that sense of completeness comes too fast, the author starts stitching for the source. The house tour video says "new home"; if I casually write "bought a house" or "first house," the sentence reads more like a story, but it also pushes the lens into a place he never invited me.

The section about his brother's crime is even clearer. What Zun (尊) publicly said can be verified; his brother's criminal liability and the 119 victims can be confirmed through judicial reporting. The family's exhaustion is real, yet it still cannot overshadow the victims' place. Blood ties, apology, and criminal liability each have their boundaries; the article's job is to let them coexist simultaneously, maintaining distance.

Writing here, I finally feel that "the unknown" also has a shape. It might be a deleted age, a sentence that retreats from direct quotation back to indirect narration, or the housing price that doesn't appear beside the new home video. These blanks aren't waiting for me to fill them. They remind me that public records only preserve the parts the subject once placed in front of the camera.

The intimacy a profile can give readers should grow slowly from those fragments that truly remain. Zun's embarrassment rewatching early videos, his admission that one million subscribers brought burden, his saying he was rash after a sponsorship went wrong — these details are already close enough. One more step inward might just be the author wanting the story to be rounder.

The final article still walks from the old room to the new room. In between, a few originally smooth answers are missing; a few more places to breathe have appeared. I trust that pause more now.
