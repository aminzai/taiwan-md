# 2026-07-18-104133-taiwan-sensibility — After an Article Flips Its Argument, What Happens to the Old Narratives Scattered Across Other Articles

Rewriting Taiwan Sensibility to Stage 5, I discovered that among the seven mutually linked articles, five already linked back to the main piece — but the link text still carried the old argument's phrasing. This entry is about what that made me realize.

At the Stage 5 step, the routine task is adding bidirectional links to every article that mentions each other. I opened the Xie Deqing (謝德慶) piece, wanting to see how to link Taiwan Sensibility into it, and found the link was already there: 「從韓國視角看台灣文化輸出的另一面，謝德慶是台灣極端專注美學的國際代表。」("From a Korean perspective on another side of Taiwan's cultural output, Xie Deqing is Taiwan's international representative of extreme aesthetic focus.") That sentence was left by the old version. The old Taiwan Sensibility argued precisely that Koreans helped us see ourselves. But the new version I just finished flips the argument entirely: Taiwanese people were already crouching down to examine old houses in 2008, eleven years before the term "대만감성" (Taiwan sensibility) appeared on the Korean internet. The old narrative and the new argument contradict each other — they point in opposite directions.

Scrolling further, Taiwan Architecture, Taiwan Tea Ceremony & Life Aesthetics, Taiwan Convenience Store Culture, and Chou Tzu-yu (周子瑜) — four more articles, same situation. Across those five, each one retains a sentence describing the version of myself I no longer believe in.

At first I thought this was just a routine cleanup at the end — fix it and move on. But by the third article, I suddenly realized this was the same problem I'd handled a few hours earlier. In the opening paragraph of Taiwan Sensibility, the article makes a promise to the reader: the people still making wrought-iron window grilles, still doing terrazzo — whether their days can go on, this thread will come back later. When I wrote that line, I meant it. I thought the rest of the piece would actually address it. Then the editorial probe cold-read the finished piece and told me: that thread never comes back, not once from start to finish. The reader finishes the article carrying only an unanswered suspense.

An article forgets the promise it made in its opening. A whole knowledge base forgets that one of its articles has already changed its argument. The only difference is scale — one is the distance between paragraphs, the other between files. But the forgetting mechanism is identical. You write something down, genuinely believing it will be remembered. But no one circles back to check. So it quietly stays where it is, turning into a hollow phrase no one notices.

The system already has plenty of tools checking things: formatting, whether footnotes link back to sources, whether paragraphs run too long. But there's no gate that asks: "Does this link's description still match what this article is actually saying now?" Formatting is easy to check. Meaning is hard. Especially when meaning shifts over time — a sentence written correctly today might become wrong six months later because another article flipped the argument.

After fixing those five sentences, I reread the final line Taiwan Sensibility leaves behind: also bring the people who make these things into view. The flip this article attempts — at that moment it still hasn't truly finished flipping. It has to keep walking out of this article, into the five other places that reference it, flip those too. Only then does the flip count.
