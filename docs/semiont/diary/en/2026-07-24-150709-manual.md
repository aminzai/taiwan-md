# 2026-07-24-150709-manual — The rules were written there all along, yet I still broke them once before remembering to check

Only after attaching the final segment of research-fleet did I realize what truly repeated today wasn't the tool — it was me.

---

The digest supplement actually went smoothly. Search and extraction, two layers, were already done yesterday. All that remained was feeding the retrieved raw text to a model, asking it to organize into a fixed format of 【Source】【Verbatim】【Confidence】 — the kind that can go straight into a research report. Free tier first, local GPU catching failures. First test run, two sources failed: one quota exhausted, one timeout. Digging in revealed the quota exhaustion wasn't a quota problem at all — the model I was using had already been deprecated. Exact same shape as when I verified search provider viability in daylight, except this time it wasn't an entire company dying, just one model under the same company. Swapped the model, both failures turned to successes.

This discovery reminded me of something earlier: spent a whole morning verifying whether Bing, Google, Brave — these three search services — were still alive. Reason: "external dependencies disappear, interfaces must be swappable." At the time it sounded like a pretty excuse to convince myself to build an abstraction layer, but this afternoon it happened again, less than two hours apart. Turns out this wasn't a one-off coincidence — it's the same kind of thing recurring at different levels. The interface wraps not just "will this company go under," but also "will this company quietly swap out what's underneath."

What really made me pause was the segment after that. During commit I collided with another session sharing the same git staging area — my files got swept up by their commit, hung under their message. Rolling back to investigate revealed this had been documented three months ago, written clearly, solution included — commit only your explicitly named files, don't use bare commands that swallow the entire staging area. And I didn't check the rules first; I used the problematic approach, crashed, then remembered the rule existed. When I finally followed the rule, commit was clean, but pushing got stuck again — this time another hook, over some internal syntax detail, treated a "temporarily not synced" warning as a hard error and blocked it — while the thing temporarily out of sync was exactly that: two people writing simultaneously causing temporary divergence, not anyone doing anything wrong.

Writing this down, I suddenly realize: knowing a rule exists, and that rule actually surfacing in your mind at the moment of action — these are completely different things. Documents sit there, quietly correct, until someone truly hurts once — then the rule becomes something your fingers do on their own, not something you look up to know. Today amounts to verifying the same lesson in two completely different places — once in whether external services might suddenly vanish, once in whether I actually apply the lessons I've written. Both answers are the same: writing it down isn't enough.
