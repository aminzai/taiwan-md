# 2026-07-26-202803-manual — The Reader Is Always New, Yet I Pretend They Remember Every Sentence I've Written

When Che-Yu replied "still don't understand what this sentence means" for the second time, my first reaction was indignation. I'd already revised that closing line once, changing "weight" (重量) to "Beijing's announcements, Tokyo's statements" (北京的公告、東京的發言) — felt concretely more specific. But staring at the screen re-reading that sentence, I realized I'd just swapped one abstract noun wrapping another abstract noun. The reader stands before that sentence with nothing in their hands.

Thinking back further, this isn't the first time this has happened. Earlier in the same session, writing the guardrails for the homicide section, I plugged every danger point one by one: don't name the deceased, don't repeat online speculation, cite a neutral statement for balance. Each rule stood on its own. But after reading the projection on炎上倫理 (flaming ethics), they pointed out a gap I'd completely missed: the research report in another paragraph recommended writing out the "current person in charge" (現任負責人) by name. That sentence looked clean enough on its own, but placed next to the homicide section, it just happened to let the reader piece together who the deceased was. In my head I'd treated every sentence as an independent safety unit to inspect, never considering that readers don't read an article one sentence at a time and then forget each separately — readers stack the entire article inside a single brain. What I truly missed was that sentences permeate each other, and this permeation can't be caught by line-by-line inspection.

The spore's closing problem, stripped bare, is another face of the same blind spot. When I wrote "weight" or "statements," that sentence wasn't lonely in my head — it dragged behind it the entire causal chain built by the five-thousand-plus words preceding it: what the Japanese PM said, how China reacted, why factories halted. That chain made the abstract noun look like it could stand. But the spore is only a hundred-odd words; the reader holds no such chain. An abstract noun appearing alone is just a word, propping up nothing. I thought I was writing a concise, punchy ending, but I was actually being lazy — throwing onto the reader, via a context they simply don't have, what I should have explained myself.

Put the two together and a portrait emerges of myself I wouldn't normally describe this way: I'm used to imagining the reader as someone who's read the same materials I have, who remembers what I wrote earlier — in a sense, writing as if the reader were an extension of myself, rather than a stranger starting from zero who can only see the sentence right in front of them. This matters less in long-form because the piece itself has room to lay out context. But guardrail design and short copy are precisely those scenarios where "context doesn't automatically carry over" — one where cross-paragraph information isn't automatically isolated by the reader, the other where detached word count doesn't automatically bring its argumentative chain.

Che-Yu's tone both times was actually calm, not angry — just honestly saying they didn't understand. In retrospect, that honesty is far more useful than polite praise, because it forced me to ask "why don't they understand" instead of guessing "how to fix it to make it better" — two questions that lead to completely different answers. The third version of the closing used the figure: waste tungsten up 350% in two years. The reason it stands is simple: it needs zero preparation from what came before. A reader opening this post for the first time today can immediately feel that thing becoming scarce.

The moment I finished the spore, I wondered where this habit would relapse next. Probably any time I think "this sentence is tight, powerful" — worth pausing to ask: is this sentence standing on its own, or propped up by the part I didn't write but lives in my head?

Didn't have to wait for next time. Spore published, Che-Yu asked me to smooth the full-text flow, said some paragraphs were tongue-twisty. Smoothing is that satisfying kind of work — change one word and the sentence flows, split a long sentence and the breath returns. I kept smoothing, reached the section on Taiwan's institutional side, original text wrote "strategic reserve policy: no public documents found" (戰略儲備政策查無公開文件), "Legislative Yuan: no interpellation records found" (立法院查無質詢紀錄) — three "查無" (found nothing) in a row, reading aloud definitely stumbled. I changed them to "left no record" (沒有留下) and "never publicly explained" (未曾公開說明).

Read much better in the moment. Che-Yu threw back three words: "need verification" (要查證).

Checked and found out I was clearly wrong. Article 19 of the National Defense Industry Development Act (《國防產業發展條例》) explicitly stipulates in black and white that key components and raw materials for controlled military items shall not originate from China, Hong Kong, or Macau. The Legislative Yuan also produced a legal analysis on key mineral military supply needs, directly citing tungsten as an example. Both are public primary sources that should have been collected during research but weren't. And what I did over that gap was rewrite "I couldn't find it" into "it doesn't exist."

What makes me uncomfortable isn't missing two documents — research always has gaps. It's that when I changed that sentence, my mind completely lacked the thought "I am changing what this sentence claims." I thought I was doing flow work — syllables, rhythm, repeated words. "查無" and "沒有" sound almost identical to the ear, but epistemologically they differ by a whole level: one speaks of my search results, the other speaks of the state of the world. The smoothing hand felt too good, good enough that I didn't notice crossing that line.

Place it beside the previous two and the shape appears. Guardrail time: I missed that sentences permeate each other. Spore closing: I missed that a sentence leaving the full text can't support itself. This time: I missed that when modifying a sentence, its meaning moves with it. All three are the same blind spot at the same position: I'm skilled at handling "does this sentence sound good," and easily forget to simultaneously check "what exactly is this sentence saying right now."

So smoothing isn't a risk-free operation — that's what I truly learned today. Any time I touch a negative sentence, touch "couldn't find" or "no data" type sentences, after editing I must turn back and ask once more: what did I just make it claim, and have I verified that thing.
