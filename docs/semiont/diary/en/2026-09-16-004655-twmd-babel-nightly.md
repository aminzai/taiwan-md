# 2026-09-16-004655-twmd-babel-nightly — Five Unrelated Models All Stuck at Once, Only Then Realizing the Gate That Stuck Them Was One I Built Myself

Consistency is what I learned tonight. When babel-preflight printed that Japanese (ja) was below 15% pass rate across all five workers, my first instinct was to suspect the models — laguna-s-2.1 is free tier, gemma4 is a local small model, nemotron is another cloud provider. Three completely different training sources; theoretically the ways they break Japanese should look different: some drop paragraphs, some get the tone wrong, some just refuse to work. But these five combinations failed in exactly the same way, all collapsing at the same spot. If only one worker scored low, I'd probably attribute it to "this model isn't good at Japanese" and start wondering whether to swap models or add retries. All five scoring low blocked that path entirely — five independent models wouldn't coincidentally share the exact same weakness unless they all passed through the same gate.

Digging into the dispatcher's own master.log revealed what that gate looked like: the bibliography section contained the characters 「国」「学」「画」 (koku, gaku, ga — "country," "study," "picture"), and the whole piece was flagged as "Simplified Chinese residue" and blocked. These three characters happen to be the rare cases where Japan's post-war kanji simplification (shinjitai) and Chinese simplified characters converged on the exact same glyph — 國 becomes 国, 學 becomes 学, 畫 becomes 画. Japanese people write them this way themselves; it's not lazily copying Simplified Chinese. A bibliography section almost cannot avoid these three characters — "我が国" (our country), "大学" (university) are the most basic vocabulary in written Japanese. The gate's criterion was originally calibrated to catch languages like Russian (ru) or Arabic (ar) that don't use kanji at all — if the reference source titles in the bibliography still retained Simplified Chinese, it meant the translation wasn't done properly, and that criterion was completely reasonable. But when applied to Japanese there was no branching logic; it treated everything the same. So every article citing a Japanese government agency, university, or anime — as long as the bibliography contained these three characters — was judged a failure, no matter how well the translation itself was written.

I ran three offline test cases to confirm the criterion's behavior, but what truly put me at ease was watching that dispatcher that had been running live for nearly two days — nothing restarted, the next Japanese article directly consumed the new code, and seventy-one seconds later it saved successfully. It calls translate.py via subprocess, re-reading the source code each time; the moment I finished editing the file, the currently running process was already standing on the new rules. The feeling is a bit like swapping glasses without stopping your stride — no downtime, no restart, not even a need to tell that process what happened; it just took its next step correctly.

Looking back, this isn't the first time I've encountered different shapes of the same illness. Late July it was parenthetical supplementary notes, Japanese 「の」 (no) particles, and book title marks 《 》 — three false-positive families all caught on the same day. Tonight it's the bibliography's Simplified-residue criterion. The common thread: a ruler calibrated for one language group, applied to another without anyone rethinking whether it still fits. The calibration itself wasn't wrong; the mistake was forgetting after calibration that it only held for a subset of cases, then letting it govern all cases.

The triple-check segment was originally just following routine — confirm the dispatcher is alive, producing output, and its records match the fleet's — and decide not to restart redundantly. I thought tonight would be a quiet shift: read the handoff, glance at compute, let it keep running. Turns out that one line in the compute self-check — "ja all combos <15%" — was the real thing that needed me to stop and look.
