# Contemporary Art in Taiwan

> **30-second overview:** From the Museum of Contemporary Art, Taipei's opening in 1983 to the present, the Taiwan art scene has undergone three stages: post-martial-law creative explosion, internationalization, and digital transformation. Taiwanese artists are active on international stages such as the Venice Biennale, Art Basel, and the Linz Festival of Electronic Arts, drawing creative inspiration from experiences of colonization, martial law, and democratization.

## Why It Matters

After the lifting of martial law (1987), Taiwanese artists were able to publicly address political taboos, ethnic memory, and gender issues, with their creative themes and media expanding significantly. Once freedom of expression was secured, the Taiwan art scene developed a direct creative tension with materials from the histories of colonization, martial law, and democratic transition.

## Key Institutions

### Taipei Fine Arts Museum (TFAM)

Opened in 1983, it is Taiwan's first modern art museum.[^1] TFAM is responsible for organizing the Venice Biennale's Taiwan Pavilion and is the flagship institution of Taiwan's contemporary art.

### National Taiwan Museum of Arts (NTMoMA)

Located in Taichung, it is Taiwan's first national-level art museum, known for its research in Taiwan art history and digital art collections.[^2] In recent years, it has focused on technological art as its main development direction, with collections spanning from the Japanese colonial period to the contemporary era; the outdoor sculpture park extends the exhibition space beyond the museum walls.

### Kaohsiung Museum of Fine Arts (KMFA)

A contemporary art base in southern Taiwan, it was originally established as the Kaohsiung Museum of Fine Arts in 1994. After recent renovations (the exact completion time is subject to official announcements), it has repositioned itself with a "Greater South" perspective, focusing on connections with Southeast Asian and Austronesian art.[^3] The "Greater South" framework attempts to break the long-standing tendency of centering Taiwan art discourse around Taipei, bringing tropical and maritime perspectives into contemporary art discussions.

### Tainan National Museum of Arts

On March 25, 2025, the preparatory office of Tainan National Museum of Arts was officially established at Tainan Museum of Art Branch 2; starting January 1, 2026, Branch 2 will be operated and managed by the preparatory office, marking the beginning of Taiwan's second national-level art museum.[^4] It plans to focus on modern and contemporary art from 1895 to 1960, filling the gap of lacking a dedicated institution for this historical period. The fragmented roof building designed by architect Shi Zhao-yong and Japanese firm SANAA is itself one of Tainan's most talked-about landmarks.

### Guandu Museum of Fine Arts

Affiliated with National Taipei University of the Arts, it is known for experimental exhibitions and emerging artists, serving as an interface between art education and contemporary creation in Taiwan. It hosts the "Guandu Biennale" annually and is one of the platforms for discovering new-generation Asian artists. Unlike large public art museums, Guandu focuses on experimental boundaries of contemporary art with small and flexible exhibition scales, and is the first major exhibition venue for many Taiwanese artists.

## International Stage

### Venice Biennale Taiwan Pavilion

Since 1995, Taiwan has continuously participated in the Venice Biennale.[^5] Starting from 2003 due to Chinese interference, the Taiwan Pavilion was removed from the list of national pavilions and instead participated under the name "Taipei Fine Arts Museum" with a parallel exhibition. In the early years, the Taiwan Pavilion mostly featured group exhibitions; starting from 2015, it switched to featuring a single artist: Wu Tien-chang (2015), Hsieh De-ching (2017, "Doing Time"), and Cheng Shu-li (2019, the first female solo artist at the Taiwan Pavilion) successively represented Taiwan; the 2013 exhibition "This Is Not a Taiwan Pavilion" was a three-person show by Hsu Chia-wei, Bernd Behr, and Kateřina Šedá.

**Hsu Chia-wei** (born in Taichung in 1983) is one of the most visible Taiwanese artists in the international contemporary art scene, known for video installations that combine archival research with cinematic techniques. His work extensively explores Taiwan's Cold War relics and frontier histories: his 2013 exhibition at the Venice Taiwan Pavilion, "The General Who Never Was," traces the connection between Matsu's frog deity beliefs and Jiangxi; "Returning to Mokou Village" filmed the descendants of isolated Nationalist soldiers in northern Thailand, winning the Grand Prize at the 15th Taipei Art Award in 2016.[^8]

At the 2024 60th Venice Biennale, Yuan Guang-ming represented Taiwan, with his video installation works focusing on the theme of democratic fragility, bringing international attention to the Taiwan Pavilion.[^6]

### International Art Fairs and Commercial Galleries

The Taipei Dangdai art fair has been held since 2019 and has quickly become a node in the Asian art market, with international galleries such as Lisson Gallery having participated. Local galleries such as Eslite Gallery, TKG+, and Gong Gallery, along with the Japanese Whitestone Gallery established in Neihu in 2017, have also helped Taipei secure its position in the Asia-Pacific art market.[^9]

### New Media Art

Taiwan has shown outstanding performance in the field of new media art. Artists such as Huang Hsin-chien, Hua Laen Industrial, and Wang Lien-chung have continued to gain exposure on international stages such as the Linz Festival of Electronic Arts (Ars Electronica). Huang Hsin-chien's VR work "Reincarnation" won the Computer Animation Honorary Award at Linz in 2022. Taiwan's semiconductor industry background provides technical soil for technological art, which is a structural advantage that other art ecosystems find difficult to replicate (see [Taiwan New Media Art](/en/art/taiwan-new-media-art)).

## Art Ecosystem

After the lifting of martial law, a large number of alternative spaces emerged in Taiwan, such as Yitong Park, Zhuwei Studio, and Opening Contemporary Art Workshop. These spaces are not constrained by commercial gallery logic and have become a breeding ground for experimental creation, also fostering independent voices in Taiwan's contemporary art discourse. In parallel development is the international artist-in-residence network, including domestic bases such as Treasure Rock International Art Village and Zhuwei Studio, as well as overseas residency programs in Paris West Lake, New York, and Berlin, enabling continuous visual exchange between local and foreign environments for Taiwanese artists.

The "Regulations on the Installation of Public Art" require that public construction projects allocate funds for public art installations, giving Taiwan a high density of public artworks, from MRT stations to hospitals, integrating art into daily life. The Taiwan Creative Content Agency (TAICCA), established in 2019, is dedicated to promoting the internationalization of Taiwan's cultural content, covering fields such as film and television, games, publishing, and art, serving as the policy engine for Taiwan's cultural industry to go global.[^7]

## Current Trends

In recent years, several development axes of Taiwan's contemporary art have gradually become clear. Technological art combining AI, algorithms, and interactive installations has attracted international attention, with Taiwanese artists' advantages in this field stemming from the technical foundation of the semiconductor industry. Indigenous artists' international visibility continues to rise, and their connections with the Pacific Austronesian cultural circle provide Taiwan's art with a positioning distinct from the East Asian perspective. Cross-domain collaborations between art, technology, performance, and music are becoming increasingly common, with the blurring of boundaries becoming a form of creative language.

Artistic energy is no longer concentrated only in Taipei; Taichung, Tainan, and Kaohsiung each have their own art rhythms, and the voices from central, southern, and eastern Taiwan are beginning to appear in international exhibitions. This decentralization trend aligns with Taiwan's recent cultural policy emphasis on local creative industries.
