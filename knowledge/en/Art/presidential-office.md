# Presidential Office: How a Governor-General's Office Building Became a Democratic Open Space

## 30-Second Overview

In June 1912, construction began on the new Taiwan Governor-General's Office building within Taipei City; when completed in March 1919, it was the most prominent administrative architecture of Japanese colonial rule in Taiwan. The Taipei Air Raid of May 1945 destroyed the front left side; post-war repairs turned it into Chieh Shou Hall, and it formally became the Presidential Office in 1950. Designated a national historic monument in 1998, its plaque was only changed from "Chieh Shou Hall" to "Presidential Office" in 2006.[^1][^9] What makes this building most worth seeing is not just its red bricks, arched windows, and central tower, but how it has changed names and functions under different regimes — transforming a place originally built to watch the people into a place where people can slowly walk in and speak at its gates.

## Don't Rush to Photograph the Red Bricks

Viewed from 122 Sec. 1, Chongqing S. Rd., the Presidential Office easily reads as a highly accomplished old building: red-brick facade, gray-white horizontal bands, central tower, symmetrical side wings spreading outward. This reading isn't wrong, yet it only describes the most immediately visible layer.

What truly makes it significant is that this building has never played just one role. It was first the Taiwan Governor-General's Office workplace, then became Chieh Shou Hall, then the Presidential Office. It has been sliced by air raids, fires, and endless functional conversions, and in each repair retains the choices of the government of the day about "what should be preserved." When Academia Historica introduces this building, it places the red bricks, white walls, arched doorways, classical capitals, construction process, and post-war reconstruction on the same continuum — because the building's appearance and the political history it carries are inseparable from the start.[^2]

So the Presidential Office is not a simple sum of a "Japanese-era building" plus a "presidential office." It is a national building that has been repeatedly renamed, patched, and used by different eras, yet still stands in its original place.

## Beyond the Provincial Administration Hall: Building Another Power Center

On June 5, 1895, first Taiwan Governor-General Kabayama Sukenori landed in Keelung and set up the temporary Governor-General's Office in the Keelung Customs. Nine days later, the Office moved into Taipei City, using the former Qing Provincial Administration Hall as its temporary workplace. This was not a simple relocation: the Japanese military demolished the shop-houses before the West Gate, opening a straight road to the main gate, reconfiguring the original Qing official compound into the colonial government's entrance.[^1]

In 1905, a major fire broke out at the Provincial Administration Hall; years of neglect and termite damage also made the old building unfit to bear the administrative scale of the colonial government. The permanent building project thus began from "the old building isn't enough," but quickly exceeded practical concerns: the colonial government needed a visible building to show who held the highest administrative power in this city.[^1]

The new building's location was set in the 1900 Taipei City Plan, in the Bunbu-cho area within the city walls. The site originally held residences, ponds, and the Lin and Chen ancestral shrines. Through negotiation, survey, and expropriation and demolition, the Chen shrine was moved to Dadaocheng, the Lin shrine to the area near the then-Taipei Station rear yard. Before groundbreaking, the land was even briefly turned into a racetrack.[^1]

This land transformation is worth remembering. Today the Presidential Office feels like it "has always been there," but it was actually built on a reordering of urban space: ancestral shrines moved away, streets straightened, administrative power using a new building to redirect the city's sightlines and circulation toward itself.

## An Office Building's Scale Is Not Just Its Facade's Scale

When completed in 1919, the Presidential Office totaled over 10,000 ping (approx. 33,000 sq m). Groundbreaking in 1912, topping out in 1915, completion in 1919 — the project itself spanned years of administrative planning before and after World War I.[^2] These figures show it was not a mansion for a few officials, but an administrative machine needing to house the Governor-General, civil affairs departments, conferences, receptions, and vast clerical work. The red-brick facade is its most photographed part; what truly supported the colonial government's daily operations were the office spaces, corridors, entry protocols, and logistical circulation.

The central tower was raised from the original six stories to eleven — not merely for height. The tower made the administrative center more recognizable from across the city, and visually elevated the Governor-General's Office above the surrounding street blocks. When architecture turns "who works here" into "the whole city can see this place," office function and political symbol have already overlapped.[^1][^4]

## The Presidential Office Is Not an Island: The Official Network Within the City Walls

Looking only at the Presidential Office, one might imagine Taipei's western district as a city arrayed around a single power center. In reality, the Governor-General's Office, the Governor-General's Official Residence, and the Transportation Bureau each handled different power scenarios. The Governor-General's Office was the administrative hub; the Taipei Guest House was the Governor-General's residence and reception venue — one for work, one for guests — forming the colonial government's two faces in the city.

The Taipei Guest House originated as the Taiwan Governor-General's Official Residence, initially built in 1901, rebuilt in 1913 shifting from Neo-Renaissance to a more ornate Baroque style. It simultaneously handled residence, daily work, and social receptions; post-war managed by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, it became the venue for state banquets and receiving foreign dignitaries, and now coordinates with Presidential Office open days for regular public access.[^11] This creates an interesting contrast with the Presidential Office: one primarily for state work, one primarily for state reception; both once represented power, both later returned to public life through restoration and opening.

Further north, the Railway Department of the Taiwan Governor-General's Transportation Bureau connected this network to transport and industry. The 1918 Railway Department building near the old North Gate was designed by Moriyama Matsunosuke. The complex includes the main building, cafeteria, octagonal tower, power room, engineering office, and wartime command center — showing the colonial government needed not just a Governor-General's Office but an entire infrastructure for managing railways, factories, materials, and wartime transport.[^12][^13] Moriyama simultaneously worked on the Governor-General's Office, Official Residence, Railway Department, Tainan Prefectural Hall, and Taichung Prefectural Hall — viewing these sites together reveals how one architect served an entire administrative system, not just left a few isolated masterpieces.[^12]

During post-war restoration, the Taiwan Provincial Administrative Executive Office temporarily occupied the Taipei City Hall — today's Executive Yuan offices. Before the Governor-General's Office resumed operations, the city's administrative power thus temporarily shifted to another official building.[^5] Architectural history is not a single road leading only to the Presidential Office's front gate; it also includes the relocation, succession, and redivision of labor among official buildings.

This timeline does not arrange buildings into a sightseeing checklist, but places them back into a single question: how does the state allocate work, reception, transport, military, and display functions in the city? And when the political system changes, how are these sealed places reopened, renamed, and reinterpreted?

## A Design Competition With No First Place

The new Governor-General's Office adopted a two-stage open design competition. Between 1906 and 1907, seven proposals were shortlisted in the first stage. The originally favored Suzuki Kichibei proposal was judged too similar to the Peace Palace in The Hague; first prize was left vacant, and Nagano Uheiji received second prize. This result was unusual: Nagano's proposal was selected, yet he did not directly obtain actual construction rights.[^1][^3]

The final design was completed by Moriyama Matsunosuke, engineer of the Governor-General's Building Maintenance Division. He re-examined Nagano's design as a blueprint and raised the original six-story central tower to eleven stories. Official explanations framed this change as increased demand and insufficient office space. StoryStudio's Moriyama feature places the Governor-General's Office alongside the three major prefectural halls, Railway Department, and Tainan Court within his diverse stylistic range and professional division of labor in designing public official buildings.[^1][^4]

There's no need to rush to judge who is the "true designer." A more accurate statement: Nagano's competition proposal, Moriyama's redesign, and the Building Maintenance Division's institutional work together formed the building seen today. In a Central News Agency interview, architectural history researcher Ling Tsung-kuei also placed the two men's relationship within the same master-disciple lineage and competition system, rather than simplifying the Presidential Office as a single architect's genius work.[^3] Research collected by the National Museum of Taiwan Literature, "Architect Moriyama Matsunosuke and His Works," also situates the Governor-General's Office within the overall context of Moriyama's Taiwan oeuvre, reminding that competition proposals and actual construction cannot be directly equated.[^10]

## The Facade Is Western, But Power's Arrangement Is Not Only Western

The Presidential Office is often understood through the "Tatsuno style" lineage: red bricks, white horizontal lines, arched windows, classical colonnades, and central tower indeed share a recognizable vocabulary with contemporary Japanese modern public architecture. StoryStudio's architectural feature notes, however, that the Presidential Office uses red-brick exterior walls rather than the stone common in colonial administrative buildings, with reinforced concrete structure beneath. It looks like brick construction, but is not simply a brick house.[^5]

But the building's sense of power lies not only in its facade. StoryStudio's Presidential Office feature reads the inverted-"日"-shaped plan, central tower, and red-brick-white-band decoration within a single official-building image. These elements make the workplace not just provide rooms, but shape the administrative center into a long-distance recognizable marker in the city. This is an interpretation of architectural form, not the building's sole "self-spoken" answer, but it explains why the Presidential Office makes one feel they are approaching a power center even without standing under the tower.[^4]

The effect of Moriyama raising the tower was not just a more spectacular skyline. The tower became a long-distance visible marker in the city; the administrative center need not be approached to be recognized. This "see first, enter later" order is precisely how official architecture turns political power into urban landscape.

## 1945: The Building Was Bombed Into Another History

The Taipei Air Raid of May 1945 heavily damaged this building originally meant to display the colonial government's stability. Official records note severe damage to the front left side, but the main structure survived. During post-war repair, the porte-cochere's original semi-circular pediment dome was changed to a flat roof, and roofing materials also changed due to cost and construction conditions.[^1][^2]

![Taipei Presidential Office architectural exterior](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/1c/Presidential_Office_Building.jpg)

_Photo: Rutger van der Maar, CC BY 2.0; [Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Presidential_Office_Building.jpg)._

Restoration did not turn time back to 1919. Official Presidential Office restoration records show a 1935 fire in the fifth-floor photographic materials warehouse damaged the south corridor, with the roof changed from wood-and-slate to reinforced concrete and steel-beam flat roof. Post-war saw successive structural, exterior wall, door/window, roof, and office space repairs. The 2003 exterior wall project still faced color inconsistency and mottled facade from repeated partial patching.[^6]

Architect Hsueh Chin long participated in Presidential Office survey and restoration. A Central News Agency interview records her saying the building took at least two direct bomb hits in wartime, damaging the porte-cochere and corridor near the Judicial Yuan. Post-war budget constraints led to partial steel structures and roof trusses rebuilt in wood, roof first covered in cheaper corrugated iron, later changed to copper. What this restoration left behind is not "original appearance," but material evidence of what the state could afford, valued, and prioritized in different periods.[^7]

Hsueh also said a line well-suited to the Presidential Office:

> "It's hard to imagine that when the Presidential Office was built in the early Republican years, it was a building just after reinforced concrete had developed, and being able to produce such a building was indeed no simple matter."[^7]

The point of this remark is not to add a halo of greatness to the historic monument, but to remind us: before restoring a building, one must first know how it was made, how it was damaged, and how it was altered. Without understanding it, one can only cover old problems with new materials.

Historic designation did not eliminate these choices either. After designation, the question shifted from "whether to keep" to "which moment to keep, with what materials, and which later alterations should also be preserved." The Presidential Office's flat roof, iron-to-copper change, partial brick color differences — none are simple replicas of the 1919 original. They simultaneously record war, budget, technology, and usage needs. Preservation is therefore not sealing the building in the past, but letting traces of different eras remain recognizable, not all wiped into a single new red.[^6][^7][^9]

## From Chieh Shou Hall to Presidential Office: The Name Itself Is Political History

In 1946, the under-restoration Governor-General's Office was renamed Chieh Shou Hall. After restoration completed in 1948, Chieh Shou Hall became the main venue of the First Taiwan Provincial Exhibition. This was a brief but important moment: a year earlier still the colonial government's highest administrative building, now packaged through exhibition as a stage for the new regime to display industry and order after receiving Taiwan.[^2][^8]

After the government relocated to Taiwan in 1949, Chieh Shou Hall and surrounding roads entered high-level control, becoming the center of administrative and military power. Central News Agency's history of the palace-front space notes that National Day military parades were held before Chieh Shou Hall, the plaza used for military-civilian celebrations, and for manufacturing the state imagery of strongman politics. A place that had just welcomed the people via exhibition once again became a restricted zone ordinary people could not freely approach.[^8]

In 1950, this building formally became the Republic of China Presidential Office; yet the name "Chieh Shou Hall" continued in use for over half a century. In 2006, the plaque was changed to "Presidential Office," with official explanation linking this renaming to constitutional significance and actual function; it also meant the building was no longer named for an individual's birthday, but for the institutional state organ.[^1][^1]

A name cannot automatically erase the past. The Presidential Office still stands in its original position, still uses part of the original space. The renaming's significance lies in later people choosing what language to face these overlapping histories with, rather than pretending the building can be reborn from a certain day.

## Palace-Front Plaza: From Restricted Zone to Civic Stage

The Presidential Office's story cannot stop at the main gate. The roads and plaza before it are equally part of the building's meaning.

Before the lifting of martial law, the palace-front space was a heavily guarded power performance venue; after lifting, assemblies, protests, performances, and social movements gradually entered this area originally off-limits to the people. In 1996, Chieh Shou Road was renamed Ketagalan Boulevard, and motorcycle/bicycle bans and other controls were relaxed; Central News Agency's compilation places this change within a series of civic events, including the 1989 obstruction of Cheng Nan-jung's funeral, 1997 laser projection on the Presidential Office facade, 2013 Hung Chung-chiu protest assembly, and 2014 Anti-Service Trade Agreement protests.[^8]

StoryStudio describes the post-martial-law change from another angle: Ketagalan Boulevard became an expressive venue for Indigenous rights, farmer welfare, energy, environment, education, legal reform, pensions, and other issues. The Presidential Office interior also once rearranged reception spaces with Taiwan's oceans, Indigenous peoples, flora/fauna, and local cultures. The building did not thereby become a "place without politics," but let more political voices appear around it.[^5]

This turn makes the Presidential Office's core contradiction clear: it was originally a building demanding people look up, obey, and detour; now it is simultaneously the workplace of state power and a place where people can enter, observe, protest, photograph, and make demands. Democracy is not demolishing the power building, but changing who has the right to approach it, who can speak before it, and how the names and exhibitions inside are reinterpreted.

## Being Able to Queue and Enter Doesn't Mean Distance Has Vanished

Today the Presidential Office opens for visits, letting people enter the porte-cochere, main hall, exhibition halls, and partial public spaces. This experience is easily written as tourist information, but it is actually the final turn in the building's political history. StoryStudio records that today's Presidential Office is simultaneously a power symbol and Ketagalan Boulevard assembly venue, and a free historic monument open to domestic and foreign visitors.[^5]

Entering the Presidential Office still requires queuing and security checks, and one cannot traverse all office areas. These restrictions remind people: it remains a functioning state organ, not a museum stripped of politics. Precisely because of this, opening for visits has meaning — people are not looking back at power in a building that has lost its function, but in a building where power still operates, seeing how it is preserved, interpreted, and also monitored by society.

The most moving thing about the Presidential Office may not be whether it looks like a European palace, nor exactly how tall the central tower is, but that it cannot belong to just one era. The Governor-General's red bricks, war-damage repairs, Chieh Shou Hall plaque, martial-law restricted zone, Ketagalan Boulevard assemblies, and today's phone-holding visitors in the tour queue — all meet in the same building.

Whether a building can become the people's depends not only on who works inside, but on whether the people can remember what it once represented, and have the opportunity to re-decide how to stand before it.
