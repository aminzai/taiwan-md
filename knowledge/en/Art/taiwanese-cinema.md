# The Taiwanese Film: The Speaker Beside the Screen and a Film History That Died and Came Back to Life

> **30-Second Overview:** Local cinema has been declared dead at least three times—the golden age of Taiwanese-language films was strangled, the local audience left after the New Cinema era, and in 2003, local films accounted for only 0.36% of total box office revenue. Every time, it came back to life. And every cycle of life and death revolved around the same question: Whose Taiwan should be on the screen, and in whose language? From the _benshi_ (live commentators) improvising in Taiwanese in 1930s theaters, to the 1956 explosion of over a thousand Taiwanese-language films sparked by _Sue Ping-kui and Wang Bao-chun_, to Hou Hsiao-hsien, Edward Yang, and Tsai Ming-liang winning grand prizes at European film festivals, to Ang Lee standing on the Oscars stage twice, and finally to Wei Te-sheng’s 2008 film _Cape No. 7_, which featured five languages and grossed 530 million NTD—this is not a straight line from bad to good. It is Taiwan’s repeated struggle to reclaim the right to "use our own language and portray our own faces."

In 1930, in Taiwanese theaters, the people on the screen could not speak, but the person beside the screen could.

He was a _benshi_ (live commentator). Halfway through the film, he stood by the curtain, improvising line by line for the audience below, using Taiwanese to narrate the plot of the silent film, reveal the characters' inner thoughts, and even share his own views on the political situation. That year, there were about sixty _benshi_ across the island: forty-one were Japanese, and nineteen were Taiwanese. Each had to pass an examination by the police department to practice[^1]. The most famous, Zhan Tianma, performed _鞍馬天狗 (Kurama Tengu)_ in Dadaocheng, filling the theater with people; Lu Bingding of the Cultural Association explained _北極探險 (Arctic Exploration)_ in Chiayi, but his commentary included political criticism, leading to his immediate interruption by Japanese police[^2].

That was the earliest sound of Taiwanese cinema, not on the film reel, but on the lips of a living person. And that sound was in Taiwanese.

The story of the film reel itself was never fully owned by Taiwanese people from the start. In 1925, Liu Xiyang led the Taiwan Film Research Society to produce _Who is to Blame?_, considered the first feature film produced by Taiwanese people[^70]. But by 1943, the colonial government’s _The Bell of Sayon_ featured the popular Li Xianglan (Lan Ma) as the lead, turning a true story of a Atayal girl drowning into a propaganda piece calling on indigenous youth to serve Japan[^71]. From live commentary to colonial propaganda, for the first forty years of Taiwanese cinema, what was on the screen and who spoke for it was almost never decided by the people on the island.

![A still from the colonial-era film *The Bell of Sayon* (1943), starring Li Xianglan. The colonial government adapted the drowning incident of an Atayal girl into a propaganda film.](/article-images/art/sayon-no-kane-1943.webp)

Ninety years later on the same island, a film called _Cape No. 7_ made five languages speak simultaneously in the theater: Mandarin, Taiwanese, Japanese, English, and Paiwan[^3]. In those intervening ninety years, Taiwanese cinema was declared dead several times, only to come back to life several times. Each cycle of life and death, on the surface, was about box office, censorship, and the market. But at its core, it was always the same question: Whose Taiwan should be on the screen, in whose language, and who gets to appear on the screen?

This article does not aim to tell the story of "how local cinema went from bad to good." That is too smooth, and too fake a straight line. The true version is a history repeatedly strangled to death, only to come back to life.

## Over a Thousand Films, No One Remembers

Let’s start with a fact many Taiwanese people do not know: Taiwan was once the world's third-largest producer of feature films, second only to Japan and India[^4].

That was the era of Taiwanese-language films. In January 1956, a gezaixi (opera) film _Sue Ping-kui and Wang Bao-chun_ premiered in Taipei. Directed by He Jiming and produced by Chen Chengsan, head of the Maoliong Gongle Society opera troupe, it was Taiwan's first 35mm gezaixi Taiwanese-language film. With a low budget, it grossed about 1.2 million NTD, more than three times its cost[^5]. One film proved that "Taiwanese people making Taiwanese-language films for Taiwanese people to watch" was a viable market, igniting a craze.

In the following decade, Taiwanese-language films grew like weeds. In 1958, production peaked at 76 films, marking the first peak[^6]. Director Xin Qi alone made 12 films in 1969 alone[^7]. In Yingge, Lin Boqiu built his own Hushan Studio and founded Yufeng Film, aiming to industrialize Taiwanese-language films into a standardized industry[^8]. The genres were diverse: gezaixi, melodramatic literary films, comedies like _Wang Ge and Liu Ge Tour Taiwan_, spy films following the 007 craze like _Number One Character Tian_, and children's fantasy films like _The Great Hero Plum Deer_[^9].

How many were made exactly? This question itself is a microcosm of the Taiwanese-language film's fate: No one can give a precise number. The National Film and Audiovisual Center's (NFAC/TFAI) education network says "over a thousand," the _Taipei Times_ reported an estimate of "1,200 to 1,500," and academic research even suggests "over 2,000"^[10]. Why such a huge difference? Because the start and end years for calculation differ, whether Amoy-language films are included, and whether submission for censorship or actual screening counts, there is no consensus. More fundamentally, most copies have been lost. The 1,238 items preserved by TFAI represent "the number currently preserved," not the total production at the time; the museum itself states that what remains is less than one-fifth of the original output[^11].

An industry that was once the world's third-largest producer of feature films, most of whose works no longer exist as films, and whose total number cannot even be counted. This is not just a problem of film reels decaying.

The common narrative is that Taiwanese-language films were "crudely made," so they were naturally eliminated by the market and forgotten by history. This narrative is smooth, but it reverses cause and effect. Historian Su Zhiheng, in _The Reluctant Film History_, offers a different version: Taiwanese-language films did not die of old age; they were strangled to death[^12].

The one who strangled them was policy, not the audience. In 1957, the _Film Negative Import Taxation Method_ was amended, excluding Taiwanese-language films from tax exemptions. In other words, making Taiwanese-language films meant paying higher costs for negatives than others[^13]. Starting in 1959, the government restricted Taiwanese-language _benshi_; in 1962, the launch of Taiwan Television moved the audience for gezaixi and Taiwanese stories into living rooms; Mandarin films received subsidies, but Taiwanese-language films did not[^14]. Su Zhiheng’s argument is sharper: The government, by controlling the import of film negatives, actively constructed the stereotype of "Taiwanese = Black and White = Vulgar." When Mandarin films could use color and wide screens, Taiwanese-language films were stuck in black and white, making "cheap" a manufactured label rather than an inherent quality of Taiwanese-language films[^12].

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The impression we have today that "Taiwanese-language films are rough" may itself be the result of previous policies. An industry stripped of the conditions for upgrading (color, funding, talent flow) is then judged by its cheapness as the reason for its elimination—a perfect closed loop. So the real point of "Over a thousand films, no one remembers" is "why did it become forgotten?" Being forgotten is sometimes a decision, not a natural result of time.

In 1969, the output of Mandarin films surpassed that of Taiwanese-language films for the first time[^15]. The last Taiwanese-language film was _Chen San Wu Niang_ starring Yang Lihua in 1981[^16]. By the 1990s, researchers began to salvage this history; it was not until 2017 that King's College London held the first English-language academic symposium dedicated to Taiwanese-language films[^17]. A world's third-largest producer of feature films had to wait thirty years after its death, across half the globe, for someone to finally treat it as history seriously.

## Healthy, Realistic, and Untouchable

As Taiwanese-language films exited the stage, another language took over the screen.

In September 1954, the Agricultural Education Company and the Taiwan Film Company merged to form the Central Motion Picture Corporation (CMPC), using equipment from US aid[^18]. Mandarin films now had an industrial foundation, and its direction was determined by policy. In 1963, Gong Hong became the general manager of CMPC and launched a line called "Healthy Realism." He gave a precise definition: "Healthy is for education, Realism is for the countryside"[^19].

These six words are worth pausing for. They mean: Films should be realistic, but only realistic in a clean, educational way about the countryside, not the dark side of society or true conflicts. _The Oyster Girls_ (1964) is the representative work of this line. It was CMPC's first self-produced color wide-screen film (note: not "Taiwan's first color film"), co-directed by Li Jia and Li Xing, and won Best Feature Film at the 11th Asian Film Festival[^20]. The following year, _Raising Ducklings_ continued on the same path.

Outside of Healthy Realism, three other forces were operating simultaneously in Mandarin films during the 1960s and 70s.

One was Qiong Yao's literary films. Starting with Li Xing's _Wan Jun's Cousin_ in 1965, about twenty-five films were made in five years, launching a batch of stars[^21]. The most famous were the "Two Qins and Two Lins": Qin Han, Qin Xianglin, Lin Qingxia, and Lin Fengjiao. Lin Qingxia debuted in 1973 with _Outside the Window_ and later became a legend in the Chinese-speaking film industry; Lin Fengjiao won the Golden Horse Award for Best Actress in 1979[^22]. Qiong Yao's tears were the shared youth for many people of that era.

The second force was wuxia (martial arts). In 1967, King Hu's _Dragon Gate Inn_ became Taiwan's annual box office champion, launching a decade-long wuxia craze[^23]. King Hu was not just a box office draw; he turned wuxia into an aesthetic. In 1975, his _Suspended Masquerade_ won the Grand Prize of the Technical Committee at the 28th Cannes Film Festival, the second Chinese-language film to win at Cannes after Li Han-hsiang's _The King's Consort_ in 1962[^24]. A detail that illustrates the situation of Taiwanese cinema at the time: _Suspended Masquerade_ was invited by French critics to exhibit under the name of "Hong Kong," not recommended by the Taiwanese government[^24]. King Hu's wuxia language of bamboo forests, negative space, and rhythm later influenced Tsui Hark and Ang Lee; the bamboo forest scene in _Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon_ is a tribute to him[^25].

The third force was political propaganda and patriotic films. In 1971, the Republic of China withdrew from the United Nations, causing public unrest. One of the government's responses, according to TFAI, was to make films "to stabilize public sentiment and promote government achievements"[^26]. _The Thousand Autumns of the Heroes_ (1974), promoted by Wang Sheng of the Political Warfare Department, directed by Ding Shanxi, and starring Ke Junxiong, was followed by _The 800 Heroes_ (1976) and _The Heroes of Jianqiao_ (1977)[^27]. These films are often remembered in people's minds as being linked to "schools booking the entire theater to watch"; however, it must be honestly stated that there is no primary source evidence for "schools forcing students to watch," and it is only certain that they were often rebroadcast on television during holidays[^28].

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** From Taiwanese-language films to Healthy Realism to patriotic films, if we view "what is spoken on the screen, what can be spoken" as a main thread, we find that the history of local cinema has always been answering the same question—the answers were written by policy, not by the audience or creators. The problem with Taiwanese-language films was "not allowed to use this language to make films well"; the problem with Healthy Realism was "only allowed to depict this one kind of reality"; the problem with patriotic films was "what are films for?" Language, subject matter, and purpose: three gates, opening and closing in turn.

It is worth mentioning the origin of the name "Golden Horse." In 1957, the public held one edition of a Taiwanese-language film festival under the name "Golden Horse Award," but it was only held once[^29]. In 1962, the Department of Information established the official Golden Horse Awards, nominally only for Mandarin films, and the ceremony was deliberately scheduled around Chiang Ching-kuo's birthday[^29]. The same name first belonged to Taiwanese-language films, then was absorbed by Mandarin films. This itself is a small footnote to the language politics of that era.

## The Apple That Was Sliced

In 1982, CMPC made a decision that seemed insignificant at the time but changed everything in hindsight: letting a group of unknown young directors make films.

_Story of a Yellow Flower_ (1982) consisted of four segments, directed by Tao Dechen, Edward Yang, Ke Yizheng, and Zhang Yi[^30]. The following year, _The Son's Doll_ (1983), adapted from Huang Chun-ming's novel, had three segments directed by Hou Hsiao-hsien, Zeng Zhuangxiang, and Wan Ren[^31]. The New Taiwanese Cinema began with these two films.

But the New Cinema hit the old gate immediately. Wan Ren's segment _The Taste of Apples_ in _The Son's Doll_, because it depicted the true face of life at the bottom of society, was intervened by the Kuomintang (KMT) Cultural and Art Association, which demanded it be trimmed. This became the famous "Apple Slicing Incident"[^32]. Finally, under public pressure, that apple was saved. A small pair of scissors nearly sliced off the ability of Taiwanese cinema to tell the truth well, which had just begun to grow. On January 24, 1987, the _Declaration of the New Taiwanese Cinema_, drafted by Zhan Hongzhi, was published in the Human Supplement of the _China Times_, effectively serving as a formal confession for this movement[^33].

The three names of the New Cinema later became world-class directors.

![Hou Hsiao-hsien holding the Golden Lion award at the 1989 Venice Film Festival. *A City of Sadness* became the first Taiwanese film to win the highest award in the world's three major film festivals.](/article-images/art/hou-hsiao-hsien-venice-1989.webp)
_In 1989, Hou Hsiao-hsien won the Venice Golden Lion for A City of Sadness, putting Taiwanese cinema at the top of the world's three major film festivals for the first time. Photo: Gorup de Besanez, CC BY-SA 4.0._

Hou Hsiao-hsien filmed from _The People from the Funchien Village_ to _ Dust in the Wind_ to _ A Time to Love and a Time to Die_, turning Taiwan's local soil, memory, and time into a very long, slow visual language[^34]. On September 15, 1989, his _A City of Sadness_ won the Golden Lion at the 46th Venice Film Festival, marking the first time a Taiwanese film won the highest award in the world's three major film festivals (Cannes, Venice, Berlin)[^35]. More remarkably, this film directly confronted the then-sensitive subject of the February 28 Incident, using the rise and fall of one family to side-write that history[^35]. How highly is Hou Hsiao-hsien's long-take aesthetic valued internationally? Iranian director Abbas Kiarostami supported his _ The Puppetmaster_; Akira Kurosawa reportedly watched _ The Puppetmaster_ four times and admitted he could not make it; Hirokazu Kore-eda was also deeply influenced by him[^36].

Edward Yang took a different path. He filmed cities, the alienation and violence of urbanites. After _ Beach Days with Frankie and Anne_ and _ The Terrorizers_, his 1991 _ A Brighter Summer Day_ was originally 237 minutes long, adapted from a true story of a juvenile murder case in 1961[^37]. In 2000, his _ Yi Yi_ won the Best Director award at the 53rd Cannes Film Festival (note: Best Director, not the Palme d'Or)[^38]. On June 29, 2007, he died of colon cancer at the age of 59[^39]. His films influenced Hirokazu Kore-eda[^40] and Ryusuke Hamaguchi[^72].

The third is Tsai Ming-liang. Starting with _ Rebels of the Neon God_ in 1992, he turned the lens toward loneliness, desire, and time so slow it seemed to stop[^41]. In 1994, his _ Vive L'Amour_ won the Golden Lion at the 51st Venice Film Festival, tied with _ Before the Rain_; the jury president was David Lynch[^42]. Here, a widely circulated error must be corrected: _ Vive L'Amour_ won the Golden Lion, not the "FIPRESCI Prize"[^42]. Later, his _ The River_ and _ Striding into the Wind_ continued to win awards at film festivals; his 2009 film _ Face_ became the first film collected by the Louvre[^43].

Regarding the position of these three directors in film history, there is another commonly misstated version that needs correction. The circulated claim is that "three Taiwanese films are in the Top 100 Films of All Time in the French _Cahiers du Cinéma_," which is false. Checking the 2008 _Cahiers du Cinéma_ Top 100 list, there is not a single Taiwanese film[^44]. What actually wrote Taiwanese films into history is the British _Sight & Sound_ 2022 Top 100 Greatest Films of All Time: Edward Yang's _ A Brighter Summer Day_ ranked No. 78, and _ Yi Yi_ ranked No. 90[^45].

## Won Awards, No One Watched

Here is the most counter-intuitive turning point in the history of local cinema, and also the part most easily turned into a toxic chicken soup (cynical moralizing).

The New Cinema won the Golden Lion and Best Director in Europe; Taiwanese cinema was seen by the world for the first time. But during the same period, in Taiwanese theaters, local films were dying. Starting in 1996, the annual output of local films dropped to 15–20, and the market share was only 1–2%[^46]. The bottom was in 2003: that year, only about 15 local films were made across the island, with a total box office of about 15 million NTD, accounting for only 0.36% of the total box office in Taiwan, less than one percent[^47].

```tw-bars

```

0.36%. Say this number out loud: Out of one hundred Taiwanese people entering a theater, less than one was there to watch a local film. And in those same years, the names of Hou Hsiao-hsien, Edward Yang, and Tsai Ming-liang were shining in Cannes and Venice. A film of an island bloomed outside the walls, withered inside the walls.

A saying grew in the public sphere, and it was told smoothly: "It's the New Cinema's fault. Those art films were boring and incomprehensible, driving the audience away, which is why local films died."

This saying deserves serious attention because behind it is real resentment; many people really went to the theater, didn't understand, fell asleep, and never went to watch local films again. But blaming the entire downturn of local films on the New Cinema simplifies a multi-causal collapse into a scapegoat.

The New Cinema did create a distance between some audiences and the viewing habits of mainstream commercial films; this need not be avoided. But the collapse of local films in the 1990s and 2000s had more structural reasons acting simultaneously: Hollywood blockbusters were fully conquering the Taiwanese market; after Taiwan joined the WTO, quotas for foreign film imports were relaxed; videotapes and the fourth channel (cable TV) changed the way people watched films; local capital withdrew massively from the film industry; and theater channels were monopolized by imported films[^48]. An industry was crushed by this entire set of things, not driven away by a few award-winning art films.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The saying "The New Cinema killed local films" is charming because it turns a complex market collapse into a story with faces—a villain (the incomprehensible director), a victim (the incomprehensible audience). But the truth usually has no face. Making Hou Hsiao-hsien, Edward Yang, and Tsai Ming-liang the culprits of local cinema's decline is equivalent to demanding a group of creators take responsibility for the collapse of the entire industry ecosystem. Their actual cost was different: they won world-class awards but could not exchange them for local audiences—glory and the market were completely disconnected in those ten years.

This is the second death of local cinema. The Taiwanese-language films were strangled by policy; this time, it was drowned by the market. The difference is: the method of death is different, and the mechanism of resurrection will also be different. The Taiwanese-language films never came back, but this time, someone was waiting for a way to bring the audience back to the theater.

## To Get on the World Screen, You Must First Leave Your Own Island

In those ten years of withering inside the walls, a Taiwanese director chose another path, going outside the walls, and going the furthest.

![Ang Lee attending the 2009 Venice Film Festival. He won the Oscar for Best Director twice.](/article-images/art/ang-lee-venice-2009.webp)
_Ang Lee photographed at the 2009 Venice Film Festival. While local theaters could hardly see local films, this Taiwanese director was winning twice at the highest hall of Hollywood. Photo: nicolas genin, CC BY-SA 2.0._

Ang Lee. His "Father Trilogy"—_ Pushing Hands_, _ The Wedding Banquet_, and _ Eat Drink Man Woman_—was filmed from 1991 to 1994. _ The Wedding Banquet_ won the Golden Bear at the 43rd Berlin Film Festival in 1993[^49]. Then he moved west to Hollywood: _ Crouching Tiger, Hidden Dragon_ (2001) won four Oscars at the 73rd Academy Awards, including Best Foreign Language Film, and became the first non-English film in US history to gross over $100 million, selling $213.5 million globally[^50]. In 2006, _ Brokeback Mountain_ made him the first Asian director to win the Oscar for Best Director; in 2013, _ Life of Pi_ made him the first Asian director to win this award twice[^51].

Ang Lee's existence complicates the term "local film history." When local theaters could hardly see Taiwanese films, a Taiwanese director was on the highest stage of Hollywood, telling the world's stories in English, in Mandarin, and in various languages. He is the pride of Taiwanese cinema, but his success was, in a sense, precisely because he left the dying local market. This is another answer to the question "getting on the screen": Sometimes, to get on the world screen, you must first leave your own island.

## Five Languages Return to the Screen Together

On August 22, 2008, a film called _Cape No. 7_ was released. No one predicted what would happen next.

Director Wei Te-sheng was not a big star at the time. To raise the 50 million NTD budget for this film, he mortgaged his house and borrowed 30 million NTD[^52]. Then this film grossed 530 million NTD across the island, with 230 million in Taipei and 300 million outside Taipei[^53]. It was the box office champion for eight consecutive weeks. The market share of local films jumped from the 0.36% bottom in 2003 to 12.09% in 2008, multiplying by over thirty times in between[^54].

```tw-figure

```

But the most important thing about _Cape No. 7_ was not the box office numbers, but what it brought back to the screen.

In this film, five languages spoke simultaneously: Mandarin, Taiwanese, Japanese, English, and Paiwan[^3]. A failed band leader spoke Mandarin mixed with Taiwanese; the town representatives spoke authentic Taiwanese; a love letter spanning sixty years was written in Japanese; and there was the indigenous Paiwan language. This was neither a "Mandarin film" nor a "Taiwanese-language film"; it was simply what Taiwan originally looked like, with various languages mixed together, none suppressing the others.

Pull the lens back to the beginning. The _benshi_ in the 1930 theater used Taiwanese to dub the silent screen that could not speak. In between, nearly eighty years passed: Taiwanese-language films were strangled, the Mandarin policy dominated, and the New Cinema quietly brought dialects back into art films. By 2008's _Cape No. 7_, the screen could finally righteously speak five languages at once, and all of Taiwan queued up to watch. The third resurrection of local cinema was essentially a restoration of language: Taiwanese people heard their own everyday speaking voices on the big screen again.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** _Cape No. 7_ is often questioned by elites as "commercially successful, but artistically inferior to the masters of the New Cinema." This question is correct, but it asks the wrong question. _Cape No. 7_ was never trying to solve "how deep to shoot"; it was trying to solve "are Taiwanese people willing to spend money to go to the theater to watch their own stories"—something the New Cinema failed to solve for those ten years. Its historical significance is not how good it is, but that it proved one thing: a film using Taiwan's own language, telling the stories of Taiwanese people, can be both critically acclaimed and commercially successful. Half a century after the Taiwanese-language films were deprived of that thing—using their own language to make films well and still selling—the _Cape No. 7_ fought for it back.

Wei Te-sheng did not stop. In 2011, he made _Warlords of the Pacific_ (Sedek Baklai), produced by John Woo, based on the 1930 Wushe Incident, telling that history of resisting Japan from the indigenous perspective. The film cost about 720 million NTD; the two parts, _Sunrise Flag_ and _Rainbow Bridge_, grossed a total of about 810 million NTD across the island[^55]. It represented Taiwan in the race for the Oscar for Best Foreign Language Film, making the top nine, but ultimately did not make the top five[^56]. That year, the local film market share surged to 17.46%, setting a historical high at the time[^54]. In 2014, he produced _KANO_, directed by Ma Chih-hsiang, telling the story of the 1931 Kainan Agricultural School baseball team making it to the Koshien Stadium[^57].

The wave ignited by _Cape No. 7_ was far more than just Wei Te-sheng; it was the return of the entire genre. In 2010, Ni Zhenze's _ Monga_ and in 2011, Nine Calls's _ You Are the Apple of My Eye_ (which grossed 425 million NTD, becoming one of the most successful Chinese-language films in Hong Kong history); in 2015, _ My Girl_ grossed 410 million NTD[^58]. Taiwanese people got used to "going to the theater to watch local films" again. Local cinema came back to life, and this time, it learned how to speak to the audience.

## The Decade When Local Films Were Box Office Poison, and Its Reverse

Let's rewind the timeline to see clearly what _Cape No. 7_ saved.

"Local Films = Box Office Poison" was the six-character phrase that represented the true collective impression of local films in Taiwanese society from the 1990s to 2007[^59]. Producers dared not invest, theaters dared not screen, and audiences did not want to watch, forming a dead loop that no one could turn. It was an era when you would be laughed at if you told your friends, "I'm going to watch a local film."

But this decade's "poison" label and the Taiwanese-language film era's "crudely made" stigma are actually two versions of the same mechanism: an industry, after losing its conditions, was defined in reverse by its results. The Taiwanese-language films had their negatives and funding stripped; the local films after the New Cinema had their market and channels stripped. The difference is that the latter eventually waited for the cure of _Cape No. 7_, while the former did not.

This is why understanding local film history cannot just look at those award-winning masters. A complete local film history is the Taiwanese-language film industry, the Healthy Realism policy, the genres of wuxia and patriotic films, the art of the New Cinema, the poison of the low period, and the revival after _Cape No. 7_—all of these added together make up what Taiwanese cinema truly is. Picking out only the four world-class names of Hou, Yang, Tsai, and Li is equivalent to seeing only the brightest spots in a larger history.

## Contemporary: Awards, Box Office, and the Politics of an Award

After _Cape No. 7_, Taiwanese cinema entered a more mature and complex stage.

On the auteur film side, Chung Meng-hung's _ Sunlight After the Rain_ (2019) won Best Feature Film at the 56th Golden Horse Awards, representing Taiwan in the race for the Oscar, making the top 15 preliminary selections, but not making the final five[^60]. Huang Hsin-yao's _ The Great Buddha+_ (2017) won five awards at the 54th Golden Horse Awards in one go; in the same ceremony, Yang Ya-che's _ Soul_ won Best Feature Film[^61].

There is one more special film to mention: _ The Pig, the Snake and the Pigeon_ (2023), directed by Huang Ching-fu. In Taiwan, it only grossed 47 million NTD, hardly a blockbuster; but in 2024, after its release in China, it exploded in popularity, grossing 665 million RMB[^64]. A Taiwanese film was mediocre in its own market but became a phenomenon on the other side of the strait. This itself illustrates how subtle the cross-strait cultural tension is in contemporary Taiwanese cinema.

And the theaters themselves are facing another crisis. The total box office of Taiwanese cinemas dropped from about 10.1 billion NTD in 2019 to about 6 billion NTD in 2024, evaporating 40%; at the same time, Netflix's streaming market share in Taiwan surged to 83%[^65]. Audiences are not not watching films; they have turned their living rooms back into theaters, repeating the same script as when Taiwan Television launched in 1962, moving gezaixi audiences from theaters into their homes. Streaming platforms are also investing heavily in Taiwanese film and television content; series like _ The Light_ (Hua Deng Chu Shang), _ Tea Gold_ (Cha Jin), _ The Magicians of the Sky Bridge_ (Tian Qiao Shang de Mo Shu Shi), and _ Sakurado_ are drawing resources from the big screen to the small screen[^66].

Finally, we return to the award called the Golden Horse.

In November 2018, at the 55th Golden Horse Awards, director Fu Yu's documentary _ Our Youth, in Taiwan_ won Best Documentary. In her acceptance speech, she said: "I hope that one day, our country can be regarded as a truly independent entity. This is my greatest wish as a Taiwanese person."[^67] This remark sparked huge controversy across the strait. Ang Lee, who hosted the ceremony that night, later told reporters: "Taiwan is free and the film festival is open. You can say whatever you want to say."[^68] The following year, on August 7, 2019, China's State Administration of Film announced the suspension of mainland Chinese films and personnel participating in the Golden Horse Awards; China's Golden Rooster Awards deliberately scheduled its ceremony on the same day as the Golden Horse[^69].

How to view this matter, everyone has their own judgment. Taiwan.md is only responsible for recording the facts clearly here: An award originally regarded as a neutral hall for Chinese-language cinema was drawn into the political struggle between the two sides of the strait because of an acceptance speech. And you will find that the core of this controversy is actually the same as the core of the entire local film history: On the screen, on the stage, who should speak, in what identity, and about what kind of Taiwan.

## Conclusion: They Have Been Shouting This for Seventy Years

Returning to the _benshi_ standing beside the screen at the beginning.

The image of him dubbing the silent film in Taiwanese, and the five languages echoing together in the theater in 2008's _Cape No. 7_, are separated by nearly eighty years. In those eighty years, Taiwanese cinema was declared dead at least three times: The Taiwanese-language films were strangled by policy, the post-New Cinema films were drowned by the market, and the ten-year low period was called "box office poison." But it came back to life again and again, and every resurrection was Taiwan reclaiming one thing: Using our own language, portraying our own faces, and putting our own stories on the screen.

So the next time you see a headline in the news saying "Local films are dead again," you will probably remember: They have been shouting this for seventy years.

[^63]: [A Boy and a Girl - Wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E9%97%9C%E6%96%BC%E6%88%91%E5%92%8C%E9%AC%BC%E8%AE%8A%E6%88%90%E5%AE%B6%E4%BA%BA%E7%9A%84%E9%82%A3%E4%BB%B6%E4%BA%8B) — _A Boy and a Girl_ (2023) grossed 363 million and reached No. 7 on the Netflix Global Non-English Film Chart.

[^64]: [The Pig, the Snake and the Pigeon - Wikipedia / Box Office Reports](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%91%A8%E8%99%95%E9%99%A4%E4%B8%89%E5%AE%B3) — Huang Ching-fu's _The Pig, the Snake and the Pigeon_ (2023) grossed about 47 million in Taiwan; after its release in China in 2024, it grossed 665 million RMB.

[^65]: [Taiwan Cinema Box Office and Streaming Market Share - Industry Report](https://www.twreporter.org/) — Taiwan cinema total box office dropped from about 10.1 billion in 2019 to about 6 billion in 2024 (evaporating about 40%); during the same period, Netflix Taiwan streaming market share was about 83%.

[^66]: [Streaming Investment in Taiwanese Film and TV Content - Industry Report](https://www.twreporter.org/) — Netflix, Disney+ invested in Taiwanese series like _The Light_ (Hua Deng Chu Shang), _Tea Gold_ (Cha Jin), _The Magicians of the Sky Bridge_ (Tian Qiao Shang de Mo Shu Shi), _Sakurado_, drawing resources from the big screen to streaming.

Speech verbatim: "I hope that one day, our country can be regarded as a truly independent entity. This is my greatest wish as a Taiwanese person."

[^70]: [History of Taiwanese Cinema (Japanese Colonial Period) - Wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E5%8F%B0%E7%81%A3%E9%9B%BB%E5%BD%B1) — In 1925, the Taiwan Film Research Society led by Liu Xiyang produced _Who is to Blame?_, considered the first feature film produced by Taiwanese people (distinguished from the 1922 Japanese director Tanaka Kinji's _The Pupil of the Great Buddha_, which had Taiwanese actors).

[^71]: [The Bell of Sayon - Wikipedia](https://zh.wikipedia.org/wiki/%E8%8E%8E%E9%9F%B5%E4%B9%8B%E9%90%98) — _The Bell of Sayon_ (1943) was directed by Shimizu Hiro, starring Li Xianglan, co-produced by Shochiku, Manchukuo, and the Taiwan Governor-General's Office, turning the 1938 drowning incident of the Atayal girl Sayon into Japanization propaganda.
