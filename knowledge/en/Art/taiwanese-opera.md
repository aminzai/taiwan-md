> **30-second overview:** Gezixi did not begin in a theater. It began with the _lāndì sǎi_ of 19th-century Yilan temple fairs. It entered commercial theaters in the 1920s, then passed through radio, film, and television, surviving by constantly changing stages. To preserve it today, we cannot save only the name of the play—we must also save the actors' singing, reciting, spoken dialogue, footwork, and the ability to respond to the audience in real time.

At the end of the 19th century, in the temple fairs, under the trees, and along the streets of Yilan, there was a form of performance that had no stage. The actors were mostly rural men from ordinary families. They did not wear costumes or apply makeup. They sang songs and performed the movements of _chēgǔ_ (drum percussion). This was the early form of what later became known as gezixi—"local gezixi."[^1] In the 1920s, it entered commercial theaters, absorbing the movements, repertoires, and stage techniques of _luànbān_ (scattered gongs), _wàijiāng_ (external rivers), Jiujia opera, and other drama forms.[^4]

The most memorable thing about gezixi is that it never locked "tradition" into a fixed version. It survived by relying on improvisation in temple fairs, learning how to sell tickets in theaters, transforming its movements into sound in radio, and breaking the stage into camera shots in television. Each transition took it further from its original form, yet also gave it more time to live.[^1] [^2]

## A Play Without a Stage

The performance of local gezixi in Yilan originally required no set design, nor did it have the thick costumes familiar today. The National Center for Traditional Arts summarizes this form as "no stage" and "no makeup": rural youth performing in temple fairs, squares, or under trees, singing and acting according to the atmosphere on site, known as _lāndì sǎi_ or _tǔdiǎn xī_ (ground-level plays).[^5]

The term "gezixi" itself is related to the storytelling art of Zhangzhou in Fujian Province. _Chēgǔ_ provided the musical and dance movements of the play. In Yilan, where there were many Zhangzhou immigrants, these melodies met local life and gradually formed their own performance style. This origin is not suitable to be described as a simple transplantation, nor can it be said to be a pure creation without any foreign influence. It is more like a group of people rearranging old materials into a play in a new place.[^1] [^5]

In the early days, local gezixi was often described as being performed by amateur male actors, with relatively simple repertoires and melodies. The performance venues were part of daily life. The audience might be watching the temple fair, passing by the square, or sitting down next to acquaintances to listen. This kind of close-distance performance later became the foundation of gezixi's "live performance": the actors knew when the audience was laughing, and they knew which line needed to be paused a little longer.[^1] [^2]

Later, gezixi developed the sheng (male lead), dan (female lead), jing (painted face), and chou (clown) roles. Actors not only had to remember the identity of their characters, but also learn their own footwork, hand gestures, singing style, and movements. The flower face (decorative painted face) was originally introduced from Peking opera and other major plays, which shows how gezixi transformed foreign stage vocabulary into its own performance system.[^1]

## Why It Quickly Became Grand Drama

When gezixi spread outward from Yilan, the original play alone was not enough to cope with the new competition. In the 1920s, commercial theaters flourished, and gezixi entered the inner theater, beginning to use lighting, sound, set design, and new scripts, while absorbing a large number of other dramas' repertoires, character roles, and movements.[^2] [^4]

This absorption was not incidental decoration, but the way the genre was formed. The singing style and recitative of gezixi used Hokkien, which most audiences could understand. The melodies and stories were also easy to integrate into daily life. On the other hand, it incorporated elements of northern and southern pipes, Peking opera, and folk songs into its own body.[^1] [^2]

The Cultural Heritage Bureau divides its development into several interconnected scenes: the _lāndì sǎi_ gezixi parade, wild stage gezixi, inner theater gezixi, radio, film, television, and theater. This route is not like a straight line that eliminates old forms, but more like a troupe constantly adding new exits to find the audience.[^4]

Wild stage plays are still connected with temple fairs, festivals, and vow-making ceremonies. Before the main play, there is often a "playing immortals" performance, which first celebrates the deities and prays for the believers. Historical plays can perform history and legends, while night plays may add currently popular songs and new stories. Thus, gezixi maintains the time of ritual while also facing the market's demand for freshness.[^1]

## The War Moved Taiwanese Into the Shadows

After the outbreak of the Second Sino-Japanese War in 1937, the Japanese colonial government promoted the policy of assimilation, and gezixi was strictly restricted. Troupes were required to change their names, rewrite their content, and even wear kimonos, carry samurai swords, and sing Japanese songs. Some performers kept the traditional content when the police were not around, and quickly changed to a compliant performance when the inspection came close.[^2]

This history gave "adaptation" an uncomfortable weight. Gezixi did survive by changing its appearance, but that was not free innovation—it was a survival technique forced by power. After the war, in the 1950s, the troupes quickly recovered, and inner theater gezixi formed a new peak. Later, radio, film, and television took over the transformation again.[^2]

A memory of Hong Xue Snow makes that era more than just a year. She said: "Before, fans had to save money and buy tickets to see me, and they could only see me by entering the theater garden." That was the distance between the inner theater star and the audience that had to be established with a ticket. Later, television brought this distance directly into the home. Actors no longer had to wait for the audience to enter the theater, and the audience could also wait for the show to start during meal time.[^2]

## Yang Li Hua and the Young Actor in Television

After gezixi entered television, one of the most recognizable figures was Yang Li Hua. According to the Taipei City Cultural Bureau, she performed in more than 100 TV gezixi plays from 1966 to 1980, and in 1981 she started a gezixi training class to train the next generation of actors.[^7]

Yang Li Hua's significance is not only in the number of performances. She brought the image, singing style, and role charm of the young actor into every household, and allowed gezixi to cross the geographical boundaries of the temple fair and the theater. TV gezixi thus became a common memory of a generation, but it also faced a problem: when the audience understood gezixi as a TV screen, could they still recognize the improvisation and movements of the temple fair?[^6] [^9]

The Reporter's collection of Yang Li Hua and her era presents an untidy answer. Scholar Su Shibo recalls that before and after the lifting of martial law, cultural evaluations once regarded TV gezixi as being included by politics and capital. But he also pointed out that if gezixi had not made compromises in different eras, it might have disappeared long ago.[^6]

The contradiction here is important: those who want to preserve tradition are not necessarily opposed to change. Those who change form are not necessarily betraying tradition. Yang Li Hua's path from radio, film, television to the national-level theater shows that the vitality of gezixi does not lie in its eternal sameness, but in its ability to turn new media into its own stage.[^7] [^9]

## "Live Performance" Meets the City

Another key word of gezixi is "live performance." Most of Taiwan's private drama troupes have to face temple fair invitations, performance fees, venues, and audience reactions, and the actors maintain the tradition of interacting with the scene and making temporary adjustments. In contrast, modern theaters require scripts, directors, music design, stage design, and rehearsals.[^2]

Neither of the two is naturally more authentic. The freedom of the temple fair may bring closeness, but it may not have sufficient sound and subtitles. The fine production of the theater can allow more people to understand, but it may turn a play that would respond to the audience into the same version every night. Gezixi's current problem is to allow these two advantages to meet.

Urbanization makes this problem concrete. Temple fairs that can set up a stage in the city are becoming fewer, noise control and performance costs are increasing, and young audiences may not understand local Taiwanese opera without subtitles. The National Center for Traditional Arts' report lists "developing audiences and actors" as an urgent issue, and also records the troupe's attempt to use subtitles and "outer cultural venues" to shorten the distance between the theater and the temple fair.[^2]

Therefore, the inheritance of gezixi cannot only be done by performing the classic repertoire again. It also needs to allow learners to come into contact with the singing style, spoken dialogue, footwork, and background music, to understand which parts can be improvised and which parts must be accumulated over the years. Inheritance is actually a set of bodily knowledge, not just copying the name of the play into a catalog.

## Preserving a Moving Art

The cultural heritage system has already established a list of preservers and preservation groups for gezixi. The Taipei City Cultural Bureau announced in 2024 that Yang Li Hua was registered as a preserver of gezixi, and the data also recorded her long-term involvement in teaching and promotion and her experience in different performance forms.[^7]

Preservation has also begun to use digital tools. The National Center for Traditional Arts announced the second phase of gezixi artist skill preservation in 2026, inviting Lin Meixiang, Chen Zhaoxiang, and Zhang Xiujing to demonstrate their skilled repertoires, decompose the singing, reciting, footwork, and character movements, so that the knowledge that was originally only flowing on the stage between the master and apprentice has one more way to be viewed, studied, and taught.[^8]

The rehearsal photo pulls the inheritance back to the moment before going on stage. The practice taken in Yilan in 2020 is not a completed performance, but the actors who repeatedly practice and grind out their movements, rhythm, and mutual understanding.

But digital records cannot replace the risk of the body on stage. The camera can save a sleeve movement, but it cannot bear the weight that the learner knows only after a long time of practice. Subtitles can help the audience keep up with the plot, but they cannot automatically generate the rhythm of mutual exploration between the actors and the audience. The value of preservation lies in making these limitations clear, rather than claiming that a video is equivalent to the completion of inheritance.[^8]

The local gezixi in Yilan was registered as an important traditional performance art in 2012, and the Zhuang San New Cool Troupe was designated as the preservation group. This decision preserves not a fixed "ancient version", but allows people to see where gezixi grew from: no stage, no makeup, a song and dance play performed by ordinary people in villages and temple fairs.[^5]

## Where Is the Next Temple Fair?

The history of gezixi has never been as simple as "traditional decline, modern rescue." It has been criticized as vulgar, and it has been restricted by the state. It went from the temple fair to the theater, from the radio to the television, and then from the television to the national theater. Every new location brings new audiences and new trade-offs.[^2] [^3] [^6]

So the most counterintuitive thing about gezixi is: it survives by changing. It is not because it rejects foreign elements that it becomes the drama of Taiwan. It is in Yilan that the Zhangzhou gezixi, _chēgǔ_, local ballads, other dramas, Taiwanese vernacular, and modern media are mixed together, and slowly grow into a voice that can be recognized by the people of Taiwan.[^1] [^4]

The next time you encounter gezixi in the temple fair, the theater, or the online video, you can first pay attention to a small thing: after the actor finishes a line, is there a response from the audience? That response may be a laugh, silence, or someone finally understanding a sentence of Taiwanese. In the end, whether gezixi can continue to move forward still depends on whether there is still a place where such a response can occur.
