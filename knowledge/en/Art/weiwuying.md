> **30-second overview:** The National Kaohsiung Center for the Arts sits in Kaohsiung's Fengshan District. Its predecessor was a heavily fortified military base that officially opened on October 13, 2018. It houses an opera house, concert hall, drama theater, and performance hall, yet it has never confined itself to a ticketed box: the banyan-like rooftop plaza lets strolling, tai chi, yoga, public pianos, and performances share the same space. The building's real challenge has never been how many seats it can hold, but whether a national-level cultural facility can truly be used by the city.

In 1992, Kaohsiung's Wei Wu Ying was still a military base sealed off from the city by high walls. A civil movement called the "Southern Green Revolution" fought not to replace the site with another enclosed large-scale development, but to preserve the land as a metropolitan ecological park and plan an international-grade performance venue in the south. This history gives Wei Wu Ying an unusual starting point: before the theaters were even designed, what was being demanded was whether people could get close to this land.[^1]

This land memory was not completely erased. The main building takes the old banyan tree as its spatial imagination, while the surrounding area still preserves the context of the metropolitan park and old barracks. Later planning did not force all activities into the new building, but let the barracks, green space, and new halls each take on different public functions. Wei Wu Ying thus resembles a cultural landscape that grew gradually, rather than a white landmark that descended from the sky on opening day.[^4] [^7]

On October 13, 2018, the National Kaohsiung Center for the Arts opened. From planning to completion, the project spanned three presidents and lasted 15 years. The Presidential Office described the venue in its opening coverage as a symbol of "spatial liberalization" and "cultural rights," because the originally fortified military land, after long advocacy by civil groups, finally became a shared public space.[^2]

## 1992: People Outside the Walls Decided Wei Wu Ying's Fate First

The government-approved construction plan already wrote cultural citizenship into its goals: letting the public participate in performing arts, and giving southern performance groups a complete venue. The planned schedule was from 2005 to 2019 (Republic of China years 94 to 108), with a budget of approximately NT$10.75 billion. These numbers are large, but they point to a very concrete imbalance: large national-level performance facilities have long been concentrated in Taipei, and southern audiences must first pay the cost of transportation and time before they can sit in halls of the same scale.[^3]

This project also stretched Kaohsiung's urban scale. Smithsonian explained Wei Wu Ying's volume to English readers in 1.5 million square feet. Conversions between different units easily make numbers lose their feeling, but placing them back on site makes it clear: this is not a theater with a few extra rows of seats, but a massive public machine that must simultaneously handle the city, the park, the backstage, the audience, and fire safety.[^11]

Kaohsiung City Government's retrospective placed this land even further back in time. It was originally a fortress against shelling and flooding, and after the war became a new recruit training ground. After the Cold War ended and military use withdrew, the 1992 civil movement turned this land into a metropolitan park, and placed the idea of a "southern dual-venue" within the greenery. The barracks, green space, and theaters thus were not three unrelated terms, but the same piece of land being repurposed across different eras.[^4]

This also explains why Wei Wu Ying cannot simply be understood as a construction project. Both the Presidential Office and Kaohsiung City Government point out that this military space later stopped using walls to decide who could enter. The building itself thus carries the memory of public institutions.[^2] [^4]

## 4,560 Metal Plates, First Learning to Breathe Like a Banyan Tree

When Dutch architect Francesca Pagliano first saw Wei Wu Ying, what caught her attention was not a plot waiting to be filled, but the old banyan tree. The official introduction states that the building's streamlined form comes from the image of the tree's canopy, branches, and roots intertwined; the official architect profile at Wei Wu Ying also summarizes Pagliano's method as starting from the local environment and human use, first understanding the local climate, culture, and needs, and then deciding how the architecture should settle.[^5] [^6]

The roof looks like a white wave. The Executive Yuan's materials break it down into 4,560 irregular metal panels, using the manta ray and Kaohsiung harbor waves as urban imagery. This is not simply "decorating" Kaohsiung onto the building's exterior: the metal roof simultaneously provides shade, guides wind, and gathers the originally scattered opera house, concert hall, drama theater, and performance hall into one continuous space.[^3]

The architect's office Mecanoo's project page explains that the center was built on the former military terrain and integrated with the adjacent subtropical park. The banyan plaza under the roof is a protected public space where residents can pass through, practice tai chi, or perform street shows informally at day or night. The core of the architecture is therefore not a main entrance, but a space for wandering, staying, and unexpected encounters in the shadows.[^7]

ArchDaily's 2010 architectural report introduced Wei Wu Ying at the northeast corner of the metropolitan park, saying it served as Kaohsiung's green lung.[^9] Under the roof folding down to the ground, it accommodates an outdoor theater, while the surrounding park becomes its stage — this passage was written by Mecanoo's own project description. This arrangement extends "watching a performance" from an indoor ticketed event to viewing, resting, and passing by in the park.[^8]

This roof also binds different professions into a single project. The Executive Yuan's materials list irregular panels, acoustic specifications, and pipe organs in the same plan summary. Mecanoo pointed out that the curved steel frame was completed through cooperation between Taiwan and the Netherlands' shipbuilding industries. Kaohsiung's port experience thus did not only appear in a symbolic form, but also entered the process of how the building is manufactured.[^3] [^7]

For a performance venue, this is an important difference. The audience sees the white wave, while workers face metal panels, acoustics, backstage, wind and rain, and evacuation paths; when these invisible systems work together, the banyan plaza can maintain that seemingly natural openness.[^7] [^12]

## Before Anyone Buys a Ticket, the Banyan Plaza Is Already in Use

Wei Wu Ying's official description calls this space the "art center of the people." The total site area is 9.9 hectares, with a building area of 3.3 hectares. The banyan plaza extending from the roof is open to the public 24 hours a day, and the venue can be entered from all sides. This openness is not only about circulation design, but also asks whether a national-level venue can accept visits without a clear purpose: some come for appointments, some come to avoid rain, and some just pass through.[^9]

Shortly after opening in 2018, this question quickly received an on-site answer. The Guardian reported that Wei Wu Ying's open day attracted over 50,000 people. People took off their shoes, exercised, lay in the shade, and even sang while walking into the concert hall. These behaviors are not within traditional theater audience norms, yet they precisely illustrate what Pagliano wanted as "informal": people do not need to first become an audience that knows how to enter the theater to be eligible to use the space.[^10]

Kaohsiung City Government's 2022 article also recorded similar daily scenes: people strolling in the park, visiting markets, walking their children, having picnics, with the banyan plaza regularly hosting yoga and outdoor movies. These activities do not mean everyone buys tickets, but they let the art center no longer awaken only during performance hours.[^4]

## Four Halls Hide Sound in Five Cores

Wei Wu Ying's publicness does not come at the cost of sacrificing professional venues. The four indoor halls listed by the Executive Yuan include a 2,260-seat opera house, a 1,254-seat drama theater, a 2,000-seat concert hall, and a 470-seat performance hall, totaling 5,984 seats indoors; Wei Wu Ying's official introduction also emphasizes the openness of the four indoor halls and outdoor spaces, with the banyan plaza available for daily public use.[^3] [^9]

| Space            | Seats       | Problems Handled                                                                              |
| ---------------- | ----------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Opera House      | 2,260 seats | Providing sufficient scale for large-scale opera, dance, and stage machinery                  |
| Concert Hall     | 2,000 seats | Vineyard-style seating and pipe organ accommodating symphony and various sounds               |
| Drama Theater    | 1,254 seats | Sharing space for drama, traditional opera, and performances requiring flexible configuration |
| Performance Hall | 470 seats   | Providing smaller-scale, experimental, and intimate performance spaces                        |

The significance of this table is not to turn Wei Wu Ying into a list of specifications, but to see that "cultural rights" requires multiple scales. With only the largest hall, the south would still lack a place for small-scale creation; with only small theaters, it could not accommodate international-grade productions. Wei Wu Ying places four different distances under the same roof, letting the venue face both large-scale programs and works that are harder to see.[^3] [^5]

The pipe organ in the concert hall has 9,085 pipes, one of the easiest numbers to remember about Wei Wu Ying. Smithsonian's English report pointed out that this center includes five performance spaces, listing the pipe organ alongside the 2,260-seat opera house and 2,000-seat concert hall as features of the venue. These numbers here are not about showing off scale, but about giving a southern city an organ that can receive different sounds.[^11]

The Guardian further described how the concert hall handles the contradiction between "large" and "close": although there are 2,000 seats, the farthest seat is about 30 meters from the conductor; the acoustic design also used model testing to try to let classical orchestras, jazz, and Taiwanese indigenous peoples' brass bands all find suitable echoes. For the audience, the value of the venue is not only about accommodating more people, but about letting different forms of performance not be flattened by the same sound standard.[^10]

These professional details also bring another layer of reality. Engineering research collected by Springer uses Wei Wu Ying as a case study to discuss the integration of performance-based fire safety design in smoke control, active protection, and fire evacuation review. The more open, composite, and indoor-outdoor the architecture is, the more it cannot be solved by a beautiful roof alone; publicness ultimately still depends on the details of engineering, regulations, and management.[^10]

## A NT$10.75 Billion Building Must Answer Who Can Use It

If Wei Wu Ying's story stops at "military base becomes theater," it misses the cost of the project itself. The Liberty Times reported that Wei Wu Ying signed the planning and design contract in 2007, broke ground on April 7, 2010, originally estimated to be completed in 2014, but was delayed due to multiple design modifications and structural and procedural issues until 2018; in 2020, Taipei District Court's first-instance ruling ordered the Ministry of Culture to pay the architect's office approximately NT$128,887,200, and the Ministry of Culture stated it would appeal. These disputes will not make the building lose its value, but they remind us: cultural rights are not a single opening speech, but a series of public resources, design choices, and responsibility distributions.[^12]

The Guardian's commentary raised another question: Kaohsiung's population was about 3 million at the time, yet there was only one 1,600-seat theater; can a massive complex venue that houses multiple halls continue to fill up after the novelty wears off? This question does not contradict the ideal of "no walls." Precisely because Wei Wu Ying wants to become the city's living room, it must be tested: who stays here, who can afford the tickets, and who still only passes by under the roof.[^10]

The government's planning for the opening season also shows how this venue is expected to be used. From October to December 2018, over 10 weeks, 41 paid programs were planned, accompanied by other public activities. This arrangement shows on one hand that the venue needs intensive programs to build audience habits; on the other hand, it also exposes the long-term challenges of large venues: the spotlight of opening day can bring people in, but sustained programs, education, and community relationships determine whether people will come again.[^3]

Kaohsiung City Government's records of the three consecutive old barracks provide another possible answer. These historically significant buildings are later planned to be converted into creation, theater, exhibition, and dance spaces, letting artists and small theater activities spread from the main building to the park. If Wei Wu Ying wants to become the city's cultural infrastructure, it cannot rely only on a single massive body; it must also let the surrounding space accommodate smaller, slower, and more everyday creations.[^4]

## Looking Back at Kaohsiung from the Theater Roof

Pagliano's description in the design office places Wei Wu Ying within Kaohsiung's transformation from an international port to a multicultural city; Mecanoo believes the continuity between the building and the park can let formal performances and informal performances permeate each other. This vision will not be automatically completed by a single building; it must be used up one by one by passersby, rehearsing teams, ticket-buying audiences, and children resting in the plaza.

This path also makes Wei Wu Ying and Kaohsiung's urban transformation mutually drive each other. The Ministry of Culture regards it as the flagship art space in southern Taiwan, while the architect's office explicitly connects the former military base with Kaohsiung's port city transformation. These statements contain policy vision and cannot be directly equated with the city having already completed its transformation; but at the very least, they provide a testable direction: whether the venue truly places artists, audiences, residents, and parks on the same daily path.[^9] [^7]

Therefore, Wei Wu Ying's most important performance may not be any opening program. It is more like a daily repeated action: a person walks out from the memory of the walls of the old military base, passes through the roof intertwined like banyan roots, and before deciding whether to watch a show, sits down to rest in the shade. The theater that is truly opened is not just a door, but the path where Kaohsiung people meet culture.
