> **30-second overview:** Completed in 1908, the Red House was never originally a theater or cultural landmark—it began as a public market serving residents outside Taipei's Ximen Gate. Built from an octagonal hall and a cruciform hall, it once sold flowers, medicine, books, and local Taiwanese goods, later transforming into a storytelling venue, theater, and cinema, before entering the realm of historic preservation and adaptive reuse. What makes this building worth seeing goes beyond red bricks and octagonal shapes—it is a structure that had to repeatedly return to people's daily lives to avoid becoming a hollow shell. [^1] [^2] [^3]

In 1908, Ximending was not yet the neon-lit, cinema-studded, pedestrian-heavy district it is today. The Xinji Market opened outside Taipei's Ximen Gate, with an octagonal entrance facing the street and a cruciform market body behind it. Those who entered might have come for groceries or simply to view a new kind of public building. [^2]

Before it stood, a simple wooden market already existed outside Ximen Gate in 1896. Twelve years later, the government converted the market into a more permanent and manageable urban facility; the early name "Market Octagonal Hall" referred not to a scenic historic site for admiration, but to a place that handled daily transactions, odors, crowds, and order. [^2]

**Curator's note:** The Red House's first identity was not a cultural landmark, but the city's way of centralizing the mundane act of "shopping." Its ability to later carry culture did not stem from being designed as a cultural space from the start, but from its enduring connection to people.

## The Octagonal Hall Conceals a Set of Urban Engineering

The designer of the Red House was Kondo Jūrō, then serving in the Taiwan Governor's Office's Public Works Department. The octagonal hall was built with red bricks, its floors reinforced with steel-reinforced concrete, and its roof supported by a radial steel truss system. These materials and structures made it more than just a "red, photogenic old building"—it was an engineering project that brought modern construction techniques into a public market. [^2] [^4]

The octagonal entrance and cruciform body were not designed purely for aesthetics. According to the Ministry of Culture's archives, it represents a fusion of bagua and cross-shaped architectural forms: the former forming the market entrance, the latter creating the main space. The historic designation documents further note that multiple orientations of entry made the market more convenient to use, while the red brick masonry and Fink-style trusses preserved the construction techniques of the time. [^2] [^4]

Inside the market, goods once sold included flowers, books, medicine, Japanese pickles, and local Taiwanese products; during festivals, textiles and dry goods would also appear. This list is significant because it reminds us: the Red House was never a building serving only a particular artistic taste—it mixed everyday goods, imported items, local products, and festive shopping from the very beginning. [^2] [^3]

Today we often separate "markets" and "culture" into distinct domains, but the early life of the Red House was far less neat. A market is economic, but also social; it is public health, but also a place where one can meet others. The value of a building has never been only in its facade, but in how that facade arranges proximity between people.

## As the City Moved East, the Red House Stayed Behind

After the war, the Red House did not immediately become a static historical specimen. It served as a storytelling venue, the Huayuan Theater, and a cinema, welcoming audiences across different eras. The official English history page describes the 1940s and 1950s as an era when theater and narrative culture converged, while the 1960s saw it become a gateway for young people to watch films and encounter Western culture. [^3] [^5]

This transition was not merely "an old building changing functions." It also reflected the movement of Taipei's urban center: as commerce and entertainment gradually shifted eastward, Ximending once lost its central position, and the Red House declined along with it. The building did not move—only the city's attention did. [^3]

In 1997, the Red House was designated a historic site; restoration was completed the following year, but the path ahead was not a straight line of revival. The challenge it faced was: who would use the restored building? How much should it charge? What activities should it host? The cost of preservation could not be paid for with a single sign. [^2] [^3]

**Curator's note:** The most dangerous moment for a historic site is often not the day it is demolished, but the day it is restored and no one comes anymore. The story of the Red House is not one of "successful preservation," but of preservation constantly being tested by daily life.

## Adaptive Reuse Is Not Just Renting Out an Old Building

In 2002, the Paper Windmill Culture and Education Foundation reconstructed the history of the Red House Theater; in 2007, the Taipei Cultural Foundation took over operations. Both official records and the Red House's English history page treat this period as a pivotal moment of repositioning: the former market building was reintegrated into the networks of arts and cultural performance, creative marketplaces, and Ximending district development. [^2] [^3] [^5]

There is an often-overlooked distinction here. Historic adaptive reuse is not about emptying an old building and inserting a few new shops. It must grapple with how new functions can coexist without erasing the original history. The octagonal hall, cruciform hall, and north-south courtyard of the Red House were placed within a single operational framework, where exhibitions, dining, creative retail, workshops, and performances each played their part while drawing crowds to one another. [^2] [^4]

Taipei Tourism Records noted that one side of the cruciform hall developed into a creative market and cultural innovation center, while the other became a live music venue. This arrangement gave creators places to sell their work and perform, and allowed visitors to Ximending to enter the historic site without first convincing themselves they needed to be "audience members." [^3]

The Red House's official English page also records the 2008 Taipei Architecture Museum's award for historic building reuse, as well as how weekend handicraft markets provided face-to-face interaction opportunities between cultural creators and consumers. The core of this kind of reuse is not replacing an old building's face with a young brand, but allowing the building to once again become a site of exchange. [^5]

In 2024, a red fox named Hongfu walked into this history: invited to an exhibition, it served as a guide to the historic site and as a bridge between heritage and contemporary creativity. TVBS reported that Hongfu was designed with imagery drawn from the Red House's architecture, allowing visitors to begin understanding the building through a relatable character without first reading a century of history. [^9]

Hongfu's significance lies not in adding a cute character to the historic site, but in demonstrating another form of preservation: translating the building's history, beliefs, and design cues into a language that people today are willing to approach. Thus, the historic site does not merely preserve the past—it begins learning how to meet its next audience.

## One Building, Three Kinds of Value That Are Hard to Achieve Simultaneously

The first is the value of cultural heritage. The National Cultural Heritage Database lists the Red House as a municipal historic site of Taipei City, citing its witness to the early development of Taipei during the Japanese colonial period, its preservation of market life history, and its retention of red bricks, slate tiles, steel trusses, and period construction techniques. [^2]

The second is the value of public use. Today's Red House still functions as a venue for performances, dining, creative products, and markets; it is not a building that opens only on special commemorative days, but one that continuously connects strangers, creators, tourists, and neighboring streets into a single flow. [^1] [^4]

The third is the value of operations. A 2025 study using the Red House as a case examined how historic sites can sustain cultural and commercial values through commissioned contracts and "joint production" relationships when both pure commercialization and non-profit management risk failure. This perspective pulls the historic site away from beautiful photographs and back into the realities of governance and responsibility allocation. [^6]

Another study, using the case of shops sold within Taipei's municipal historic sites, reminds us that commercial spaces within historic sites should not merely function as ordinary retail areas; they must become extensions of the site itself, with product displays, business models, and visitor experiences all tied to the cultural meaning of the place. [^7]

Therefore, the success of the Red House cannot be measured solely by "how many people visit." Crowds can animate a building, but they can also turn it into nothing more than a backdrop. The real difficulty lies in making those crowds leave behind some understanding: that this place was once a market, that red brick walls contain urban engineering, and that even a marketplace can be part of cultural policy.

**Curator's note:** The best new use for a historic site is not to make it look as though it has never changed, but to let people see that it has changed—and yet has not lost its origin.

## The Red House's Next Question

Today, the Red House appears to be a place that has found its answer: the octagonal hall is a landmark, the cruciform hall houses shops and activities, and the courtyard can accommodate markets and performances. Yet research continues to discuss it precisely because "activation" is not a one-time project, but a relationship that must be renegotiated by every manager and every generation of users. [^6] [^7]

Commerce brings revenue, but it may also reduce a historic site to nothing more than a consumer shell; cultural activities bring meaning, but they may also fail to endure due to a lack of stable resources. These two directions are not naturally harmonious—they must be bound together through contracts, management, design, and public oversight. The history of the Red House does not prove that all historic sites can be successfully activated, but it offers a case that can be tested. [^6]

Next time you pass No. 10 Chengde Road, don't rush to photograph the octagonal hall. Try to imagine the market entrance of 1908, the storytelling venue of the postwar era, the cinema of the 1960s, and the space waiting for its next use after restoration. You will discover that what the Red House has truly preserved is not the appearance of any single era, but the capacity of Taipei's people to repeatedly bring public life back to this place.

It was originally a market. It still is a market—only the things being exchanged have changed: from vegetables, medicine, and textiles, to artworks, performances, memories, and whether a city is willing to let an old building continue participating in today's answer.
