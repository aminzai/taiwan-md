> **30-second overview:** In 1999, Chang Ronggui and Chun Shui Tang created Aguī with Flash. This character with a round face and distinctive voice first circulated through forwarded online clips, then entered online games, music, film, and Japanese websites. By 2003, it had even become the protagonist of SARS prevention campaigns. What makes Aguī most memorable isn't that it was everywhere, but that it showed Taiwanese people for the first time: a net character can be raised together by audiences, and may be left with only voice and fragments after a website stops updating. ## 1999: A Round-Faced Character Pops Up from the Dial-Up Internet

In 1999, Chang Ronggui established Chun Shui Tang Science and Entertainment and began producing the Aguī Flash animation series. Back then, the internet wasn't the endless scroll on smartphones, but a home computer, a school computer class, and the wait for animations to load. Aguī's humor grew from this everyday life: round face, simple design, close-to-life plots, plus a voice you'd never forget. [^1] [^2] [^10]

The starting point was small, but the ambition was not. Reports described Chang Ronggui's vision as "the dream of an E-generation Disney." Meet's startup gathering recorded that the original plan was to use the internet as a training ground for digital cartoons. What was seen first wasn't a feature film, but a few-minute short animation that was easy to watch and forward to classmates. [^1] [^10]

📝 **Curator's note:** Aguī's first stage wasn't a movie theater, but the computer of whoever was willing to forward the link to the next person.

## Characters Are Not Just Settings, They Are People Who Will Be Remembered

Aguī quickly became more than just the protagonist. Grandma Aguī shaved her feet with a kitchen knife, Mom Aguī was good at bargaining, and Teacher Aguī had a mole on her face and was always looking for a good man. These descriptions were effective not because they were detailed, but because every character seemed to come from a Taiwanese family, school, or neighborhood, allowing the audience to laugh first and then recognize someone familiar. [^1]

In the early 2000s, this "local" wasn't a slogan, but a character bringing air, actions, and awkwardness into daily life. Aguī didn't need to explain what kind of animation it was; the audience completed its positioning in the moment of "this is just like my classmate" or "we have someone like that at home too."

This is also one of the differences between Aguī and large-scale animation characters. It didn't polish life into exotic scenery, but took computer class exercises, family chatter, romance, and bargaining directly as jokes. These small things didn't need translation, yet they circulated among different generations in Taiwan.

📝 **Curator's note:** Aguī's sense of local isn't about what the characters wear, but about knowing what small things Taiwanese people would roll their eyes at.

## From Watching Animations to Deciding Together Who Stays

Chun Shui Tang also made online mini-games. Baojiugong mentioned "inflating balloons": players quickly press the specified keys to compete with Aguī and Gangzhang to see who can pop the balloon first. These games were simple, but turned watching into doing. Aguī not only performed on the screen, but also sat next to the user's keyboard. [^1]

In 2001, Chun Shui Tang released the internet cartoon character music album "Aguī's Happy Paradise." The album promotion was designed as "one person, one vote to save the meat ticket": the characters of Chun Shui Town were kidnapped, and the one with the lowest vote count would disappear from the story. Piggy Wukung once lagged behind, and netizens pleaded for her, even figuring out how to write her back into the plot. Reports recorded that the event even caused Aguī's internet servers to be repeatedly overloaded. [^1]

The contrast here is interesting: the physical music market had already begun to decline, yet Aguī used internet voting to create a sensation for the album. The audience's participation wasn't just "like or dislike," but directly changed the fate of the characters. Looking back today, this seems like an early version of social media interaction. Put back in 2001, it was more like a group of people suddenly discovering they could save a side character.

## A Short Animation Begins to Move Outward

Aguī's next stop was a bigger screen. "Aguī Hits You" was released by Chun Shui Tang Science and Entertainment and is included in the Taiwan Film Information Database with its director, screenwriter, and producer information. Multiple sources describe it as an animation film made entirely with Flash. [^3] [^8] [^9]

iThome's 2001 report already recorded the planning of the movie website: audiences could watch trailers and participate in the birth of the film. This arrangement continued Aguī's original habit, allowing the work to not only hand over the finished product to the audience, but also share the production process. [^4]

Aguī also went beyond Taiwan. Taipei Times reported in 2002 that it launched Japanese and Chinese websites. Local cultural units later reviewed Aguī, placing the animation, film, and games in the same line of cultural memory. Its cross-border spread didn't necessarily mean long-term success, but proved that a very Taiwanese sense of humor once had the chance to find other audiences through the internet. [^5] [^6]

📝 **Curator's note:** The internet allowed Aguī to move quickly, but didn't preserve the files left at every stop.

## From Character Licensing to a World That Can Be Used

Aguī's expansion wasn't just about playback counts. Chun Shui Tang put the character into music, online games, film, and promotional activities, effectively breaking up the same set of people into different entry points: those who wanted to listen bought music, those who wanted to operate played games, those who wanted to follow the story watched animations, and parents and children re-encountered Aguī in promotional content. Each form added a new usage scenario for the character. [^1] [^2] [^3]

"Aguī's Happy Paradise" especially shows this transformation. It wasn't just about turning animation soundtracks into an album; the promotional activities also handed the character's fate over to voting. Piggy Wukong's crisis gave the audience a reason to intervene, and netizens' pleas turned online discussions into plot material. The character thus gained a second life: the original team designed the characters, and the audience decided which characters were worth continuing through voting and discussion. [^1]

This model looks very much like social media management today, but back then it had to rely on email, website comments, and limited server capacity. Repeatedly causing server overload was both a technical limitation and a physical trace of popularity. The number of people wasn't just shown on the statistics page, but directly affected whether a website could be opened.

The film project pushed this logic to a larger scale. iThome's 2001 report mentioned that the movie website arranged trailers and participation mechanisms. The Taiwan Film Information Database preserved the production and release data of "Aguī Hits You." From websites to film, Aguī continued with the same question: should the audience watch after the work is completed, or can they enter the production process in advance? [^3] [^4]

## The Technical Imagination and Reality of "Full Flash Film"

Multiple sources call "Aguī Hits You" a full Flash animation film. The meaning of this term is that it pushed the technology, which was mostly seen as web content at the time, into the imagination of long-form narrative and cinema scale. The film data preserved by the Taiwan Film Information Database gave this claim a work title, publisher, and staff to check against. [^3] [^9]

But the technical "full Flash" didn't mean the production was simple. Character design, storyboarding, dubbing, music, action, editing, and output still required collaboration across different specialties. It was just that the image production and playback medium both pointed to Flash. If it's only written as a record of a "first," it would miss what's truly important: a Taiwan content team once tried to use internet-era tools to handle an animation that required long-term viewing.

This also explains Aguī's contradiction. Its recognition came from simplicity, but its ambition required it to enter the complex process of long film production. It relied on the rapid spread of the internet, but film also needed physical release and promotion. The flexibility of short animations and the weight of the film industry met on the same character.

## Japanese Websites and the Export of "Local"

Taipei Times' 2002 report recorded Aguī launching Japanese and Chinese websites. This detail is worth noting because it didn't understand "local" as content that could only circulate within Taiwan. Aguī first used Taiwanese life as a source of humor, then used websites to try to cross language and market boundaries. [^5]

Cross-border didn't mean all jokes could be understood without change. Character appearance, action rhythm, and voice could cross some language barriers, but family relationships, campus vocabulary, and Taiwanese-style complaints needed to be reprocessed. When Aguī went overseas, it truly faced cultural translation: which details must be preserved, and which jokes need a new entry point.

Local cultural units and media later placed Aguī alongside Doraemon, letting it enter the narrative of "Asia's Top Ten Outstanding Figures." This title brought visibility, but also easily flattened the complex history of popularity into a medal. A more accurate reading is: Aguī was seen in the public discourse of Asian popular culture, and its actual influence still needs to be checked one by one against the works, websites, games, and audience memories. [^6] [^7]

📝 **Curator's note:** Aguī's "local" isn't a wall, but a starting point that can carry Taiwanese daily life and then be re-understood by others.

## During SARS, Aguī Took on a Different Mission

As the SARS epidemic rose in 2003, Chun Shui Tang and other game and education teams launched children-oriented epidemic prevention animations and games. Chun Shui Tang's "Aguī's Secrets to Fighting SARS" used animation to explain mask-wearing, hand-washing, ear temperature measurement, and reducing visits to public places, and also referred to those in home quarantine as "living Bodhisattvas." [^1]

The same character, one moment telling jokes about family and school, the next moment explaining to children how the virus spreads. Its credibility didn't come from medical expertise, but from the fact that children already recognized its voice. Public information borrowed familiar characters, and the character finally took on the mission of "letting people understand something important."

This history also reminds us that when a character is used by public institutions, humor and responsibility will enter at the same time. Animation can reduce the sense of distance, but can't replace correct information. Aguī's value lies in delivering epidemic prevention concepts to the place where children are willing to stop and watch.

## Not Every Internet Star Can Be Preserved

Looking back at Aguī today, the easiest misconception is "it was popular, so it would naturally stay." But the data provides another ending: the official website has stopped updating, new news occasionally appears on the Facebook page, and many people recognize it by voice and a few clips. [^1]

This isn't unique to Aguī. The disappearance of Flash technology has caused an entire generation of online works to face problems of format, server, copyright, and preservation. When the original website is no longer maintained, search results, re-posted videos, and audience memories become the main entry points. The entry point still exists, but it doesn't mean the complete context of the work is still there.

Therefore, Aguī shouldn't be packaged into a flawless "Taiwan animation classic." It did leave behind intergenerational memories, and it did let the audience participate in the fate of the characters. At the same time, it reminds us that the popularity of internet culture often outpaces the preservation system. Today you might remember a line of dialogue, but tomorrow it might only be a broken link.

## What Aguī Leaves Behind Is an Entry Point

There's another layer to the preservation problem: the original version of a work, the later edited version, the TV broadcast version, the game materials, and the audience's saved files may each live in different places. They can all prove that Aguī existed, but they may not be able to piece together the complete production order. Researchers who only look at later nostalgic articles may easily mix the initial work of 1999, the interactive activities of 2001, and the epidemic prevention materials of 2003 into the same time point.

Therefore, reviewing Aguī requires preserving two types of materials at the same time. One is the work itself, including animations, dubbing, games, and film projects. The other is the circulation traces, including newspapers, websites, voting activities, server conditions, and audience responses. The former answers "what did Aguī do," and the latter answers "how did the Taiwanese people bring it into their lives."

## Why People Still Remember That Voice Today

Being remembered by Aguī is related to its appearance, and also to its voice. The round face design makes it easy to identify at a glance, and the unique voice allows it to be recognized even without the picture. This kind of memory is especially suitable for the early internet: files may fail, but friends would use a line of imitation to call the entire animation back. [^1]

The memory of junior high school students about Aguī is often not a single work, but a series of places: computer classes, home desktop computers, TV commercials, website games, record stores, and movie theaters. Aguī passed through these scenes, transforming from "a Flash character" into a shared coordinate for a generation.

This shared memory doesn't need to be romanticized. It may ignore those who didn't go online, and may mistake later re-edited clips for the original version. Nostalgia is an entry point for research, not the end of evidence. To understand Aguī, we still need to put the memory back into dates, media, and sources.

Aguī's story began with a Flash short film in 1999, went through online games, the 2001 music interaction, the full Flash film, the Japanese website, and the 2003 SARS promotion. It didn't complete the entire version of the "Taiwan Disney" dream, but it did something harder to plan: let a group of people jointly own a character.

That character once appeared in computer classes, once made people press keys to pop balloons, and once asked children to wash their hands and wear masks. Its life isn't just in the files, but in the moment when the audience says to each other "do you remember?" and briefly recovers.

So, the real question Aguī leaves behind isn't "is it still popular," but whether we are willing to preserve the works of the next internet generation. When a round-faced character peeks out from the screen, it might just be telling a joke. Or it might be leaving evidence for future people about how the Taiwanese once laughed together.
