> **30-Second Overview:** Amis age sets are not merely lines of peers. Different tribes train groups together, share public labor, and even name the entire cohort after contemporary events; in the Malan region, group names have been inspired by "the ROC" and "computers." This system simultaneously handles education, politics, and memory, leaving one question for today: when a tribe moves to the city, who names the new generation?

_Further Reading: [Amis Age Sets of Lirou Village in Dongchang, East Coast Ritual](https://aborgpedia.alcd.center/?option=com_fileman&view=file&routed=1&name=%E9%98%BF%E7%BE%8E%E6%97%8F-1.jpg&folder=%E6%97%8F%E7%BE%A4%E9%A1%9E%E5%B1%AC%2F%E9%98%BF%E7%BE%8E%E6%97%8F&container=fileman-files)_

Some history is written on steles; some history resides within the nomenclature of a group of people. The Amis age set belongs to the latter: it travels with a group through training, festivals, and public work, potentially embedding the sense of an era—such as the completion of a bridge, the appearance of computers, or dynastic change—into the tribe's daily vocabulary.[^1]

The Amis refer to themselves as **Pangcah**, primarily distributed across Hualien, Taitung, and the Penghu islands. Their socio-culture varies due to region, migration, and tribal history. To treat the Amis as a monolithic entity misses the most interesting aspect of this system: the same framework gives rise to different names and roles in different tribes.[^2]

📝 Curator's Note: What is most noteworthy about the age sets is not that they appear ancient, but their capacity to incorporate new things into their memory system.

## How One Name Positions a Generation

The core of the Amis system is the **age set**. In some tribes, men of similar ages enter the same group for training and subsequently take on responsibilities in tribal affairs sequentially. These individuals share a collective identity; thus, the age set is not just a birth year on a household register but a name that influences social relationships for life.[^3]

It is necessary to distinguish two easily confused terms here. An age set is a group formed by the same cohort. An age grade (or _age class_) refers to broader social positions such as youth, prime adult, and elder. The system of the Tulan tribe describes the age set as a unit for mutual assistance and shared labor, while also separating responsibilities across different stages: youth executes public affairs and learns skills, while older members are responsible for decision-making, resource allocation, and order.[^4]

Boys typically enter the community hall during adolescence to learn labor, civic responsibility, and tribal norms. Official ethnic data describes the age set as a system with military, administrative, and political functions. This means the end of training is not just "learning a set of dances," but learning how to work with others in public life.[^1]

In the Malan-type age set system, after the apprentice level, **pakarongay**, members acquire a shared group name. Examples compiled from research include names derived from major bridges, Banmo projects, and local experiences. If a group name is continuously used, an event ceases to be just news from those few years but becomes a lifelong designation for that group of people.[^3]

This naming method also carries risks. The name must be borne by both tribal elders and members; one cannot treat any aesthetically pleasing word as "Amis tradition." What the sources record is the system of specific regions and tribes, not how all Amis tribes operate in the same way.[^3]

## Nanshi Type vs. Malan Type: Two Temporal Sensibilities within One System

The Amis age set system is often categorized into two regional types. The Nanshi type around Nanshi in Hualien is characterized by name succession, where group names are reused. The Malan type from the East Coast to Malan in Taitung tends toward creation, with group names derived from actions, places, or historical events experienced by the members.[^3]

The temporal sense of the Nanshi type is like a circle. Data from Lirou village in Jiantang, Hualien shows that the tribe holds an coming-of-age ceremony approximately every eight years, and group names are cycled through different generations. Even if older members are alive, the new cohort may share the same group name.[^3]

The temporal sense of the Malan type is more like a street that continuously adds characters. Research on Amis in Taitung records that traditional society was divided into three age strata: youth, prime adult, and elder, with several age sets within each stratum. A single set is usually formed by people aged 3 to 5 years.[^5]

These two forms cannot be simplified as "one more traditional, one more modern." Name succession preserves continuity across generations; name creation preserves a generation's judgment of its own era. The former allows the name to return; the latter allows new events to enter.

📝 Curator's Note: A single system can choose to repeat an old name or create a new name for a new era. Tradition, therefore, is not just preservation but also judgment.

## How Age Sets Operate a Tribe

In an ideal systemic image, different age sets have distinct functions. The father of youth, **mama no kapah**, is central to the youth grade, responsible for transmitting decisions between the elder and youth grades. Younger groups undergo training, perform labor, and gradually become familiar with tribal rules.[^3]

This vertical relationship also extends to festivals. The Amis Harvest Festival is typically held from July to September according to the tribe's schedule and harvest season, traditionally related to millet harvesting, ancestral spirits, and social reunion. As agricultural modes change, the timing and content of the festival also adjust. The age organization, songs, dances, and public division of labor mean that it is not merely a show for outsiders but an annual occasion for the tribe to reconfirm its relationships.[^6]

Therefore, the songs and dances of the Harvest Festival are only the most visible surface. What the festival truly requires is a system of division: who convenes, who prepares, who handles labor, who preserves lyrics and ritual knowledge. The Amis Encyclopedia also notes that the number of pieces, melodies, dance steps, and sequence of festivals vary among tribes; there is no fixed version of the festival applicable to all places.[^7]

The system still involves issues of gender and power. Research on indigenous Taiwanese reports found that media often emphasizes collectivism and tribal subjectivity but may also cause the labor of women outside the festival to disappear. Researchers therefore question how the public performance of men in the festival is built upon the off-stage work of women.[^8]

This reminder is important because "tradition" should not be used to obscure internal differences within the system. Different tribes have developed female age sets, and women may participate in singing and ritual affairs through their own organizations. The article's role is to present these differences to the reader, rather than drawing a single conclusion for all tribes.[^7]

## When Tribes Enter Cities, Must the System Move Too?

After the 1960s, Amis people migrated to cities like Taipei, New Taipei, Taichung, and Kaohsiung for employment and social change, forming new communities outside their ancestral lands. Official data treats these urban settlements as part of the contemporary distribution of the Amis. Consequently, the age set has encountered new spatial problems: community halls, land, and fixed tribal boundaries cannot always be replicated in cities.[^1]

Reports from Xincun Village in Hualien provide a concrete case. After the 921 earthquake, Amis people and other indigenous peoples established temporary settlements near Wufeng and Dali in Taichung. Years later, residents reorganized the age sets through youth groups, hoping to continue public participation, cultural education, and tribal life. The reports also documented concerns about language loss, unstable housing, and the performance-ification of tradition.[^9]

Urban training in New Taipei adopted another approach: dividing Amis men aged 15 to 70 into nine age sets, using local names like Tamsan Mountain, Oriole, and Kinguin to name them. This is a specific case of cooperation between local government and the tribe; it cannot be generalized as a common pattern for all urban tribes, but it shows that age sets can still find names in new urban landscapes.[^10]

📝 Curator's Note: The hardest part of urban transmission is not moving ancestral activities to an empty lot, but enabling the system to continue addressing real public issues, rather than just leaving behind costumes, songs, and photo poses.

The first change brought by urbanization was a change in the space of the system. Ancestral tribes had community halls, fixed household relationships, and shared working environments where age sets could be repeatedly seen in daily life. Urban residents may live in different administrative districts with inconsistent work schedules; the relationships maintained through communal living must be reestablished through meetings, courses, and short-term training. The Xincun Village reports prioritize stable housing over cultural transmission because the reality is: without a place to gather and live, youth groups cannot sustain themselves on slogans alone.

The second change was that the power of naming became more complex. The New Taipei case incorporated urban mountain names, birds, and public landscapes into age set names, allowing residents to find symbolic positions in their new residences. However, these activities still require collective decision-making by the tribe; they cannot be chosen solely by administrative agencies based on how "nice" a name sounds. Age set names are not just promotional labels; they are used by members and are tied to responsibilities, training, and mutual address.[^10]

The third change is that the roles of women and youth need clearer articulation. A review of indigenous Taiwanese reports reminds us that when cameras only capture male dance circles, the preparation, caregiving, translation, and labor behind the festival are easily cut out. Data compiled by the Amis Encyclopedia also shows that some tribes have developed female age sets, with women participating in ritual affairs through their own organizations. These changes should not be packaged as a unified progress of the entire tribe, but they prove that the system adjusts according to tribal needs.[^7][^8]

## Transmission is Not About Sealing Names

Age sets are often treated by outsiders as background knowledge of Amis culture—terms one must memorize before a festival. But for the tribe, it is closer to a system that places people within time, responsibility, and relationships: those in the same set are named together, trained together, and take on different tasks at different stages.[^3]

It should not be romanticized as a conflict-free community. Research indicates that while indigenous Taiwanese sometimes reverses mainstream media bias when reporting tribal disputes, it may not fully reveal internal problems. The Xincun Village case directly places land, housing, language, and youth participation at the core of cultural transmission.[^8][^9]

The true insight left by Amis age sets might lie in this uncomfortable place: if the system only copies forms, it becomes a single event. Only if the system allows the new generation to take responsibility within their own city, language, and public issues does it remain a tribal system.

The next time you hear an Amis age set name, think of it as a small archive. It stores the training of a group of people, the judgment of elders, the tasks youth must complete, and the events that were deemed worthy of remembrance by that era. The name does not conclude the era; it merely reserves a position for a generation.

This system also explains why "tribe" cannot be understood as just a point on a map. The grouping of Amis across various locations is related to migration, language, clan, and village organization. Some places use the village as the most important unit of analysis, while others still show clan systems. Researchers repeatedly remind that when discussing the Amis, one must state the tribe's name and location; a universal template cannot replace local history.[^2]

From the perspective of public governance, age sets also provide a language for distributing responsibility. Elders are not just objects of respect; they are responsible for decision-making, teaching, and ritual knowledge. Youth are not just required to obey; they gain competence in labor, training, and meetings. This mutually constraining relationship is the condition under which the system can be maintained. Retaining only the grade titles without retaining mutual responsibility leaves only an exterior shell.[^3][^4]

Similarly, age sets cannot be treated as a ready-made answer for urban governance. The youth in urban tribes may not share the same work and living rhythms, nor will ancestral rituals automatically apply to new lands. Both the Xincun Village and New Taipei cases show that transformation requires negotiation: which norms must remain, which tasks can be completed in new ways, and which names are truly recognized by the tribe. Without tribal participation in these decisions, the system is easily transformed into a one-time cultural event.[^9][^10]

Therefore, the modernity of Amis age sets lies not in their ability to perfectly replicate the past, but in their capacity to bring contemporary issues into collective responsibility. Language education, living space, youth public participation, and the visibility of female labor all require someone to be assigned, someone to be taught, and someone to hand over in the next meeting. That act of handover is what truly continues behind the group name.

## How Much Consent Does One Name Require?

The naming of an age set seems like mere nomenclature, but it actually connects an entire system of social relationships. The Tulan tribe's data refers to members of the same set as Kaput, who work and assist each other, clearly defining the address and care responsibilities between upper and lower sets. This indicates that once a group name is established, it changes how one person interacts with others. It is not an exhibition of individual creativity but the beginning of collective relationships.[^4]

The historical sense in Malan-type creation stems from turning "what this generation encountered" into "how this generation is named." A bridge, a systemic change, a new job, or a local event can all become the background for naming. Later generations who did not experience that era only need to hear the group name to know that their elders once considered something worthy of being remembered as a shared experience. This memory does not arrange everything like a timeline; it selects one name, allowing people to encounter an era again through daily address.[^3]

But naming also requires boundaries. The Amis are widely distributed, and tribal differences are vast; the Nanshi type and Malan type mentioned in research data are classifications for understanding, not universal answers that every tribe must follow. A creation system, female participation method, or festival performance from one tribe cannot be directly transplanted to represent another place. Clarifying local differences is actually closer to the true state of cultural transmission.[^2][^7]

This also requires outsiders to change their questions. Instead of asking "What is Amis tradition?", it is better to first ask, "Which tribe, which age set, and which period of history is this?" The songs and dances of the Harvest Festival, the space of the community hall, and the training of the age sets all have their own context. When context is removed, culture easily reduces to costumes and movements that can be filmed. The research's reminder about media representation demands that viewers put back into the frame the invisible labor, gender relations, and internal discussions.[^8]

This question is particularly crucial for urban tribes. Residents may be born in the city but still learn about themselves, their families, elders, and peers through tribal meetings, mother-tongue classes, festivals, and youth organizations. The Xincun Village reports on youth group reorganization, mother-tongue classes, and cultural education are not replications of ancestral systems, yet they allow urban life to grow its own public culture.[^9]

Therefore, the outcome of transmission cannot be measured only by participation numbers or event days. A more important question is whether the youth know what they are responsible for in the organization, whether the elders are willing to pass on knowledge to the next set, whether women's labor is visible, and whether the tribe can define its boundaries against external observation and administrative arrangements. These questions have no fixed answer, but all require the age sets to provide a space for negotiation.[^8][^10]

For this reason, this article lists specific tribes and research pages in footnotes, deliberately avoiding expanding a single case into a universal law of the entire Amis people. The value of the Amis age set is seen in its differences: the shared form of responsibility—one generation receiving the name left by the previous one, and then deciding how to pass on their era to the next. This process allows culture to become practical daily life from memory, allowing the tribe to maintain its own judgment, voice, and right to choose a mode of transmission amid change, and giving each age set the opportunity to explain what it must protect in its own language, and how to complete it with the next generation, while continuously practicing mutual care and shared responsibility in daily life.
