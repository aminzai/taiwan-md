> **30-Second Overview:** The Pangcah (Amis) age-set system does more than line up peers by age. Different tribes have groups undergo joint training and share public duties, while also naming entire cohorts after contemporary events; in the Malan area, group names inspired by "Republic of China" (the era) and "computers" have appeared. This system thus handles education, politics, and memory simultaneously, leaving a question for today: When tribes move into cities, who names the next generation?

Some history is carved into stone; some history lives in the names people call each other. Pangcah age-set names belong to the latter: they travel with a cohort through training, rituals, and public service, preserving the sense of an era—such as the completion of a bridge, the arrival of computers, or a regime change—in the daily nomenclature of the tribe. [^1]

The Pangcah call themselves **Pangcah** (People). They are primarily distributed in Hualien, Taitung, and the Hengchun Peninsula. Their social culture varies by region, migration, and tribal history. Describing the Pangcah as a uniform whole misses the most interesting aspect of this system: the same structural skeleton grows different names and roles in different tribes. [^2]

📝 **Curator’s Note:** The most memorable feature of the age-set system is not that it looks ancient, but its capacity to absorb new things into its memory system.

## How a Group Name Positions a Generation

The core of the Pangcah system is the **age set**. In some tribes, males of similar age enter the same set together, undergo training, and then assume responsibilities in tribal affairs in sequence. These individuals share a collective identity; the age set is thus not merely a birth year on a household register, but a name that shapes social relationships throughout one’s life. [^3]

It is necessary to distinguish two easily confused terms here. An _age set_ refers to the group formed by the same cohort. An _age grade_ refers to broader social positions such as youth, adults, and elders. The Taitung tribe’s institutional description characterizes age sets as units for joint work and mutual assistance, while separating responsibilities across stages: youth execute public affairs and learn skills, while elders handle decision-making, resource allocation, and order. [^4]

Boys typically enter the assembly house during adolescence, learning labor, public responsibility, and tribal norms. Official ethnic data describes the age-set system as an institution combining military, administrative, and political functions. This means the endpoint of training is not just "learning a dance routine," but learning how to work together in public life. [^1]

In the Malan-type age-set system, after the probationary stage of **pakarongay**, members acquire a shared group name. Examples documented in research include names derived from the Zhonghua Bridge, formwork construction work, and local experiences. If a group name persists, the event ceases to be just news from those few years and becomes an identity that can be called out for a lifetime. [^3]

This naming method also carries risks. Names must be borne jointly by tribal elders and members; one cannot simply treat any word that sounds impressive as "Pangcah tradition." The sources record the institutions of specific regions and tribes, not all Pangcah tribes operate in the same way. [^3]

## Nan Shi-Type and Malan-Type: Two Temporalities of the Same System

The Pangcah age-set system is often categorized into two local types. The **Nan Shi-type** (Southern Hualien area) is characterized by eponyms (reusing names), where group names cycle. The **Malan-type** (coastal Taitung to Malan area) leans toward creating new names, drawing from behaviors, places, or events experienced by the cohort. [^3]

The Nan Shi-type feels like a circle. Data from the Loushe (Palunan) Tribe in Dongchang Village, Ji’an Township, Hualien, shows that the tribe holds coming-of-age ceremonies approximately every eight years, with group names cycling across generations. Even if older members are still alive, a new cohort may share the same group name. [^3]

The Malan-type feels more like a street that keeps adding words. Records from the Taitung City Pangcah show that traditional society was divided into three age layers: youths, adults, and elders, each composed of several age sets. A single set usually consists of people aged 3 to 5 years apart entering together. [^5]

These two forms cannot be simplified into "one is more traditional, the other more modern." Eponyms preserve cross-generational continuity; creating new names preserves a generation’s judgment of its own era. The former brings names back; the latter lets new events in.

📝 **Curator’s Note:** The same system can choose to repeat old names or create a new name for a new era. Tradition thus involves not just preservation, but judgment.

## How the Age-Set System Runs a Tribe

In the ideal institutional image, different age sets have distinct duties. The **mama no kapah** (Father of Youth), positioned at the core of the youth grade, is responsible for transmitting decisions between the elderly and the youth. Younger sets undergo training and execute labor, gradually familiarizing themselves with tribal rules. [^3]

This hierarchy extends to rituals. The Pangcah Harvest Festival (Ilisin) is typically held between July and September according to tribal and harvest schedules, traditionally linked to millet harvest, ancestral spirits, and social reunion. As agricultural patterns change, the timing and content of the festival also adjust. The age organization, song-and-dance, and public division of labor make it not just a program for outsiders to watch, but an annual occasion for the tribe to reaffirm relationships. [^6]

Thus, the song-and-dance at the Harvest Festival is merely the most visible surface. What the festival truly requires is a division of labor: who convenes, who prepares, who handles labor, who preserves lyrics and ritual knowledge. The Taiwan Indigenous Affairs Commission’s Encyclopedia notes that the number of songs, melodies, dance steps, and sequence of proceedings vary by tribe; there is no fixed version applicable to all places. [^7]

The system still faces issues of gender and power. Research on media coverage of Indigenous festivals found that while media often emphasize collectivism and tribal subjectivity, it may also render women’s labor outside the festival invisible. Researchers therefore ask: How is men’s public performance at the festival built upon women’s off-stage labor? [^8]

This reminder is crucial because "tradition" should not be used to mask internal differences. Different tribes have developed female age-sets; women may also participate in leading song-and-dance and ritual affairs. This article aims to leave these differences before the reader, rather than imposing a single conclusion on all tribes. [^7]

## When Tribes Enter Cities, Does the System Move With Them?

Since the 1960s, Pangcah people have migrated to urban centers like Taipei, New Taipei, Taichung, and Kaohsiung for employment and social changes, forming new tribes outside their homelands. Official data considers these urban settlements part of the contemporary Pangcah distribution. The age-set system thus encounters new spatial problems: assembly houses, land, and fixed tribal boundaries may not be reproducible in cities. [^1]

Reports on Huadong New Village provide a concrete case. After the 1999 Jiji Earthquake, Pangcah and other Indigenous peoples established a temporary settlement at the border of Wufeng and Dali in Taichung. Years later, residents reorganized the age-set system through youth groups, hoping to sustain public participation, cultural education, and tribal life. The report also records concerns about language loss, housing instability, and the performative commodification of tradition. [^9]

New Taipei’s urban training adopts a different approach: dividing Pangcah males aged 15 to 70 into nine age sets, using local place names such as Tamanshan (Mt. Tamanshan), Egret (Luzi), and Jinguashi as naming bases. This is a specific case of cooperation between local government and tribe members; it cannot be directly generalized as a common model for all urban tribes, but it shows that age-sets can still find names in new urban landscapes. [^10]

📝 **Curator’s Note:** The hardest part of urban transmission is not moving homeland activities to empty lots, but letting the system continue to handle real public problems, rather than leaving only costumes, dance, and photo poses.

The first change brought by urbanization is the change of space. Homeland tribes have assembly houses, fixed household relations, and shared labor environments, where age sets are repeatedly visible in daily life. Urban Pangcah may live in different administrative districts with inconsistent work schedules; relationships originally sustained by shared life must be rebuilt through meetings, courses, and short-term training. The Huadong New Village report places housing stability before cultural transmission for practical reasons: without a place to gather and live, youth groups struggle to sustain themselves through slogans alone.

The second change is that the power of naming becomes more complex. The New Taipei case places urban mountain names, birds, and public landscapes into age-set names, allowing tribe members to find symbolic positions in new residences. But such activities still require joint decision-making by tribe members; administrative organs cannot simply pick "nice-sounding names" for culture. Age-set names are not just promotional labels; they are used by members and bound to responsibility, training, and mutual address. [^10]

The third change is that the roles of women and youth need clearer articulation. Research on Indigenous TV (PTV) coverage reminds us that when the camera only captures men’s dance circles, the preparation, care, translation, and labor behind the festival are easily cut out. Data compiled by the Taiwan Indigenous Affairs Commission’s Encyclopedia also shows that some tribes have developed female age-sets, allowing women to participate in ritual affairs through their own organizations. These changes need not be packaged as a unified tribal progress, but they prove the system adjusts to tribal needs. [^7][^8]

## Transmission Is Not About Sealing Names in Amber

The age-set system is often viewed by outsiders as background knowledge of Pangcah culture, like terms that must be memorized before the festival. But for the tribe, it is closer to a system that places people into time, responsibility, and relationships: members of the same set are named together, trained together, and assume different work at different stages. [^3]

It should also not be romanticized as a conflict-free community. Research indicates that when PTV reports on tribal disputes, it sometimes prefers to reverse mainstream media biases but may not fully expose internal tribal problems. The Huadong New Village case directly places land, housing, language, and youth participation at the core of cultural transmission. [^8][^9]

The true lesson left by the Pangcah age-set system may lie in this uncomfortable place: If a system only replicates form, it becomes a one-time event. If a system allows the new generation to assume responsibility in their own cities, languages, and public issues, it remains a tribal institution.

The next time you hear a Pangcah age-set name, think of it as a small archive. It stores the training of a group, the judgment of elders, the work youth must complete, and what that era deemed worth remembering. The name does not conclude the era; it merely reserves a position for a generation.

This system also explains why "tribe" cannot be understood merely as a point on a map. The Pangcah’s regional clusters are related to migration, language, clans, and village organization. In some places, villages are the most important unit of analysis; in others, clan systems are still visible. Researchers repeatedly remind us that when discussing the Pangcah, one must specify tribal names and regions; one cannot replace local history with a single tribal template. [^2]

From the perspective of public governance, the age-set system provides a language for distributing responsibility. Elders are not just objects of respect; they are also responsible for decision-making, teaching, and ritual knowledge. Youth are not just required to obey; they must also gain capabilities through labor, training, and meetings. This mutually binding relationship is the condition for the system’s sustainability. Retaining only the grade titles while discarding mutual responsibilities leaves transmission as merely an empty shell. [^3][^4]

Similarly, the age-set system cannot be treated as a ready-made answer for urban governance. Urban tribe youth may not share the same work and housing rhythms; homeland rituals will not automatically apply to new lands. Both the Huadong New Village and New Taipei cases show that transformation requires negotiation: which norms must remain, which work can be done in new ways, and which names are truly recognized by tribe members. Without tribe member participation, the system is easily transformed into a one-off cultural event. [^9][^10]

Thus, the modernity of the Pangcah age-set system lies not in whether it can fully replicate the past, but in whether it can bring contemporary issues into collective responsibility. Indigenous language education, housing space, youth public participation, and the visibility of women’s labor all require someone to be assigned, someone to be taught, and someone to hand over the baton at the next meeting. That act of handover is what truly continues behind the group name.

## How Many People’s Consent Does a Name Require?

Age-set naming seems like merely choosing a name, but it actually mobilizes a whole set of social relations. Taitung tribe data refers to cohort members as **Kaput**, working together and helping each other, clearly defining the address and care responsibilities between upper and lower sets. This means once a group name is established, it changes how a person interacts with others. It is not a display of individual creativity, but the beginning of collective relationships. [^4]

The Malan-type creation of new names has a historical feel precisely because it turns "what this generation encounters" into "how this generation is called." A bridge, a institutional change, a new job, or a local event may become the background for naming. Later generations need not experience that era firsthand; by hearing the group name, they know that elders once considered a certain event a shared experience worth leaving. This memory does not list everything like a chronology; it picks one name, allowing people to re-encounter the era through daily address. [^3]

But naming also requires boundaries. The Pangcah are widely distributed with significant tribal differences; the Nan Shi-type and Malan-type discussed in research data are classification aids for understanding, not standard answers every tribe must obey. A certain tribe’s creation system, female participation methods, or ritual song-and-dance cannot be directly transplanted to represent another place. Clarifying local differences is closer to the true state of cultural transmission. [^2][^7]

This also requires external observers to change their questioning method. Instead of asking "What exactly is Pangcah tradition," one should first ask "Which tribe? Which age set? Which history?" The song-and-dance of the Harvest Festival, the space of the assembly house, and the training of age sets all have their own contexts. When context is removed, culture easily becomes only photographable costumes and movements. Research on media representation demands that observers put invisible labor, gender relations, and internal discussions back into the frame. [^8]

For urban tribes, this question is particularly critical. Tribe members may be born in cities but still relearn their relationships with family, elders, and peers through tribal meetings, mother-tongue courses, festivals, and youth organizations. The Huadong New Village report writes of youth group reorganization, mother-tongue courses, and cultural education; these activities are not replicas of homeland systems, but they allow urban life to grow its own public culture. [^9]

Therefore, the results of transmission cannot be measured only by participation numbers or event duration. More important questions are: Do young people know what they are responsible for in the organization? Are elders willing to pass knowledge to the next set? Is women’s labor seen? Can the tribe define its own boundaries against external observation and administrative arrangements? These questions have no fixed answers, but all require the age-set system to provide a field for negotiation. [^8][^10]

Thus, this article lists specific tribes and research pages in the footnotes, deliberately avoiding expanding a single case into a tribal law. The value of the Pangcah age-set system lies in seeing common forms of responsibility in difference: one generation takes up the names left by the previous one, then decides how to hand over its own era to the next. This process turns culture from memory into practicable daily life, allows the tribe to retain its judgment, voice, and right to choose how to transmit, and gives each new age-set the opportunity to use its own language to explain what it guards, and how to complete it together with the next generation, continuously practicing mutual care, shared responsibility, and guardianship in daily life.
