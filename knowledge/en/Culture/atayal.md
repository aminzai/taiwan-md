In 2001, Yuma Tali established the Miaoli Indigenous Handicraft Development Association in the Xiandong (Elephant Nose) tribe of Miaoli’s Taian Township. In 2003, the association introduced a multi-ethnic employment development program, giving tribal women the opportunity to relearn dyeing and weaving. The starting point of this path was not a beautiful piece of clothing, but an Atayal person discovering that they did not recognize their own tribe’s textiles.[^1]

Long ago, there was a kind of speaking line on the faces of Atayal people: facial tattooing. Men’s tattoos were related to adulthood, hunting, or guarding, while women had to learn weaving first. Facial tattooing was not merely decoration, but a mark of becoming someone capable of bearing responsibility in society. In the early 20th century, the Japanese government banned this custom, and the transmission of facial tattooing was thus severed.[^2]

Facial tattooing stopped on the faces of the previous generation, but weaving did not stop only in museums. This is precisely where Atayal cultural revitalization becomes most intriguing: what is being recovered is not a pattern that can be copied, but an entire set of life rules that places people, clans, land, and ancestors together.

## A River and a Song, Bringing People Back to the Tribe

_Photo: Exhibition view of the Wulai Atayal Museum’s “Weaving. Returning — 20th Anniversary Special Exhibition.” Image provided by the museum’s official special exhibition page, embedded via hotlink, image not downloaded.[^9]_

The Council of Cultural Affairs’ online magazine once wrote that Yuma Tali stood on a bridge leading to the Xiandong tribe, with the Dajia River flowing beneath her feet. She sang songs about walking with ancestors and began to speak about why she returned. In her youth, she had worked as a junior high school teacher and a government official. Later, due to work needs, she began searching for Atayal textile materials, and only then discovered that as an Atayal person herself, she was unfamiliar with her own people’s textiles.[^3]

This “unfamiliarity” was not an individual oversight. The preservation plan of the Graduate Institute of Architecture and Cultural Heritage at National Taipei University of the Arts pointed out that the loss of Atayal dyeing and weaving involves not only the break in techniques, but also the loss of oral traditions, land knowledge, patterns, clothing, and the connections between ethnic culture. The Wulai Atayal Museum’s recent “Weaving. Returning” special exhibition also juxtaposed Atayal language, patterns, daily utensils, and in-depth interviews with tribal members’ life experiences, showing that what the museum preserves is not a single piece of clothing, but a set of still-interacting cultural relationships.[^4] [^9] When a piece of cloth is no longer woven by anyone, what disappears along with it may also be the person who can explain it, the place that remembers it, and the social context that knows when to wear it.[^4]

Yuma Tali chose to return to her tribe, relearning from her grandmother and elders, and placing field research, museum collections, and the hands of weavers on the same table. This action looks like preserving craftsmanship, but is actually closer to a cultural archaeology: not digging up an ancient relic, but asking how a piece of cloth once entered a person’s life.

**Curator’s note:** The hardest part of cultural revitalization is often not making lost objects, but making them needed again in daily life.

## Facial Tattooing Is Not a Totem, It Is a Social Resume

The Council of Indigenous Peoples refers to Atayal facial tattooing as ptasan, explaining that it was once a marker of adulthood, carrying meanings of beauty, warding off evil, and ancestor identification. Women became eligible for facial tattooing only after completing their weaving studies, while men were linked to hunting, combat, or guarding abilities. The patterns on the forehead, chin, and cheeks also differed by gender.[^2]

These norms made facial tattooing like a social resume carved onto the skin. It did not answer “what patterns do you like,” but rather “what have you learned, who can you take responsibility for, and what is your position in the group.” To see facial tattooing merely as an indigenous-style totem would miss its most important part: facial tattooing points to human abilities and relationships.

The thematic collection of the National Digital Library of China also reminds us that facial tattooing once consolidated identity within the Atayal people and left complex cultural traces in interactions between different ethnic groups. However, social change caused this custom to gradually fade away, and later revitalization efforts had to be jointly undertaken by tribal members, scholars, and cultural workers.[^5] Here, “revival” should not be understood as rewinding history backward. A body that has been banned, ridiculed, and forced into silence will not return to its original position because of a single exhibition.

An article in the English edition of _Cultural Survival_ on Atayal facial tattooing and weaving also pointed out that male facial tattooing is related to adulthood and bravery, while female facial tattooing is connected to weaving skills. The article also mentioned that ramie was once the traditional material for weaving, but nowadays wool—more convenient and with more colors—is often used instead, and the use of ramie is decreasing.[^6] This change shows that culture is not a fixed set of materials: when materials change, the negotiation of skills and identity changes with them.

## How a Piece of Cloth Bears a Whole Gaga System

The Atayal term gaga is not just another beautiful word for “tradition.” An article in the Social Economy Portal describes it as a system of divisions and norms covering sacrifice, production, hunting, and combat. In this society, women’s weaving is as important to maintaining the group as men’s hunting and combat.[^1]

Therefore, when Yuma Tali and the weavers reproduced traditional garments, the problem they faced was not just how the warp and weft intersect. They had to compare techniques, patterns, clothing, and cultural relationships from tribal fields, interviews with elders, historical documents, and old objects collected by Japanese museums. The online magazine of the Council of Cultural Affairs recorded that this work took nearly 20 years and resulted in the 2008 publication of _Reconstructing the Atayal: An Illustrated Catalog of Pan-Atayal Traditional Garments_, collecting 217 traditional garments from eight major clans.[^3]

These numbers are important, not because they make revitalization look large in scale, but because every piece of clothing pushes the question of “who remembers” one step forward. If only the garments in the museum remain, Atayal textiles will become a watched past. When tribal weavers disassemble, learn, and reweave, they become a present that can teach, feed families, identify land, and discuss identity.

_Photo: Atayal weaver’s textile display in the Wulai Atayal Museum’s “Between Warp and Weft — A Dialogue with Wulai Atayal Weavers” exhibition. Image provided by the museum’s official special exhibition page, embedded via hotlink, image not downloaded.[^9]_

The preservation plan at National Taipei University of the Arts therefore placed ramie restoration, thread spinning, weaving, garment reproduction, documentation, and field research within the same set of work. This approach does not separate “technique” from “culture”: the growth of materials requires land, the meaning of patterns requires language, the way garments are worn requires memory, and memory must be spoken by someone willing to pass it on to the next generation.[^4]

## From the Museum Back to the Children’s Bodies

_Photo: Official exhibition image of the Wulai Atayal Museum’s “Siyon trakis — Dear Little Rice: The Evolution of Wulai Atayal Seasonal Ceremonies” special exhibition. Image provided by the museum’s official special exhibition page, embedded via hotlink, image not downloaded.[^9]_

Yuma Tali’s work once encountered an awkward threshold: reproduced traditional garments are not ancient relics, and museums may not necessarily collect them. But they are not purely new creations either, and art museums may not necessarily accept them. The Social Economy Portal recorded that she later allowed more than 40 sets of reproduced garments into the collection of the New Taipei City Museum of Art, giving reproduction a position between cultural assets and contemporary creation.[^1]

This position is important. It refuses to lock “authentic tradition” in the past 100 years, and also refuses to downgrade what today’s tribal members create into imitation goods. The weavers are not workers making copies for the past; they are also deciding which techniques to preserve, which patterns can change, and which materials need to be replanted on the land.

An interview published in the online magazine of the Council of Cultural Affairs made this judgment clearer: the patterns of Atayal weavers are not fixed, but develop new creations on the basis of tradition as the weavers’ skills and the times change. After gaining recognition from the tribe, new patterns may also become part of tradition.[^3] This is not diluting tradition into fashionable design, but acknowledging that tradition itself has the ability to grow.

Since 2010, Yuma Tali has led tribal workshops, tribal members, and designers to hold the Atayal Forest Fashion Show in Yushan National Park, bringing garments back to the ancestral land of the central mountain range, and generating new connections between culture, performance, design, and tribal autonomy.[^3] This history reminds us that cultural revitalization does not necessarily have to hide in “purity.” It can also enter public art, education, and industry, as long as the decision-making power remains in the hands of tribal members.[^3]

**Curator’s note:** True revitalization is not preserving tradition more beautifully, but giving the next generation the opportunity to rewrite it as their own life.

## After Facial Tattooing Disappeared, Language Is Still Chasing

What facial tattooing and weaving carry is not just a set of visual symbols. A report by the Public Radio Culture noted that the Indigenous Languages Research Institute has, since 2020, cooperated with 16 indigenous language promotion organizations across Taiwan to hold tribal language camps. The report mentioned Atayal language promotion specialist Siiyimay Doiyum, who, through long-term field research, saw the importance of cultural inheritance to the elderly, and began collecting language materials.[^7]

This work carries a sense of urgency: the history and culture that the elderly can speak will not automatically flow into the ears of the next generation. For language to return to daily life, someone must speak at home, practice in the classroom, and reuse the collected content in the tribe. For the Atayal people, this is the same issue as the revitalization of weaving, but from two sides: one side is how the hands make, the other side is how the mouth speaks.

The case of the Atayal Northern Paiwan (Beishan) gaga oral tradition registered on the National Cultural Assets Network presents another level: knowledge of social operation, dispute resolution, and matchmaking is passed down through oral narration and singing. When gaga is simply translated as “norms,” it is easily reduced to an abstract noun. Returning to oral tradition, we can see that it is something people say out in concrete situations, sing out, and use to deal with relationships—life knowledge.[^8]

Therefore, when we talk about facial tattooing today, we cannot only ask “is anyone still tattooing their face.” We should instead ask: what responsibilities that facial tattooing once required a person to learn are now being shouldered by what? Is it weaving classes, tribal language textbooks, tribal festivals, family memory, or a child returning to the tribe after school and finally understanding what the elders are saying?

## Not All Atayal Tribes Weave the Same Answer

The Atayal people are not a single template that neatly copies culture to every tribe. The ethnic group page of the Council of Indigenous Peoples has already reminded us that weaving materials, colors, patterns, and the positions of facial tattoos all have specific differences. The preservation plan at National Taipei University of the Arts also lists the complex and extensive dyeing system as one of the reasons for investigation and organization.[^2] [^4] Therefore, when discussing “Atayal tradition,” the first thing to avoid is treating the patterns of one tribe as the common answer for everyone.

This is also why the work of weavers cannot be completed with just one illustrated catalog. A catalog can preserve the shape, materials, and patterns of garments, but it cannot tell us when a certain pattern was used in a certain tribe, who passed it to whom, and in what occasion it should appear. Those judgments live in oral traditions, and also in the weavers’ feelings about materials.[^3] [^8]

Contemporary design needs even more caution. Yuma Tali and the workshops put patterns into modern garments, exhibitions, and public art, which does not mean anyone can arbitrarily extract a diamond or line, paste “indigenous style” on it, and complete the cultural translation. The case on the Social Economy Portal is worth paying attention to not only because the work was collected or had a market, but also because it binds research, technology, the labor of tribal women, and decision-making power together.[^1]

This difference also changes the meaning of “preservation.” Preservation is not keeping an answer defined by outsiders for the people, but giving the people enough data, language, and economic conditions to decide what to reproduce, what to change, and what should not be displayed. The life of cultural assets is not in their eternal stillness, but in whether someone can still move them responsibly.

## The Banned Marks Are Not Just Nostalgia

Recent reports in the English edition of _Cultural Survival_ still place Atayal facial tattooing in the context of a gradually disappearing custom, and point out that the language and memory on the tattoos are not just appearance, but cultural records carrying history and spirit.[^6] Nearly a generation later, facial tattooing is still easily treated by outsiders as the first visual sign to identify the Atayal people, but for tribal members, it should not be reduced to a single face under tourist gaze.

The history of facial tattooing being banned allows us to see how state policies cut off a form of bodily transmission. The revitalization of weaving and language allows us to see how people re-arrange their own knowledge after the break. The two cannot replace each other: a piece of cloth does not equal a face, and a tribal language class does not equal a lost ritual. But they can jointly leave the question to today—who has the right to decide what counts as tradition, and who can bring tradition into the daily life of the next generation.

The reason Yuma Tali’s story is important is not because she “found back” a fixed past for the Atayal people. What she does is more troublesome and more alive: connecting the museum’s collection, the grandmother’s memory, the labor of tribal weavers, the education of children, the land of ramie, and the social norms of gaga back together. Culture is therefore no longer just an object to be preserved, but a relationship that tribal members can continue to negotiate.

Facial tattooing may not return to every face. This fact should not be romanticized, nor should it be written as if culture has already died. When weaving has someone to teach, when tribal language has someone to speak, when the tribe has someone returning, and when patterns can still change with the consent of tribal members, the adulthood, responsibility, and identity represented by those lines are still searching for new skin.

This also explains why “cultural products” cannot be judged solely by sales volume. Commodification may give skills income, materials, and apprentices, but it may also flatten complex tribal memories into an easily recognizable pattern. Yuma Tali’s case shows that culture and industry can meet, but in between there must be research, co-creation, and the choice of the tribe, rather than simply using tradition as a market package.[^1] [^3]

For schools, this question takes on another shape. The Social Economy Portal recorded that Yuma Tali has been cooperating with scholars since 2011 to plan ethnic education, and in 2016, the Bozilima National Elementary School was established. The Public Radio Culture reported from the perspective of tribal language camps and language material collection, presenting how language returns to an environment that children can use.[^1] [^7] On one side is the institution, on the other side is daily life; without either, it is difficult for culture to last long.

Education should not only put Atayal culture into festival performances. If students are only told “this is Atayal” when they put on garments and finish singing, they learn is a sorted image. If they can know how ramie is processed, why patterns cannot be arbitrarily moved, and how elders speak a piece of history in the tribal language, they only begin to touch the responsibility behind the culture. The plan at National Taipei University of the Arts, which combines land knowledge, oral traditions, and techniques, just illustrates that this kind of education cannot only remain in the easiest-to-display parts.[^4]

The same is true for museums. Collecting reproduced garments can preserve an important process of revitalization, but the collection should not become the final residence of culture. It should let people know who wove the garment, which tribe it came from, which information is still controversial, and also allow later tribal members to return to use the data and correct the statement. If cultural assets are left only with labels in display cases, they will be separated from life again.

The blank left by facial tattooing is therefore not a gap waiting to be filled by outsiders. What outsiders can do is to make the source clear, keep the differences, and return the decision-making power to the tribal members. What to truly recover, how to teach, and how to display must be continuously negotiated by the tribe and the tribal members. This kind of slowness is not a failure of revitalization, but a refusal to define the Atayal people with external eyes again.

In the end, what is left behind will not be just photos of facial tattoos or a piece of traditional garment. It may be a child finally understanding what the grandfather and grandmother are saying, a weaver knowing where her patterns come from, and a tribe being able to decide which culture can be made public and which can only be passed on in relationships. When these choices still exist, gaga is no longer just a word of the past, but the rules that people are still practicing in their common life today.
