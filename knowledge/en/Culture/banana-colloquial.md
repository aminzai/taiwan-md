> **30-second overview:** Around 1930, linguist Asai Erin left comparative recordings of Taiwanese and Banana Vernacular in Taiwan; in 2014, local interviews in Pingtung's Sizhongxi encountered a group of elders in their eighties who could still speak this language. From existing materials, Banana Vernacular is not a fixed, single dialect name, but a linguistic practice where different places in southern Taiwan transformed familiar Taiwanese or Taivoan into secret communication. Its origins may relate to colonial-era silencing and anti-Japanese memories, but what truly remains are the distinct pronunciations, stories, and silences of each tribe.[^1]

Around 1930, linguist Asai Erin left comparative recordings of Taiwanese and Banana Vernacular in Taiwan. In 2014, local interviews in Sizhongxi again encountered a group of elders in their eighties who could still speak Banana Vernacular. 2018 reporting then placed Baihe's Liuchongxi, Jiaxian's Aliguan, and Xiaolin Village into this thinning linguistic trail.[^1] [^2] [^3]

It sounds like a deliberately mangled Taiwanese sentence, but behind it is no joke. Banana Vernacular and Qiong-jiao Vernacular — these two names point to a secret language or argot of Taiwan's southern mountains: the speakers know each other well, but outsiders find it hard to immediately understand. The "vernacular" (baihua) here is not the vernacular literature opposed to classical Chinese in literary history, but a living speech that resides in the mouth, recognizable only through shared memory.

## It Is Not a Fixed Dialect, But a Shared Name for Several Places

First, let's separate three concepts easily confused. Taivoan is the historical language of the Taivoan people. Banana Vernacular is the linguistic phenomenon of processing Taiwanese or Taivoan into a secret communication method. "Banana" does not refer to an article about fruit, but the name this system of speech has carried through long transmission. The National Museum of Taiwan History names it "Qiong-jiao Vernacular (Taiwanese Sound Variation)" and says it may be based on Taiwanese, mixed with Plains Indigenous languages.[^1]

| Easily Misunderstood Claims                                            | More Stable Understanding                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| ---------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Banana Vernacular is a fixed dialect                                   | Different tribes may have their own systems; records from Xiaolin, Aliguan, Paoziliao, and Sizhongxi cannot be directly treated as the same set of rules.[^2] [^3]                                                                                                      |
| Banana Vernacular is Taivoan                                           | It may use Taivoan for "encryption," but the two are not the same thing.[^2] [^4]                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| It has been proven to be a code created by anti-Japanese organizations | "Anti-Japanese secret language" is an inference proposed by researchers and local interviews; the 1915 Jiasian Incident itself has official historical records, but the direct causality between the incident and Banana Vernacular has not been fully proven.[^3] [^6] |

This distinction matters. If Banana Vernacular is written as an already-finalized language, the most precious part gets erased: it grew different sounds in different places, and many details exist only in elders' memories. The 1996 study collected by Academia Sinica Digital Archives has a title that includes "It Was Once Mistaken for the Siraya Plains Indigenous Mother Tongue." This bibliography itself reminds us that classification is not a one-time answer, but a question that changes with fieldwork materials.[^5]

## How a Sentence Becomes Understandable Only to Insiders

The key is not necessarily replacing entire vocabularies, but modifying sounds. The Key Commentary Network's field report records that when speaking Banana Vernacular, users insert specific consonants and vowels into their original mother tongue, making it harder for outsiders to parse sentence boundaries. The report also preserves a Taivoan example converted into Banana Vernacular: "Masahansanrusu."[^2]

This method has a very practical advantage: no need to invent a separate dictionary, no need to first announce "we are now using a code." Those who know can hear which syllables were added; those who don't only feel each word sounds familiar, yet strung together they cannot catch the meaning. In Sizhongxi community interviews, elders recalled it was used to discuss outsiders' gossip, allowing companions to retain a small space not understood by bystanders even in their presence.[^3]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** This is not language deliberately making itself difficult, but a community making "who can understand" part of the language.

## From Yujing to Nanzixian Creek, Language Walks with People

Banana Vernacular should not be treated merely as a "strange accent" because it relates to how people move. Indigenous Sight reporting notes the Taivoan homeland relates to the Tainan Yujing Basin area, with at least some moving to the Kaohsiung Nanzixian Creek and Laonong Creek basins before the 18th century. In the 19th century, some clansmen moved along the Xinwulu Creek toward the Huadong Valley (East Rift Valley).[^4]

The English language preservation organization Wikitongues' compilation on Taivoan also describes migration routes from southwestern Tainan to Kaohsiung and the Huadong Valley, and notes Taivoan was once classified as a neighboring Siraya dialect, with recent research and revitalization work gradually highlighting its distinctiveness.[^7] These materials discuss the Taivoan language, not Banana Vernacular itself. But they let us see the same background: language does not obediently stay at one point on the map; it moves along with marriage, flight, work, kinship exchange, and tribal migration.

2018 reporting records that a few elders in Baihe's Liuchongxi, Jiaxian's Aliguan, and Xiaolin still speak Banana Vernacular; among them, the Taivoan tribes' Banana Vernacular is described as forming its own system, different from versions researchers previously collected in Neimen.[^2] This "difference" is not a flaw, but evidence it was once used by people: each place took a common need and shaped it into their own sound.

## After 1915, "Anti-Japanese Secret Language" Became the Handiest Answer

In 1915, an armed resistance in the name of faith broke out in the Jiasian area; the National Museum of Taiwan History's special topic notes that after the resistance failed, participants faced severe punishment and judicial trials.[^6] In such an era, local elders understanding Banana Vernacular as a secret language preventing Japanese or outsiders from understanding is not hard to comprehend.

But between "sounds reasonable" and "has been proven" lies a distance that cannot be skipped. PeoPo citizen journalism cites Yang Sen-fu's 1996 research inference, placing Banana Vernacular's possible initiation around 1910 and linking it to anti-Japanese secret communication during the Jiasian Incident period; the same report also proposes that Sizhongxi's version may have been brought by fleeing anti-Japanese fighters, transmitted through goods exchange, or formed locally due to police wiretapping.[^3]

None of these three possibilities can be confirmed by legend alone. Moreover, the National Museum of Taiwan History's circa-1930 recordings prove people were already using and recording Qiong-jiao Vernacular then, yet cannot alone answer in which year, by whom, or because of which event it was created.[^1] What we can confirm: in the era where colonial rule, tribal migration, and local exchange intersected, it was used to protect conversation; what we cannot confirm: from whose mouth the first sentence of Banana Vernacular began.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** What truly remains is not just the answer to "what is this code," but the historical predicament of "we once needed a code."

## Same Name, Three Kinds of Local Memory

The language puzzle turns another corner here. Xiaolin villagers told interviewers Banana Vernacular can encrypt Hokkien or Taivoan; Aliguan elders, however, once judged the Neimen version was not what they recognized as "true" Banana Vernacular. Sizhongxi elders described it as whispered talk passed down by elders, learned for fun.[^2] [^3]

These accounts are not fully consistent with each other, precisely illustrating it is not a language standard issued by a central institution. It is more like a map composed of many small paths: some entered from anti-Japanese memory, some from women's talk while washing clothes, some just learned a few syllables following elders in childhood. Local memory even connects it to ancestral spirits, tribal women, or field life; these accounts differ from each other, yet jointly point to one thing — Banana Vernacular was never a language game detached from life.[^2]

If only the four words "anti-Japanese code" remain, readers miss the following decades. It was also jokes between coworkers at work, tribal children's games, signals during kinship visits. Language did not disappear immediately after trauma; instead, relying on those seemingly unimportant moments in daily life, it continued to be passed to the next person.

## When the Recorder Appeared, the Language Was Already Receding

The Taiwan History Museum's archives say Qiong-jiao Vernacular was once widely known in the countryside, now nearly vanished. The museum preserves "Asai Erin's Taiwan Recordings" spanning 1930 to 1938, and says Asai Erin recorded comparative sounds of Taiwanese and Qiong-jiao Vernacular around 1930, with related digital materials provided by Tokyo University of Foreign Studies.[^1]

This history has a painful contrast: we can now hear it again thanks to recordings left by an outside linguist; but the people who can explain these syllables, say when they were used, who taught whom, are decreasing. The Key Commentary Network's 2018 report notes that with elders passing and insufficient documents, verifying Banana Vernacular's transmission and diffusion grows increasingly difficult.[^2]

Taivoan language revitalization work provides another reference. Wikitongues interviewed language revitalizers, recording them collecting vocabulary from different communities, comparing colonial-era language materials, and attempting to compile dictionaries; this is Taivoan preservation action, not a direct answer for Banana Vernacular, but it illustrates that the most basic work of language revitalization is not romantic: one must preserve sounds, speakers, place names, usage contexts, and version differences together.[^7]

## To Preserve Banana Vernacular, Don't Rush to Find It a Single Ancestor

What Banana Vernacular needs most may not be a standard answer unifying all versions, but a record that does not delete differences. How does each place insert sounds? Who taught whom? Was it used in fields, worker sheds, while washing clothes, or in children's games? Is the same sentence truly identical in Xiaolin, Aliguan, Paoziliao, and Sizhongxi? These questions matter more than picking a pretty origin story for it.

Researchers, museums, and tribes can each do different things: museums preserve sounds, researchers compare syllables and contexts, tribes preserve speakers' memories, later people write sources clearly, not dressing inferences as conclusions. This division of labor will not make Banana Vernacular immediately "revive," but lets the next generation know what they receive is not a specimen organized without a single wrinkle, but a segment of language that once lived in different places.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** For a language on the verge of disappearance, the greatest danger is not that we don't know everything yet, but that to appear knowledgeable, we first fill in a single version for it.

The question Banana Vernacular leaves for Taiwan is not just "can you speak it." It is more like asking: when a group must modify familiar speech until outsiders cannot understand, just to protect their own conversation, how do we today hear that history?

Around 1930, Asai Erin recorded two sounds together; in 2014, Sizhongxi elders still received a few syllables from their elders; in 2018, memories from Xiaolin, Aliguan, and Liuchongxi pulled this thread back to the tribes. What remains in the end may not be an answer a dictionary can lock down, but evidence that on this same island, people once used different sounds to recognize each other.
