In the 1950s, a pair of lightweight slippers designed by a military rubber factory began moving from factories into civilian use. On August 26, 1960, Central News Agency (CNA) photojournalists documented the processes of cutting, sewing, gluing, and last-making at the Lianqin Rubber Factory in Tainan. At that time, the shoes were not a nostalgic symbol but an everyday item within the military supply industry.[^1]

Today, blue and white slippers look so ordinary—so ordinary they are often found by convenience store entrances, dormitory hallways, or household shoe racks, making them hardly worthy of special introduction. But their counter-intuitive nature lies here: they were not designed first as "Taiwanese flavor" before being manufactured; rather, they responded to the post-war society's need for cheap, durable footwear, and only later were they worn by people as cultural memory.

Official online curation materials also situate blue and white slippers within the living conditions of the 1950s: at that time, many people still walked barefoot or wore straw shoes, making foot injuries a daily risk following increased public road use and transportation activities. This narrative aligns with the military factory records preserved by CNA, but some details are part of the curator's historical explanation; this article treats it as a sourced cultural narrative, not an expansion into statistical conclusions covering all citizens.[^9]

## Military Technology First Served Daily Life in Tainan Factories

Records from the Ministry of Culture’s "Golden Decade of Taiwan Migration" indicate that the first generation of blue and white slippers is related to the Lianqin Rubber Factory in Tainan. In the 1950s, American military advisors noted that many local people habitually walked barefoot, leading the Ministry of National Defense to instruct the Lianqin Rubber Factory—which produced military boots—to develop lightweight slippers for civilians. Early models only had soles and laces; the blue and white color scheme was taken from the colors of the Republic of China's national emblem.[^1]

This origin cannot be simplified as "military invention, civilian copying." The predecessor of Lianqin Rubber Factory was established in Guangzhou in 1947, moved to Anping, Tainan, in 1949, and became an independent entity in 1950. It produced military supplies such as leather shoes, rubber shoes, life jackets, raincoats, and balls, in addition to army boots.[^1] In 1976, it was renamed Lianqin Factory No. 304; it was dissolved in 2007 and transferred to the Tainan City Government in 2008.[^1]

This timeline adds weight to the blue and white slipper: it was not born in some private trendy shop, but emerged from a factory that simultaneously handled shoe bodies, rubber, vulcanization, and military supplies, producing a civilian version. Its "grassroots" nature does not mean a lack of technology; rather, it means the technology was lowered in barrier to enter daily life without requiring uniforms or class credentials.

📝 Curator's Note: What is most worth looking at about blue and white slippers is not how beautiful they are, but that they once translated the capabilities of a military factory into shoes that ordinary people could afford.

## More Than Just a Factory: The Prehistory of Taiwan's Rubber Industry

The story of blue and white slippers cannot begin solely with the Lianqin Rubber Factory in the 1950s. Cultural Ministry records point out that primary products such as tire patches, water pipes, and rice milling rollers appeared in Taiwan's rubber industry during the Japanese colonial period. In 1941, related enterprises merged to form "Taiwan Protective Film Company." After the war, this existing equipment and technical workforce were reallocated; some personnel left their original organizations to start businesses independently, becoming an important source of later private-sector rubber industries.[^1]

This history means that "grassroots resilience" is not just a praise for individual life but also points to how industrial organization persisted through regime changes, post-war reconstruction, and market fluctuations. Blue and white slippers appear to be simple shoes, but they are connected to an entire production chain involving rubber compounding, extrusion, cutting, sewing, vulcanization, and finished product finishing.

## Rubber Sheets, Vulcanizers, and Mechanization: Turning a Shoe into a Commodity

CNA's factory images preserve scenes of labor in the early rubber industry: workers cut rubber sheets, sew shoe bodies, apply vulcanizing agents, glue them, fix the shape with lasts, and then hang the finished products to dry. Vulcanization is an important process for rubber products, as it changes the physical properties of the material. These steps demonstrate that "durable" is not just an adjective but something created by materials, machinery, and the hands of workers.[^1]

StoryStudio's historical compilation points out that early military rubber factory shoe models were later improved and mass-produced by civilians using cheaper raw materials and mechanized production methods. It also places blue and white slippers at the intersection of post-war material scarcity, the rubber industry, and the petrochemical industry development.[^2] However, existing public data is insufficient to support claims that "all blue and white slippers uniformly switched to PVC in a certain year" or "all styles use the same material," so this article does not write the evolution of materials as a single linear path.

The Taiwan Watch Association recorded markings on a commercially sold Taiwanese-made pair of shoes, where the sole was marked EVA and the shoelaces were marked PVC. This case cannot infer the material of all blue and white slippers but serves to remind readers that even if they look similar, the actual formulas might differ, and consumers should rely on product labeling.[^4]

The Taiwan rubber industry was not always advancing upward. Cultural Ministry records mention that Taiwan's rubber industry began exporting in 1959, with an increase in small household processing factories earning foreign exchange. After 1985, exports declined due to factors such as the cancellation of raw material tax rebates, the appreciation of the New Taiwan Dollar, the oil crisis, labor shortages, and rising environmental awareness; after 1988, some rubber shoe industries moved overseas to mainland China or Southeast Asia.[^1]

Therefore, the popularity of blue and white slippers cannot be explained merely as "Taiwanese people liking cheap, durable shoes." It is also connected to a period's factory network, household processing, export markets, and petrochemical material supply. When industrial conditions changed, products might remain in cultural memory, but the original production landscape that supported them may not have been fully preserved.

This industrial history adds complexity to the three characters "Made in Taiwan." It points both to a manufacturing capability of a certain period and reminds us that the place of manufacture, working conditions, and market position all change. We cannot directly extrapolate the product markings seen today into an unbroken local production history.

This creates an uncomfortable yet more historically accurate contrast between the durable image of blue and white slippers and industrial reality: a pair of shoes can remain, but the industry that made it may have left its original location.

## From Factory Floor to Shoe Rack: How Blue and White Slippers Became Shared Memory

The key to the popularity of blue and white slippers is not just their recognizable color scheme, nor just their low price. CNA and StoryStudio's data collectively present a path: "Military Technology $\rightarrow$ Civilian Improvement $\rightarrow$ Mechanized Mass Production $\rightarrow$ Daily Use."[^1] [^2] The so-called national shoes were not successfully announced by one brand on a single day, but slowly became recognizable objects through repeated use in homes, factories, schools, and retail settings.

An article from the National Immigration Agency describes blue and white slippers as a shared memory of the Taiwanese people, compiling the narrative from the 1950s prototype, private mechanized production, to daily popularization. This is a cultural promotion article and cannot independently prove that "everyone wore them" or any world record. However, it can serve as material for public discourse, showing how official narratives place blue and white slippers within the framework of grassroots Taiwanese culture.[^3]

Cultural memory is not static. Shoes originally produced with military processes in factories were used in different spaces after entering civilian life: bathrooms, dormitories, front porches, shops, and workplaces. These specific scenes may not all be summarized by statistical data, but they can be cross-referenced through official cultural articles, factory images, and design reports. Blue and white slippers became symbolic precisely because they had both the starting point of national industry and the continuation of everyday life.[^1] [^3] [^5]

## The Political Symbolism of Blue and White vs. The Lived Experience of Wearing Them

The origin of the blue and white color scheme is linked in official cultural materials and multiple historical compilations to the blue and white of the Republic of China's national emblem.[^1] [^2] But for most people who wore them, blue and white slippers were first not a flag; they were shoes worn after bathing, shoes worn when going out in the rain, or shoes slipped on casually when family members asked you to go buy something at the alley entrance.

An article from the Ministry of the Interior's National Immigration Agency describes blue and white slippers as part of grassroots Taiwanese culture and shared memory, compiling the narrative from the 1950s prototype, private mechanized production, to daily popularization.[^3] These types of official cultural articles also include folk claims such as "anti-slip," "memory insoles," and "eco-friendly materials." However, these claims do not provide sufficient technical or testing evidence in the text to be directly considered world records or material science conclusions.[^3]

StoryStudio also points out that online circulation of "five world records" includes claims about family shoe models, memory insoles, eco-friendly marketing, and anti-slip technology, but the article places them within a narrative context of "don't rush to believe," without providing independent certification sufficient to prove "world firsts."[^2] Official online curation pages also feature similar narratives like "memory insoles," "automatic decomposition," and "global innovation," but the page notes that the exhibition content is curated, and not every claim is accompanied by material testing or third-party certification.[^9] Therefore, rather than hyping up blue and white slippers as a series of unverified champions, it is more honest to say: their cultural power comes from being worn, seen, and remembered over time, not from a non-existent world ranking table.

## From Household Item to Public Vocabulary

Beyond being reinterpreted by designers, blue and white slippers have also been borrowed as public vocabulary in different eras. Official online curation materials record that during political negotiations before the 2023 Taiwanese presidential election, the term "blue and white alliance" was used, and some citizens jokingly referred to it as "blue and white slippers." This is not proof of blue and white slippers' original cultural meaning but an example of how a name for an object extends into political rhetoric.[^9]

This semantic shift should be viewed separately from design commodities: designers transform blue and white slippers into prints, coasters, or other goods, placing the household item into aesthetics and the market. Political vocabulary transforms its "slipper" aspect into an evaluation of negotiation speed and political process. Both show that once a common object is widely recognized, it can detach from its original use and enter new narrative fields.

## After Being Considered Tacky, Blue and White Slippers Entered Designers' Patterns

Blue and white slippers were once associated with the term "Tâi-gí" (Taiwanese), which carries judgments of class and taste. This appellation both placed them in the context of everyday life and potentially reduced the wearer to someone who was "not refined enough." The article lacks sufficient sources to prove that all Taiwanese people understood it in the same way, so a more accurate statement is: blue and white slippers may have been seen as practical items or used as symbols of Tâi-gí flavor or tackiness across different generations, contexts, and narratives.

A report from National Taipei University recorded the cases of designers Yang Tzu-hsing (楊子興) and Jin Yuan-xing (錦源興): he transformed daily elements such as carp roe, blue and white slippers, and Black-faced Spoonbill into prints and commodities, incorporating "culture is the accumulation of life" into his creative thinking.[^5] This was not about turning blue and white slippers into luxury goods but looking at an overlooked object from a different angle—life itself can be design material.

A campus report from National Kaohsiung University of Science and Technology mentioned that blue and white slippers were transformed into cultural and creative products like coasters and used as subjects in cultural and creative training related to Taiwanese subculture and common life.[^6] A relevant report from National Tainan University also recorded Jin Yuan-xing transforming blue and white slippers, carp roe, Guanmiao dried noodles, and flies into fabric prints.[^7]

📝 Curator's Note: When blue and white slippers are printed on fabric, they are no longer just "things worn on the feet." They begin to remind us in reverse that the everyday things once considered ordinary have preserved a local aesthetic of the island.

## A Pair of Shoes Cannot Represent All Taiwanese People, But It Can Show Taiwan's Complexity

Equating blue and white slippers directly with "Taiwanese national identity" risks compressing diverse experiences into a single symbol. Their color scheme certainly carries the symbolic origins of post-war state machinery, but after circulating in the public sphere, they were reinserted into homes, factories, schools, markets, and design commodities. Different people wearing them might recall the military, rental housing, front porches, construction sites, night markets, or just a temporary shoe purchase experience.

Therefore, the meaning of blue and white slippers is not about answering for Taiwanese people "who we are," but about putting the question back into daily life: which objects were retained? Who had the ability to make them affordably? Which industries once supported families, and which have left after globalization? When a pair of plastic slippers is re-printed as a fabric pattern, are we consuming Tâi-gí flavor, or are we relearning to see common labor?

## Walking Out the Front Door, But Leaving Questions in the Sole

The blue and white slippers of the 1950s started from the military technology of the Lianqin Rubber Factory. Later, they underwent private improvement and mechanized production to become familiar everyday objects for many people. This path can be written as "the story of Taiwanese grassroots resilience," but it cannot obscure the issues of materials, labor, industrial relocation, and environmental costs. The Ministry of Culture preserved the factory processes; Taiwan Watch reminded us of plastic materials and consumption consequences; design education reports allowed us to see how everyday objects re-entered cultural creation.[^1] [^4] [^5] [^6] [^7]

The pair of shoes at the beginning of this article still rests by a duplex door. It did not proclaim a singular Taiwan for anyone, nor was its history washed away by redesign. It simply makes us look closely at the upper part, sole, and markings before wearing them: how a pair of shoes was made, who wore it, and whether we are willing to treat an ordinary object as a piece of Taiwanese culture worthy of verification and remembrance.
