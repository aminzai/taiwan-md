> **30-second overview:** The Bunun millet ritual is not a festival that appears after the harvest; it is a living time system that follows the growth cycle of millet, arranging sowing, weeding, harvesting, storage, and singing. Millet cultivation was once interrupted by land governance, crop shifts, and religious changes. Today's revival begins again from seeds, school gardens, and tribal lands, reconnecting crops, families, and memory.

In the experimental forest of Nantou Xinyi Township, there was once a small millet field covered with bird nets. In 2022, 28 local Nantou millet varieties were planted here, brought back from the American seed bank. This was not a simple agricultural experiment, but a conversation between Bunun youth and elders about the beginning of millet. [^1]

The same crop has more than nine different names in Bunun life, corresponding to different growth stages. It is not only food, but also a coordinate of family wealth, social relationships, and ritual order. [^2] Counterintuitively, the interruption of millet culture is not just the loss of a food source; it also removes the anchor point of a way of life marked by crops.

Nowadays, those who return to farming millet are not just looking for an ancient crop. They are asking: If the ritual was originally meant to follow the growth of millet, can the songs on stage bring back that time when millet is no longer planted?

## A Year Does Not Begin with the Calendar

The Bunun millet seasonal ritual cannot be reduced to a "cultural activity" in a fixed month. In the sowing ritual text of Hsinchu Township, the ceremony arranges prayers for blessings, test sowing, driving away evil spirits, prayers for farming tools, drinking, chanting pasibutbut, and finally actually going to the field to sow before dawn, putting people, land, objects, and millet into the same action. [^3]

The timing of the sowing ritual also varies from tribe to geography. Cultural heritage keeper Hu Qinfu recorded that reclamation and test sowing are roughly between November and December, and the sowing ritual is around January to February of the following year. Judging the time also depends on plant growth and dreams, not just the calendar. [^4]

This makes the word "festival" seem too small. What is truly arranged is the following whole segment of work: who enters the field first, who sows, who covers the soil, which animals cannot see, whether the tools can touch each other, and how a family cooperates on the same slope.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** We are accustomed to thinking of rituals as fixed days on the calendar, but the millet seasonal ritual challenges this assumption. It is more like a time system determined by plant growth, dreams, and family division of labor.

Before sowing, the chief priest takes millet seeds, hoes, gourd shells, and bamboo grass to the field before dawn. The gourd shell is used to symbolize the heavy grain of millet, and the bamboo grass symbolizes vitality. During the test sowing, the chief priest inserts the ritual vessels into the land while praying for the millet to grow fast and well. [^3]

Then comes the driving away of evil spirits and prayers for farming tools. The bamboo grass is taken into the field, praying for diseases to leave. Family members pour millet wine dregs onto the roof, and then pray for the hoe. The tools are not accessories to the work; they are placed in the ethics of farming, becoming objects that can be thanked and cared for. [^3]

The sowing itself is also divided into roles. Hu Qinfu's records show that the female elders usually take charge of sowing, and others follow behind to cover the soil and clear obstacles. The soil covering cannot be inconsistent, and the hoes cannot collide with each other. These details may seem trivial, but they turn the field into a place of shared work, rather than each person completing their own production line. [^4]

## As Millet Grows, the Ritual Moves Too

After the millet sprouts, the Bunun life does not enter a waiting period. Hu Qinfu recorded that about seven days later, thinning can be carried out, and after thinning, pasibutbut is chanted. Later, there are the Ear Festival, Harvest Festival, and Storage Festival, and even when the new rice can be eaten, there are corresponding regulations. [^4]

From this order, it can be seen that pasibutbut is not a "indigenous song" separated from farming. It is embedded in the growth cycle of crops, and is linked with sowing, weeding, harvesting, and storage ceremonies. According to the National Cultural Heritage Data, this song is only spread in the Bunun tribes of Jiaxian and Xiluo. The Gufeng Tribe in Hualien once resumed the interrupted singing. [^5]

Hualien County Cultural Heritage Registration further pointed out that the Gufeng Tribe's singing style starts with the lead singer, and the rest of the singers gradually climb up the main tone, pursuing a common resonance. This inheritance has been functioning as a traditional continuation since the 1980s. [^6]

The Ministry of Culture's English report in 2022 described pasibutbut as an eight-part polyphonic song of the Bunun people praying for a good millet harvest, usually sung by about six to twelve adult men in a circle. The report also recorded that it was recorded by Japanese musicologist Kurozawa Toshio in 1952. [^7]

These data all point to an easily overlooked difference: the reason why the harmony is moving is not only because there are many voice parts, but also because it originally had a common goal to complete. The rising of the voice was originally in the same living scene as whether the millet could grow and whether the family could work together.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** When pasibutbut is moved to the stage, the most easily seen is the "eight-part" sound spectacle. Another easily overlooked possibility is that it was originally not an independent music program, but a common action of farming, family, and praying for a good harvest.

## Interruption Is Not Natural

Millet has been cultivated in Taiwan for a long time, but the Bunun millet culture later declined, and it cannot be simply explained by "modernization so the tradition was lost." The National Taiwan University English Spotlight records that the policies of the Japanese colonial period and globalization changed the crop choices. Nantou farmers later turned to vegetables, fruits, and flowers for higher-yielding crops, and millet cultivation almost disappeared. [^1]

The English report of Taiwan Panorama in 2023 pointed out that the group migration in the 1930s changed the original location of the Bunun tribe, and rice replaced millet as a more important crop. The introduction of Christianity also disturbed the seasonal agricultural rituals. This is not that a generation suddenly stopped cherishing the culture, but that the land, crops, and institutions of life were changed together. [^8]

The study of millet cultural ecology takes the Nantou Wangxiang Tribe as an example, and understands millet as a land interaction, farming methods, and traditional ecological wisdom. The abstract of the study pointed out that the Bunun people have many names for different growth stages of millet, and millet is simultaneously a symbol of food, family wealth, and social status. [^2]

Therefore, when the millet field is lost, what is lost is not just a dietary choice. The order of the ritual loses its object, the tribal language names leave the daily work, and the elders lose a common occasion to pass on knowledge to the younger generation.

## Revival Is Not Copying the Past

The revival in Nantou Xinyi Township began with a small flower pot and a school garden. Taiwan Panorama reported that Jiumei Elementary School put millet cultivation and seasonal rituals into the curriculum, letting students understand the images on the wooden calendar that could only be read through life from sowing, caring, harvesting, and storage. [^8]

The key to this approach is not that the school simulates a "traditional scene," but that millet becomes a life that students must take care of. When the crops are eaten by birds and affected by rain, the humility and gratitude expressed in the ritual are no longer abstract nouns in the textbook.

The cooperative project of National Taiwan University in 2022 dealt with another scale of the problem. American scholar Wayne Hazen Fogg collected nearly a hundred local millet varieties in Taiwan in 1977, of which 28 came from Nantou Xinyi. Professor Guo Huarong of National Taiwan University reintroduced the related germplasm back to Taiwan in 2011, and then cooperated with the Xinyi Tribe to restore them. [^1]

These varieties are not putting history in a freezer, but letting the tribe face a concrete question again: which millet is suitable for which land? How to sow? How to prevent birds? Who still remembers its name? When the crops return to the field, knowledge must be re-passed through the body, not just stay in the file.

Taiwan Panorama's report wrote that in 2022, the first year of the "Garden Program" attracted more than 30 people to participate. After the returnees planted millet, the elders began to actively talk about memories and cultivation methods, and the family relationship was also reconnected by taking care of the field together. [^8]

There is a detail worth keeping here: the participants underestimated the bird damage and rainfall, and the harvest was taken away by nature. The revival is not to turn the tradition into a set of guaranteed success procedures. It puts people back into the farming that will fail, try again, and learn from others.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Cultural revival is often measured by the number of activities or participants, but millet pulls the problem back to another scale. The real choice is not to hold another activity, but whether someone is still willing to put the seeds back into the same land next year.

## From Ritual Songs to Shared Life

The activity data of National Taiwan Dongmen University pointed out that pasibutbut has gradually become a song of identity recognition shared by six Bunun communities. The same data also emphasized that the traditional performance of the Zhushan Bunun tribe adds new sounds to the existing singing, presenting contemporary imagination. [^9]

This reminds us that cultural inheritance is not to treat all changes as pollution. As long as the community can still decide when to sing, why to sing, who can teach, and which content cannot be arbitrarily moved, new arrangements, courses, and performances may become tools of inheritance.

But the performance also has its boundaries. The data of the Ministry of Culture puts pasibutbut in the context of Bunun agricultural rituals and millet harvest. If it is only packaged as a "magical high-pitched chorus," it will be separated from sowing, labor, ancestors, land, and family. [^7]

In the same way, the sowing ritual cannot be made into a tourist process. The ritual records of Hsinchu Township have dreams, taboos, tools, food, family division of labor, and actions before dawn. If any of these is removed, what is left is only a stage that looks like a festival.

The importance of the Bunun millet seasonal ritual lies in this contradiction: on the one hand, it must be preserved, on the other hand, it cannot be alive only by preservation. It needs to be planted, cooked, named, sung, and also needs to allow different tribes to re-arrange the inheritance methods according to their own land and history.

## Millet Is Not a Single Past

When talking about the Bunun millet ritual, we also need to be careful about another simplification: to think of "Bunun people" as only one fixed version. The ritual names, months, songs, and tribal practices in the data are not exactly the same. Hu Qinfu explicitly reminded that the reclamation ritual is based on the family, and there is no standardized process, and it will be adjusted according to the land conditions. The National Cultural Heritage Data also pointed out that pasibutbut only exists in the Jiaxian and Xiluo communities, and the Zhushan tribes have their own characteristics. [^4] [^5]

These differences are not data inconsistencies, but the traces of culture actually living. Mountain height, tribal migration, family division of labor, church history, and contemporary school systems will all affect how a ritual is remembered, when it is held, and who can participate. Folding all tribes into a "Bunun tradition" will instead erase the people who are actually carrying out the inheritance.

The same pasibutbut should not be described only by the acoustic spectacle. Hualien Cultural Heritage Data says that the Gufeng Tribe starts with the lead singer, and the rest of the singers follow the main tone to gradually climb. The English report of the Ministry of Culture records that adult men usually sing in a circle of six to twelve people. [^6] [^7] These information tells us the form, but not the meaning. The meaning must return to the occasion where it appears: before and after sowing, harvesting and storage, and a group of people working, eating, and praying together.

Without this layer of context, it is easy for outsiders to regard the Bunun polyphonic songs as performance segments that can be arbitrarily disassembled. If there is only context but no voice of the tribe itself, researchers may describe the living culture as a completed specimen on behalf of the tribe. A safer approach is to put the official registration, academic research, cultural heritage keepers' statements, and contemporary tribal activities side by side, letting different sources be responsible for what they can prove.

### Who Needs to Revive Together

There is another easily overlooked level of revival: it is not only the restoration within the tribe, but also the re-negotiation between different systems. The school provides a stable curriculum time, the research institution assists in preserving germplasm and cultivation data, and the tribal elders provide names, taboos, and actions. The three have different jobs, but none can replace the other two. The experience of Jiumei Elementary School reported by Taiwan Panorama is exactly to put the classroom, the field, and the ritual in the same learning path. [^8]

This cooperation also changes the question of "who is the teacher." The knowledge of millet culture is not only exists in the narration of an expert, but in a number of actions that need to be done together: identifying seeds, preparing land, waiting for germination, avoiding taboos, and moving the harvest into the house. Each action will let people of different ages stand in the same place, making "inheritance" a visible relationship, rather than an abstract cultural slogan.

The name of the millet variety may be identified by the elders, the sowing gesture must be demonstrated in the field, and the students may record the process and bring it back to the family for discussion. Culture is no longer only preserved by a certain formal textbook, but scattered among seeds, bodies, languages, and land. This is also why the revival should not be understood as bringing a fixed process back: every time it is replanted, it will expose new environmental conditions and new learning relationships.

Taiwan Panorama's report also recorded that the youth participating in the Garden Program lost half of the harvest due to bird damage and rainfall. This failure was not deleted, but made "plant again next year" a concrete and honest commitment. [^8] For a culture that has been interrupted, the ability to withstand failure and pass on experience to the next person may be closer to the true continuation than a successful demonstration.

This also explains why "revival" cannot be measured only by quantity. Several hectares of farmland, several activities, and how many tourists are attracted, may leave a record, but it does not necessarily mean that the knowledge has returned to life. The question closer to the cultural core is: do the elders have the opportunity to tell the name of the millet to the younger generation? Do the children really touch the seeds and land? Does the family still assign tasks in a segment of shared work? Are the ritual songs sung at the corresponding farming time, rather than just arranged in the time when the audience is waiting?

On the other hand, the revival should not push all the responsibility back to the tribe. The millet field needs land, time, and stable care, the youth may work in the city, the school has curriculum pressure, and the tribe still has to face bird damage, rainfall, and crop markets. The cooperation between National Taiwan University and the Xinyi Tribe, and the experience of Jiumei Elementary School putting millet into the curriculum, show that cultural inheritance needs to span the collaboration of tribes, schools, research institutions, and public resources. [^1] [^8]

Going back to the millet field in Xinyi Township, the most worth remembering is not the number of 28 varieties, but the work behind the number: finding back the seeds, identifying the names, letting the elders speak, letting the students go to the field, and then bearing the bird damage and rain. These things turn a culture from "knowing" to "doing."

The Bunun millet ritual is therefore not a closed past tense. It was once interrupted by colonial governance, crop shifts, and religious changes, and it is precisely because of the interruption that today's revival cannot be a simple replay. The true continuation is to let millet become a present that a tribe must face together.

When the sower puts the seeds into the soil before dawn, when the family covers the soil together, when the adult men sing pasibutbut in the ritual, the prayer of the song is not only the harvest. It also confirms: this land still has people who remember how to start, and next year there will still be people willing to take over.

Millet cuts the year into many segments that must be completed together: a dream may determine whether to start work, a hoe must be blessed in the ceremony, a line of lyrics must be sung in the wish of harvest, and after storage there are new rules. These segments are not necessarily in the same form in each tribe, but they all point to a relationship: people are not standing outside the land to manage the crops, but to arrange their lives in the growth of the crops.

Therefore, the Bunun millet seasonal ritual really left behind is not a process chart that can be copied. It left behind a way of judging things: first look at the land, then look at dreams and seasons. First take care of the people who work together, then talk about the harvest. First hand the seeds to the next season, then hand the songs to the next generation. When millet returns to the field, time also regains the shape that can be remembered together.

Therefore, understanding this set of rituals cannot only ask "how much is left now." The more important question is: who is making it happen again, and whether this happening is still controlled by the tribe's own language, boundaries, and interpretation. The seeds can be preserved by research institutions, the data can be made public by museums, and the songs can be performed abroad. But only when the tribe puts it back into the land, family, and the next season, these preservation will not stop in the file. The return of millet is not a return to an unchanged past, but an opportunity for the Bunun people to decide how to remember themselves in their own way, and also lets the outside world learn to listen to the boundaries of the tribe before watching, respect the decision of different communities on their own culture, and let this knowledge not only be collected, but also be used and sung by the next generation.
