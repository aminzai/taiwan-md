> **30-second overview:** On July 27, 2024, the Taiwan Pavilion at the Paris Cultural Olympiad in Parc de la Villette distributed 500 qiezhi bags filled with Taiwan snacks to entering visitors; they were all distributed within one hour, with approximately 4,000 attendees that evening.[^1] The story of this red, blue, and green striped tote, however, does not begin in Paris, but in the rural craft of Houbi, Tainan, where rush grass was dried, flattened, and woven into "grass bags." [^2] It was seen anew not merely due to retro appeal; rather, it brought a history of daily life that was not particularly refined but sufficiently durable into today's design language.

_Extended viewing: [Red, Blue, and Green Striped Qiezhi Bags at the Paris Cultural Olympiad](https://ialive.bwnet.com.tw/AC_Gallery/2024/07/ceb3c148-2b78-2acb-9511-afd73e3ecb35.jpg)_

_Image: Provided by the Ministry of Culture, published in "alive Taiwan." Audience members in the photo hold red, blue, and green striped qiezhi bags._

This photo fills in a detail often obscured by the "Taiwan LV" moniker: the qiezhi bag's power lies not in whether it resembles a luxury item when placed alone in a display window, but in the fact that when carried by people, filled with items, and standing among friends, it remains instantly recognizable. The red, blue, and green stripes did not fade into the background on the Paris stage; instead, they brought the everyday sensibility originally belonging to markets and grocery stores into a transnational cultural venue.[^1]

## One Hour in Paris, Packed into an Undisguised Bag

On July 27, 2024, rain fell on Parc de la Villette in Paris. One hour before the opening of the Taiwan Pavilion at the Cultural Olympiad, queues had already formed at the entrance; approximately 4,000 attendees arrived that first evening. The 500 qiezhi bag gift sets prepared by the organizers, containing Taiwan Beer, cream puffs, pearl milk tea ice cream, and other snacks, were distributed within one hour of doors opening.[^1]

_Extended viewing: [Opening Scene of the Taiwan Pavilion at the Paris Cultural Olympiad, Parc de la Villette](https://file.moc.gov.tw/001/Upload/470/relpic/10550/220783/bccb9f43-e69d-4642-b277-a29fbf32a0dc.jpg)_

_Image: Ministry of Culture. Opening scene of the Taiwan Pavilion at the Paris Cultural Olympiad._

This scene is easily written as "Grandma's market bag conquers Paris." But such writing flattens the most important thing about the qiezhi bag: it was not brought to Paris to look like a luxury item. The bag's mesh body, handles, and red-blue-green stripes were not hidden; it remains a daily-use item that can hold, be carried, get wet, and not fear getting dirty. The Cultural Olympiad placed it in the gift pack precisely to let an ordinary tote serve as an entry point for recognizing Taiwan.[^1]

The Taiwan Pavilion was never a product showcase to begin with. The English press release states that the program assembled 22 performance troupes, over 120 participants, and arranged nearly 60 music, theater, and dance performances.[^3] In this context, the qiezhi bag was not a souvenir pushed onto the stage alone, but a commoner symbol alongside temple plazas, banquet catering, and performances: first letting people hold it in their hands, then letting them wonder where it came from.

## Houbi's Stone Rollers, Pressing Grass into a Carriable Bag

Its origin lies in Houbi, Tainan. Local government records indicate that Houbi became a weaving industry hub due to rush grass cultivation; local farmers made rush grass into tote bags, which gradually formed the industrial memory of the qiezhi bag.[^2] AgriMedia's interview in Jingliao preserves a more tactile picture: triangular rush grass is first sun-dried, then flattened with a stone roller, before entering the weaving process.[^4]

Chou Pao-feng began learning rush grass weaving from elders at age eight. Tainan City Government records note that Houbi's grass material is broader and thicker, suitable for bearing weight, but also more prone to breaking; the process requires sprinkling rice bran to increase toughness, then standing on the stone roller to repeatedly flatten it.[^5] These actions remind us that the qiezhi bag was not originally a pattern drawn by some designer, but an answer gradually worked out by farming families to make the material withstand weight.

It was also never merely a "market bag." The National Cultural Memory Bank records that before plastic materials became widespread, qiezhi were used for transport, cooking, and daily carrying; the same woven structure had to meet a household's hauling needs from kitchen to fields.[^9] Confining it to the imagination of "grandma shopping at the market" makes this bag appear far narrower than the life it actually participated in.

Huang Cheng-hsiung, chair of the Houcuo Community Development Association, interviewed by AgriMedia, said: "Taiwan's LV also started from here."[^4] This remark naturally carries humor, but it also articulates a contrast: a bag that once supplemented household income for grocery money and children's tuition later became an object recognizable enough to be imitated and redesigned.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** What is most worth remembering about the qiezhi bag is not that it is "very Taiwan," but that it originally possessed a design logic forced out by life: materials must be obtainable, the bag must bear weight, and it must not be ruined when wet. The later aesthetics were not added out of thin air, but rather people finally turned back to see this logic.

## Nylon Mesh Replaces Rush Grass, Also Changing a Way of Life

Rush grass bags and today's common mesh bags are not the same material. Regarding the precise starting point of the transition, sources differ slightly: AgriMedia records that Jingliao switched to red, blue, and green plastic mesh bags in the 1960s; Tainan City Government places the rise of the plastics industry and the spread of nylon mesh in the 1970s.[^4] [^6] For a daily object, this discrepancy itself is interesting: it was not "upgraded" overnight, but slowly taken over by another material amid industrial changes from the 1960s to 1970s.

| Period                 | Primary Material                              | Bag's Function                                                                                  | Verifiable Context                                                    |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Early rural            | Rush grass, triangular rush grass             | Sun-dry, flatten, weave into weight-bearing totes                                               | Houbi's household side businesses and local weaving industry[^2] [^4] |
| 1960s–1970s transition | Plastic mesh / nylon mesh                     | Respond to daily use with more moisture-resistant, abrasion-resistant, weight-bearing materials | Rise of plastics industry and material substitution[^4] [^6]          |
| Late 2000s onward      | Rush grass, plastic strapping, improved totes | Craft experience, community products, modern storage needs                                      | Revival of Jingliao workshop and product extensions[^5] [^7]          |

Plastic mesh indeed handles moisture and hauling better. Tainan City Government notes that nylon mesh, being waterproof, abrasion-resistant, and weight-bearing, gradually appeared in rural areas, wet markets, and grocery stores.[^6] But writing this simply as "tradition is inferior to modernity" is unfair: the decline of rush grass, rural outmigration, and the disappearance of traditional market stalls also changed the life scenes that hand weaving originally depended on.[^5]

Therefore, material change is not a straight upward line. On one hand, it brought faster, cheaper, more durable options to daily life; on the other, it let the craft of standing on stone rollers to flatten grass, completed through family division of labor, gradually cease to be a necessary skill for every household. The qiezhi bag later became nostalgic precisely because people began to realize that those once-unremarkable actions were retreating from daily life into memory.

## Red, Blue, and Green Borrowed for National Day, Commoner Object Stands at Center Stage

In 2017, the qiezhi bag's red, blue, and green were borrowed into the National Day main visual. The Golden Pin Design Award's preserved work data states clearly: the design team took inspiration from Taiwan's traditional craft weaving, paired with the qiezhi bag's three primary colors, bringing folk colors into the national celebration, and used interwoven lines to express the unity of different generations and ethnic groups.[^8]

_Extended viewing: [2017 Double Ten National Day Main Visual, Composed of Qiezhi Bag's Red, Blue, Green Color Scheme and Woven Imagery](https://www2.goldenpin.org.tw/file/2018/12/01-1.png)_

_Image: Golden Pin Design Award work data page._

This was not the first time the qiezhi bag was called "Taiwan's LV," but it was the moment it was placed at the center of the national visual. What deserves attention is not that the state finally "certified" a daily item's value, but that the design chose not to remodel it into something else: red, blue, and green remain the color scheme that recalls markets and grocery stores, and weaving remains the picture's structure. Commoner culture did not need to don formal wear to gain entry.

This line did not stop at National Day. The National Cultural Memory Bank records that the 2019 Hong Kong "Taiwan Way" market's main visual also used the qiezhi bag as an element, with actual qiezhi bags hung on the stage backdrop.[^9] From the woven lines before the Presidential Office to the physical display at an overseas market, this bag gradually became a Taiwan visual vocabulary needing no translation: seeing the grid and stripes, one knows it comes from a way of life that has long loaded the everyday.

In the late 2000s, the Jingliao qiezhi workshop also continued in this direction. Local data records the founding year as community actions after 2007; AgriMedia records Huang Cheng-hsiung establishing the workshop on Jingliao Old Street in 2008; both at least agree this revival occurred in the late 2000s.[^5] [^7] The workshop extended totes into business card holders, backpacks, lunch bags, coin purses, and water bottle bags, letting old weaving experience continue to be practiced in new usage contexts.[^5] [^7] The National Cultural Memory Bank adds that in 2008, Houcuo Community launched rush grass and "Taiwanese-style bag" hand-weaving classes through the Dream-Building Project; the workshop also experimented with stiffer strapping, multi-color mesh bags, and mixed-material fabrics to make crossbody work bags fitting A4 documents, waist packs, and storage bags.[^9]

The focus of these transformations is not slapping a "cultural creative" label on old objects, but acknowledging that usage patterns have changed. Farming families once needed a bag that could hold, be carried, and be replaced when broken; today's work bags and storage bags must respond to commuting, school, and office work. Materials differ, but the problem is shared: how to make a lightweight thing last a little longer in real life.

## Being Seen Anew Also Means the Original Scenes Are Receding

"From tacky to trendy" is the handiest qiezhi bag story, but not the whole story. It makes the transformed results sound too easy, as if switching a design language solves local problems. Tainan City Government simultaneously writes in Chou Pao-feng's story about rural outmigration, the disappearance of traditional market stalls, and the community's attempt to retain the craft through workshops, experiences, and innovative products.[^5]

This is not opposition to cultural creative industries. On the contrary, precisely because the workshop makes business card holders, backpacks, and lunch bags, Houbi's rush grass processing and weaving methods have the chance to be seen, touched, and asked about by the next generation. The problem is: if only the red-blue-green pattern remains, but the labor it grew from is forgotten, the qiezhi bag becomes merely a reproducible Taiwan business card.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** True cultural transformation is not renaming "tacky" to "trendy." It is letting people know: a thing often has beauty not because it was designed to be pretty first, but because someone lived life precisely enough for long enough.

The 500 gift sets distributed in that Paris hour let more people hold a qiezhi bag for the first time.[^1] And Houbi's stone rollers still remind of another matter: what deserves to be recognized by the world should not be just three color stripes, but those who flattened the grass, sewed the bags, and packed their days into them. The qiezhi bag need not disguise itself as a luxury item to deserve being cherished; its original form already explains how Taiwan makes the everyday sufficiently resilient.
