> **30-Second Overview:** Every year in the third lunar month, hundreds of thousands of devotees, holding incense sticks or dressed in casual attire, follow the Matsu palanquin from Dajia Zhenlan Palace in Taichung on a nine-day, eight-night, hundreds-of-kilometer徒步 (foot) pilgrimage. This event, known as the "Dajia Matsu Procession and Pilgrimage," which international media has hailed as one of the world's top three religious spectacles, has long transcended the realm of simple folk belief. It is a civic society experiment that connects cross-countery strongholds in central Taiwan, erases class differences, and perfectly demonstrates the strong cohesion and mutual aid spirit of Taiwan's grassroots society through the "snack stall" culture of unpaid contributions along the route.

If one were to choose the most震撼力 (shocking/impactful) and mobilizing collective ritual in Taiwan's annual calendar, the "Matsu Mania" of the third lunar month is undoubtedly the answer[^3]. When the bronze gongs of Dajia Zhenlan Palace sound, millions of devotees and various performance troupes converge into an endless dragon-like stream of people, embarking on a pilgrimage journey spanning over 300 kilometers across four counties and cities: Taichung, Changhua, Yunlin, and Chiayi[^8]. This ceremony, listed by the internationally renowned Discovery Channel as one of the world's top three religious events, not only carries centuries of historical memory but has also evolved into a unique cultural and social phenomenon in contemporary society[^4].

However, viewing the Dajia Matsu Procession merely as a traditional religious superstition or local deity-welcoming festival would cause one to miss its core modern value. This nine-day, eight-night pilgrimage is, in essence, a large-scale civic society experiment deeply rooted in the everyday life of Taiwan's common people.

## Historical Foundations and the Evolution of Divine Networks

The history of the Dajia Matsu Procession and Pilgrimage can be traced back to the Yongzheng reign of the Qing Dynasty[^8]. According to historical records and the梳理 (sorting/analysis) by the Ministry of the Interior's Taiwan Religious Culture Map, around 1730 (the 8th year of Yongzheng), Lin Yongxing from Meizhou, Fujian, brought the incense of Matsu he personally enshrined to settle in Dajia Bao, Taiwan. Subsequently, local devotees and gentry gradually established the flourishing Zhenlan Palace[^8].

In early historical development, the incense and status of Zhenlan Palace were not initially dominant. Scholarly research indicates that early Dajia Matsu actively promoted long-distance pilgrimage and procession activities partly to seek spiritual empowerment and route connections from external sources (such as Beigang Chaotian Temple or other ancestral temples), thereby consolidating its position in the regional religious network and expanding devotee loyalty[^2]. As time progressed, the procession route evolved from specific early locations to the current fixed nine-day, eight-night itinerary to Xingang Fengtian Palace. Every temple and village along the way has established dense political, ethnic, and economic alliance relationships along the historical vertical axis.

## Snack Stalls and the Flowing Economics of Unpaid Labor

For foreign observers or the younger generation participating in the Dajia Matsu Procession for the first time, what is often most shocking and incredible is not the majesty of the palanquin, but the continuous culture of "free结缘 (karmic connection)" along the way[^3].

Along the long roads traversed by the procession, one can often see "snack stalls" spontaneously set up by residents from various places, enterprises, and even volunteer groups. From freshly fried rice balls, meat zongzi, and rice noodles, to cooling mung bean soup, fruits, sports drinks, and even free massage stations and foot-washing water, these supplies are all paid for out-of-pocket by devotees and provided free of charge to all unfamiliar pilgrims (xiangdingjiao).

This unrewarded generosity is not based on any official administrative order or commercial exchange, but stems from a deep-seated religious psychology of fulfilling vows and a tradition of mutual aid. Devotees firmly believe that "it is more blessed to give than to receive," expressing gratitude to the deities through the contribution of labor and food, while also accumulating merit for themselves and their families. This flowing economics of unpaid labor appears particularly peculiar and warm in a highly capitalist modern society. It breaks down interpersonal defenses within just a few days, turning the entire procession route into a utopia filled with mutual aid and trust.

## The Dissolution of Class and a Microcosm of Contemporary Democratic Society

Another fascinating sociological feature of the Dajia Matsu Procession lies in its temporary dissolution of daily social classes.

In the hundreds-of-kilometer徒步 (foot) column, entrepreneurs, political and business elites, university professors, factory workers, taxi drivers, and retired elders all change into the same casual clothing, braving the same scorching sun and wind to take steps. Here, no one cares about your social status, wealth, or educational background; the only thing that determines whether you can persist is your legs and perseverance.

This "de-classified" collective experience generates a strong sense of identity and community consciousness among participants. Many political figures view participating in the procession as an important political ritual to demonstrate their approachability and groundedness. However, no matter how political figures shuttle through the crowd, what truly dictates the rhythm of this grand event are always the grassroots people silently walking along the sides of the queue, writing history with their feet.

## Digital Waves and the Modern Rebirth of Traditional Folk Customs

Entering the 21st century, the Dajia Matsu Procession has also undergone drastic technological and digital transformation. The era when one could only rely on word-of-mouth or paper manuals to track the position of the procession has long passed; today, the Dajia Matsu Procession is hailed as one of the "most technologically advanced" religious activities on Earth.

Devotees can not only use the official "Zhenlan Palace" mobile application or GPS positioning system to track the precise meter-level real-time location of the Matsu palanquin at any time, but also watch 24-hour uninterrupted live streams on various social media platforms. This perfect combination of traditional folk customs and modern high technology not only significantly lowers the threshold for participation by the younger generation but also attracts a large number of domestic and international tourists to participate remotely through digital devices, successfully bringing Taiwan's indigenous religious culture to the international stage.

## Conclusion: The Flowing Imprint of the Taiwanese Spirit

This million-person migration every year in the third lunar month has long transcended a simple prayer ritual. It is a living fossil of Taiwan's historical memory, a democratic exercise of ethnic and local social compromise and connection, and the largest stage for grassroots people to demonstrate inclusiveness, generosity, and resilience.

As the Matsu palanquin slowly passes through the streets and alleys surrounded by tens of thousands of people, the deafening sound of firecrackers and the unceasing shouts of "Jin! Jin!" (Advance!) are not only pious calls to the deities, but also collective declarations of the people on this island repeatedly凝聚 (condensing/gathering) consensus in the wind and rain, continuing to move forward.
