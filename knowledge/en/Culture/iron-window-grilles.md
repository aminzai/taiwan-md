> **30-Second Overview:** Ornamental iron window grilles appeared in Taiwan's architecture during the Japanese colonial period; wartime metal recycling interrupted their production, and they spread widely in residential settings from the late 1960s through the 1970s. Serving both security and lighting functions as well as decoration, they also inscribed homeowners' professions, beliefs, and family memories onto the streets. This article traces a path from the 48 grilles of the 1958 Taiwan Provincial Assembly building to the fieldwork of the Old House Research Studio, Lukang's historic houses, and heritage building preservation, explaining how an unsigned piece of iron became part of the history of everyday life.

![The window grille of a mid-lane house in Beitun, Taichung, showing how ironwork became a daily component of the residential facade](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a6/Houses_with_window_grills_in_Beitun.jpg)

The image is used to illustrate the streetscape context and does not constitute evidence for any specific era or homeowner's story._

In 1958, the windows of the Taiwan Provincial Assembly Building were fitted with 48 ornamental iron grilles. Each grille measured approximately 136 cm in length and 86 cm in width, with no two grilles sharing the same pattern. This was not the standardized security mesh common today, but rather a set of iron pieces that placed geometric lines, hand-welded craftsmanship, and political architectural grandeur on the same wall. [^1]

Looking further back, ornamental iron window grilles had already appeared in Taiwan's architecture during the Japanese colonial period. After the escalation of war in 1937, the Japanese government issued metal recycling orders, and the iron grilles and railings on private residences were dismantled and repurposed as wartime materials. Ornamental iron window grilles were not always present on the streets; they once disappeared from daily life due to war. [^1]

Therefore, Taiwan's familiar ornamental iron window grilles cannot be simply summarized as "decorations of old houses." They are the result of post-war housing, industrial supply, family security, and handicraft skills growing together. The most counterintuitive aspect is that something originally meant to keep people out ended up displaying the homeowner's own story to passersby.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Taiwan's ornamental iron window grilles are not the last line of defense for keeping a home secure, but the first time a home speaks its story to the street.

## From Wartime Shortage to Street House Daily Life

The data from the Legislative Yuan's Democratic Governance Park clearly pinpoints the timeline. In the early post-war period, steel was primarily used for reconstruction and national defense, with civilian use restricted. By the late 1960s, industry shifted from agriculture to manufacturing, and after China Steel Corporation was founded in 1971, steel supply became more stable, allowing ornamental iron window grilles to gradually enter ordinary residences. [^1]

This timeline explains why ornamental iron window grilles are often seen as a visual symbol of "post-war Taiwan." The 1950s to 1970s were precisely the period of rapid housing growth, with row houses and apartment buildings emerging in large numbers. Reports from the Taiwan Panorama magazine indicate that this was also an era when houses were built quickly but designs were very free-form. [^2] Here, "popularization" should not be understood as every household using the same pattern, but rather as material supply, housing demand, and ironworking techniques gradually making window grilles affordable and customizable household components.

At that time, window grilles were not selected from a catalog with a simple model number. The old house renovation manual from the Kaohsiung City Urban Development Bureau points out that early window grilles were mainly made of wrought iron, shaped through forging and bending, without fixed styles; geometric arrangements, floral patterns, and figurative motifs could all become a craftsman's compositional materials. [^3]

This approach made the windows on the same street look like relatives, yet not like mass-produced products. Some wanted flowers, some wanted landscapes, and some incorporated their professions, beliefs, or family preferences. When compiling the book "Old House Faces and Iron Lattice Windows," the Taipei Public Library also specially mentioned that landscapes, flowers and birds, music and dance, animals and insects, as well as religious feng shui elements could all appear in ornamental iron window grilles. [^4]

## One Grille, One Owner's Life

![The facade of an old house in Lukang, providing a visual background for understanding the relationship between local row houses and windows and doors](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/02/Old_taiwanese_house_in_Lukang%2C_Taiwan.JPG)

_Photo: Old house in Lukang. By Yug, licensed under [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/), [Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Old_taiwanese_house_in_Lukang,_Taiwan.JPG). This image is used to explain the relationship between old house facades and living scales, and does not claim that every window shown in the photo is the ornamental iron window grille described in this article._

In 2020, the reading program "Dushu Xianxing" interviewed Hsin Yung-sheng and Yang Chao-ching from the Old House Research Studio. Originally, they were just riding motorcycles through the alleys of Tainan taking photos of old houses; as they continued shooting, they discovered that the patterns on the window grilles could tell more about the residents' personalities than the house's exterior walls. Within six months, they had taken one or two thousand photos of ornamental iron window grilles. [^5]

Their working method was very similar to field research. They not only took frontal photos but also talked with homeowners, asking how the patterns came to be and how the houses were built. In Yilan's Toucheng, there is a pair of dragon and phoenix iron grilles; in Lukang, there is a homeowner who turned memories of traveling to Europe with his daughter into a triumphal arch design. The iron grilles were originally inanimate pieces of iron, but the stories only emerged after the homeowner opened the door and offered drinks. [^5]

Hsin Yung-sheng said, "What we're looking for is the flavor of memory." This statement was originally about breakfast and old shops, but it also applies to ornamental iron window grilles. Ornamental iron window grilles are not artworks detached from life; together with polished stone floors, ancestral halls, door frame heights, and potted plant branches and leaves, they form the living space used by a family every day. [^5]

This also changed our way of looking at old houses. Real estate agents refer to houses as "objects," but homeowners treat them as containers where people have lived. The value of window grilles lies not only in their appearance but also in the fact that they allow a stranger to know what this family once believed in, who they remembered, and what they wanted to leave visible to those outside the door. [^4] [^5]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The most precious part of ornamental iron window grilles is often not how beautifully they are welded, but how they give homeowners the willingness to spend five more minutes talking about their home.

## The 48 Grilles of the Legislative Yuan: Why Each One Is Different

The Taiwan Provincial Assembly Building provided a public architecture version of comparison. The building was completed in 1958, using large-scale ornamental iron window grilles that simultaneously served security and decorative functions. The front had 30 grilles, with 8 on each side, totaling 48 grilles, each with a different design. [^1]

The data page indicates that the grilles feature shapes resembling Western rapiers. Local legend offers an explanation, suggesting that provincial assembly members defended the people's cause in the assembly hall, engaging in verbal sparring with officials or other legislators, hence incorporating the imagery of sword fighting into the window grilles. Since the original design intent of this story is unverifiable, it is more appropriate to treat it as a local memory of the architecture rather than a definitive conclusion in design history. [^1]

This example is important not because it proves that every flower has a secret code. It reminds us that ornamental iron window grilles in Taiwan have never only existed at the scale of private residences. They can be a small piece of window grille outside a kitchen, or they can be an entire row of architectural vocabulary on the facade of a public institution. Function and symbolism have been intertwined from the very beginning.

## Who Records These Unsigned Works

The creators of ornamental iron window grilles rarely left their names behind. Craftsmen might have worked based on a homeowner's casual request for "a little bit of decoration" or a hand-drawn sketch; once the house was completed, the work became part of the architecture. Unlike sculptures with plaques or paintings with signatures, usually only the people living inside know why it looks the way it does.

Founded in 2013, the Old House Research Studio made these unsigned streetscapes finally subject to large-scale organization and sharing. The Taipei Public Library records that Hsin Yung-sheng and Yang Chao-ching started by sharing photos online, gradually building a community, publishing books, and turning ornamental iron window grilles from "a very ordinary thing on old houses" into a preservable element of Taiwanese culture. [^4]

The special exhibition page of the National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts records artist Wu Chuan-yi's creative and research work on ornamental iron window grilles since 2015. When ornamental iron window grilles are placed in artistic creation, they are no longer just accessories to old houses but also become a medium for re-examining collective memory. [^6]

In 2026, the Tainan Jiujin Art Museum arranged a lecture on Yang Chao-ching's "Record of Taiwan's Iron Lattice Windows," showing that this topic has moved from personal photography to public discussion at local cultural institutions. [^7]

This transformation carries a bit of contradiction. What makes ornamental iron window grilles so moving is precisely that they were not originally meant to be seen. When they are organized, published, and exhibited, Taiwan begins to see its own daily life, but it must also be careful not to over-package the homeowners' life stories into a consumable retro style.

## Preservation Does Not Mean Turning Every Window into an Exhibit

The difficulty in preserving old houses usually comes from the fact that the houses simultaneously need safety, lighting, ventilation, and new ways of use. Removing old windows and replacing them with aluminum windows is fast and easy to meet modern needs. Keeping the iron window grilles requires understanding their structure, degree of rust, fixing methods, and original usage context.

The historical building data page of the Hualien County Cultural Affairs Bureau lists "iron lattice windows and white walls" as one of the identifiable features of the Ministry of Forestry Staff Dormitory buildings at No. 33 and 33-2 Jingwu Street. When introducing the preservation plan for the Shuangxing Star Village in Pingtung County, it also mentioned that 120 historical buildings retained the traditional iron lattice windows, cement decorative tiles, and stained glass windows. These cases show that the focus of preservation is not to move decorations into a museum separately, but to keep them as much as possible within the original architectural context. [^8] [^9]

The old house renovation manual from Kaohsiung City provides another direction. Old houses can be reused, and iron lattice windows can also become materials in new spaces, but the premise is to understand the original craftsmanship and proportions, rather than simply copying a pattern that looks "very retro." [^3]

A report from the Department of Journalism at Soochow University mentioned that ornamental iron window grilles are being transformed into cultural and creative crafts. This path can provide new income sources for traditional craftsmanship, but it may also flatten the originally complex living context into commodity patterns. What is truly worth preserving includes not only flowers, butterflies, or geometric lines, but also how the craftsmen made them, why the homeowners chose them, and which kind of life these patterns were once attached to. [^10]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The minimum standard for preserving ornamental iron window grilles is to polish them up but not let them lose the house's original purpose of protection, lighting, and storytelling.

## What We See Today May Be the Last Layer of Iron Paint

Taiwan's urban renewal and old house renovation will continue. Not every iron window is worth keeping intact, and not every new building needs to imitate post-war flower windows. A more honest approach is to record first and then judge: take photos of the patterns, interview homeowners, identify the craftsmanship, confirm the age of the house, and finally decide whether to repair, repurpose, or demolish.

A report reprinted by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' NGO Bilingual Website mentioned that a Lukang homeowner incorporated the local attractions of Shiyi Pavilion and the Half-Edge Well into the window grille. Such examples show that window grilles are not simply decorative frames but a way for residents to shrink the local map to their doorstep. [^11]

If we only regard ornamental iron window grilles as anti-theft devices, we will miss their second function. If we only see them as retro aesthetics, we will forget that they once responded to real security anxieties, material shortages, and family needs. They are both protective and demonstrative. They block a little of the outside world and reveal a little of the lives inside.

## Three Things to Clarify When Viewing the Images

First, images can help readers identify the scale of iron window grilles, windows and doors, and old house facades, but they cannot alone prove the era of a particular pattern. Second, the author and license information on the Commons file page and the historical origin of Taiwan's ornamental iron window grilles are two separate evidence chains. The former answers "Can this photo be used?" while the latter only answers "How was this window formed?" Third, the presence of window grilles in old house photos does not mean that the homeowner's story can be freely supplemented by the viewer. What can be confirmed should be based on the file page and article sources; what cannot be confirmed should be kept as a visual impression and not written as historical fact.

This distinction may seem conservative, but it is the most basic respect for preserving everyday architecture. Ornamental iron window grilles rarely have signatures, and once photos are separated from their original explanations, they are easily reattached with incorrect locations, eras, or homeowner identities. Therefore, images are not decorations for the article but also entry points for historical materials that need to be verified together.

Next time you walk through the alleys of Taiwan, don't rush to find a beautiful old house. First, look at the windows. Look for an asymmetric corner, a pattern resembling a travel souvenir, a flower obscured by potted plants, or a layer of black paint that has already faded. There may be no author's name there, but someone once made gestures in front of a craftsman, someone once installed it for safety, and someone once placed the most wanted thing in their life outside the window.
