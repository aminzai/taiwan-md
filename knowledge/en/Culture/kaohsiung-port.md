> **30-second overview:** In 1908, the first phase of Takao harbor construction began; by 1970, Kaohsiung Port's first container center was completed. The decades between were not a string of naturally occurring years, but a time when railways, sugar mills, bananas, customs, warehouses, factories, the military, and urban residents pulled against one another. Kaohsiung Port let southern Taiwan's products reach the world faster, while also causing harbor-front land to be renamed again and again. Today, walking along Penglai Road, the red-brick Port History Museum and the emptied banana sheds still remind us: a port's history never belongs only to ships and cargo[^1].

## A Small Harbor with a Narrow Entrance

In nineteenth-century texts, Takao Port was not a naturally fine harbor suited to large vessels. Materials on the north-south dual ports compiled by the National Archives Administration describe it as originally a small fishing port used for seasonal mullet fishing and shelter. The entrance had massive rocks, the channel was narrow, and the harbor interior suffered from shallows and siltation[^1]. Local history research adds that the area of today's Yancheng was once salt fields, and life around Qijin, Shaochuantou, and the harbor environs was first composed of fishing, salt drying, and small-scale trade — not predetermined by a modern port plan[^4].

After the Qing court opened ports including Takao to trade following its 1860 defeat, sugar became the key commodity drawing foreign merchants to southern Taiwan. Britain established a vice-consulate at Takao in 1864, not because the port was already mature, but because merchants believed it still held the potential to be made into a proper harbor[^4]. This distinction is worth preserving: a port's value is determined not only by natural conditions, but also by the bets merchants, governments, and engineers place on the future.

Kaohsiung Port is often remembered as "Taiwan's largest port," but looking back from the early landscape, this outcome was hardly inevitable. The bay had to be surveyed, dredged, and reclaimed, then connected to railways and warehousing. Each project converted the original sea, salt fields, fish ponds, and settlements into new public or industrial spaces. The port did not wait on the shore for cargo; it was manufactured as an urban organ by institutions and engineering.

## The Railway Turned the Port into an Export Machine

After Japan colonized Taiwan, the sugar industry became a pillar of the colonial economy. The problem soon appeared: how to ship southern Taiwan's sugar back to Japan? If all goods had to detour through Keelung for export, neither time nor cost made sense. Local history articles record that around 1900 the Governor-General's Office began surveying Takao Port and viewed linking the railway trunk line's southern terminus to the harbor as the solution. Between 1904 and 1907, land reclamation and railway extension simultaneously transformed the scale of the harbor front[^4].

The trunk line opened its full length in 1908, the same year the Takao harbor construction plan launched. The National Archives Administration's compilation notes that the first-phase harbor works between 1908 and 1912 laid the foundation of modern Kaohsiung Port and forged a new intermodal relationship among railway, port, and rice-sugar exports[^1]. "Intermodal" here is no abstract noun: sugarcane from the fields, sugar from the factories, freight cars on the trains, and stevedores on the quays were from then on placed into a single schedule.

Reclamation also simultaneously changed the city's residential order. Local sources describe Hamasen as deriving from the Japanese reading of the "coastal line" (Hin-sen), pointing to the relationship between the coastal railway and the newly created land. The new district had grid streets, electricity, streetlights, sewers, and a public market, becoming the concentration of Japanese administration and commerce[^4]. But modernization was not a light evenly sprinkled on everyone. Japanese held more political and economic resources, while immigrants from Penghu and the Tainan coast largely entered laboring jobs. The port attracted population, but also slotted different ethnic groups into unequal spaces.

| Project or Institution          | Capability Gained by the Port                         | Life Rearranged                                             |
| ------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------- |
| Trunk line & harbor spur line   | Let sugar, rice, and daily goods reach quays quickly  | Cargo routes cut through existing settlements and coastline |
| First-phase harbor construction | Dredged channels, improved vessel access              | Sea, salt fields, and new land rezoned                      |
| Port & customs administrations  | Enabled taxation, inspection, and management of goods | Port became a front line of state institutions              |
| New districts like Hamasen      | Concentrated commerce, administration, transport      | Different immigrants segregated by resources and occupation |

This also explains why Kaohsiung Port's story cannot be written only as "Japanese built the port, therefore Taiwan modernized." Construction did bring transport efficiency, but it simultaneously concentrated land, labor, and decision-making power in new port-city mechanisms. As the port expanded, who could approach the quays, who could work on the harbor front, whose houses would be absorbed into the new district — all were part of modernization.

## Tax and Sea Inside the Red-Brick Port History Museum

Beside Pier 3 in the Penglai Commercial Port Area, the Kaohsiung Port History Museum is a two-story red-brick building. The Kaohsiung Cultural Assets website records it was started in 1916, completed in 1917, originally serving as the field office of the Taiwan Governor-General's Bureau of Finance stationed at Takao Port, responsible for collecting the sugar consumption tax. It later passed through customs, maritime, and port administrations before being renovated in 1997 into the Port History Museum[^7] [^8]. The National Cultural Assets website confirms via announcement data its groundbreaking in December 1916, completion in 1917, Western-classical-style brick exterior, and historic building status[^10].

The most interesting thing about this building is not just its red bricks and arched windows, but that it fixes the port's "invisible" work into a concrete space. Before a ship arrived, the cargo's value had already to be registered, classified, appraised, and taxed. Sugar did not simply vanish from production sites into the sea; at the port it was redefined by state organs. That the museum was once an official office perfectly illustrates that port modernization relied not only on cranes and quays, but also on forms, seals, tax rates, and human judgment.

The academic article "Study on the Historical Changes of the Kaohsiung Port History Museum" further organizes this building's institutional succession: from the Takao Field Office to the Kaohsiung Customs, Kaohsiung Maritime Field Office, then the Kaohsiung Harbor Bureau and Joint Inspection Center, its functions shifted with changes in the port administration system[^3]. If treated merely as a "Japanese-era old house," one misses its work history handling maritime affairs, anti-smuggling, and trade administration. The red bricks are not decoration; they are evidence of how the port fixed fluid maritime activity into state procedure.

The museum also makes "preservation" less romantic. The Kaohsiung Cultural Assets website notes the building once faced a demolition plan in 1994, but was retained, restored, and adaptively reused due to its historical significance and cultural value[^7] [^8]. Whether a building survives depends not only on its age, but on whether the city is willing to acknowledge that tax, maritime, and port work — not easily made into postcards — equally constitute Kaohsiung's history.

## Bananas Brought the Countryside to the Quay

In postwar Kaohsiung Port, one of the most memorable cargoes was bananas. The National Archives Administration's "Banana Exports to Japan and Taiwan's Economy" notes that by the 1930s bananas already ranked alongside rice and sugar as one of Taiwan's three major exports. In the early postwar period, Taiwan bananas were first shipped in large volumes to mainland China; around 1949 the situation changed, and only then did government and private sectors more actively consider the Japanese market[^11]. This pivot was not a simple market choice, but was also shaped by cross-Strait transport, trade systems, and Japanese import policy.

What banana exports to Japan truly required was not just farmers growing quality-stable fruit. Archival articles record that fruit cooperatives and export associations competed over loading ratios. To prevent bananas from sun and rain exposure, operators demanded trucks be fitted with canvas covers, and the government addressed cold storage, railway spurs, bamboo basket and cardboard box standards, and other issues[^11]. A single bunch of bananas from field to Japanese market had to pass through harvesting, grading, packaging, rail, cold storage, quay, and shipping. The port was therefore not the production site's endpoint, but the place where agriculture was re-standardized by the international market.

The banana sheds beside Pier 3 are the architecture this chain left behind. The National Cultural Assets website records that by the late 1950s banana production and exports exceeded Japanese-era scale, and the original open-air piling left fruit exposed to sun and rain. The banana sheds, begun in 1962 and completed in 1963, adopted an open design using ventilation to improve storage, and installed mechanical transport equipment, with a capacity of 23,300 baskets[^2]. They look like spacious warehouses, but are in fact the concretization of the agricultural export system.

| Before and After Bananas Enter the Port | Required Technology                     | Performed By                                        |
| --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------- |
| Harvesting & grading at origin          | Control ripeness & appearance           | Banana farmers, marketing groups, inspectors        |
| Land & rail transport                   | Avoid bruising, sun, rain               | Stevedores, railway & transport operators           |
| Temporary storage in banana sheds       | Ventilation, stacking, quay connection  | Harbor Bureau & quay workers                        |
| Export to Japan                         | Packaging specs, trade quotas, shipping | Government, cooperatives, exporters, shipping lines |

The banana boom also had its limit. The National Archives Administration records that after Japan's 1952 trade liberalization, other producing countries entered the Japanese market, and Taiwan bananas' advantage gradually shrank. Cited statistics show that at its peak Taiwan bananas exported over 400,000 tonnes annually, holding 82% of the Japanese market; by 2014 only about 4,000 tonnes remained, a 1% share[^9]. These figures cannot be swapped for a single Kaohsiung quay's throughput, but they clearly explain one thing: what an export port fears most is not lacking history, but that when the world market changes, the existing transport, varieties, and systems lose their place.

The banana sheds' historic building registration rationale is therefore not merely "Taiwan once exported bananas well." It preserves how an agricultural era was organized by port, rail, and warehousing. When containerization and industrial transformation gradually idled the banana sheds, the building leaves a question for posterity: can a space designed to accelerate exports still be understood when the export mode disappears, rather than merely consumed as a nostalgic backdrop?

## From Container Center to Port-City Separation

The second great postwar change for Kaohsiung Port came from the twelve-year expansion project begun in 1958. The National Archives Administration records the project completed in 1970, including channel dredging, new land reclamation, pier and shallow-water quay construction. The reclaimed new land of approximately 544 hectares provided space for industrial use and the later commercial port area[^1] [^5]. This was not simply making the port "bigger," but pushing Kaohsiung's urban scale toward the coast and industrial zones.

Kaohsiung Port's first container center completed in 1970, marking Taiwan's formal entry into a new stage of container transport[^1] [^5]. Container efficiency made cargo easier to standardize, transship, and schedule across borders, and caused old-style bulk warehouses and quay operations to gradually lose their central position. For the port itself, this was competitiveness. For the old port area, it was another kind of slow exit. Work that once required masses of workers, railways, and harbor warehouses was rearranged by new equipment, deep-water quays, and larger hinterlands.

Kaohsiung Port once ranked as the world's fifth-largest container port in 1982, and in 1993 surpassed Rotterdam to become the world's third-largest container port. These achievements have a clear timeline in the National Archives articles[^1]. But these rankings should not be used to paper over cracks in the port-city relationship. The more efficient the port, the more it may push the real operating zone away from citizens' daily life. The more a quay needs security and control, the more it may let shore-side residents see the sea but not enter the port.

Thus Kaohsiung Port's transformation from the 1990s onward is not just tourism development, but Kaohsiung attempting to re-answer the question of "whether the port and city must share the same shore"[^10] [^11]. The Pier-2 Art Center, warehouse clusters, Hamasen Railway Cultural Park, and Port History Museum each preserve different layers of memory: warehouses preserve cargo volume, railways preserve direction of movement, the red building preserves state administrative procedure, and the harbor shore preserves the city sightlines once walled off.

## The Old Port Area Does Not Automatically Become a Cultural District

In a 2025 press release, the Kaohsiung City Government Urban Development Bureau reviewed the Yancheng-Penglai old port area's transformation, linking the 1998 "Ocean Capital" initiative, the 2005 removal of the port area wall, the 2011 Awan (Asia New Bay Area) construction, and subsequent development of Pier-2, the Great Harbor Bridge, and warehouse clusters[^6]. The material also mentions the city government's plan to make the Banana Pier a research base for the Smart Kaohsiung Lighthouse Project, rewriting the old port area with technology, humanities, and cultural-creative tourism.

These contents must retain the tone of "planning." The banana shed cultural assets page currently indicates the building was repossessed for self-management by the Taiwan International Ports Corporation Kaohsiung Branch in late February 2024, with closure planning underway, and marked as not open to visitors[^2]. Therefore, one cannot take travel websites' descriptions of free admission, banquet restaurants, or a banana story museum as current conditions. Port area transformation is not just hanging new signs; building management, opening, restoration, and use rights remain practical problems.

The museum offers a comparatively stable case. It transitioned from official office to a hall displaying port history, and official cultural assets data still lists opening hours and management unit[^10]. But whether exhibitions let people understand the lives of port staff, quay workers, customs officers, and immigrants depends on curatorial choices. If only port rankings, ship models, and city skylines are displayed, port history shrinks again into "successful Kaohsiung." If sugar tax, banana packaging, harbor railways, wartime bombing, and postwar recovery are placed together, audiences will see that the port is actually a system that sorts, classifies, and accelerates people and goods.

## Who Did the Port Bring Before the World

Kaohsiung Port's story is often written as southern Taiwan's leap from a small fishing port to an international harbor. This direction is not wrong, but it is not enough. The small fishing port became an international harbor through government investment, colonial engineering, sugar and banana markets, railways, and the labor of quay workers. It was accompanied by reclamation, expropriation, militarization, immigrant stratification, and port zone control. If one only watches ships leave the port, one misses how land was taken to build the harbor, and how people were slotted into different positions in the cargo chain.

The 1908 harbor construction linked Takao to the trunk line; the 1958 expansion gave Kaohsiung a larger industrial hinterland; the 1970 container center pushed the city into global logistics. These three time points are not three unrelated projects, but the stepwise acceleration of a single mindset: "make circulation faster." Kaohsiung Port's modernity is built precisely on speed: sugar had to load faster, bananas had to chill faster, containers had to turn faster, industrial raw materials had to move in and out faster.

But urban memory is often not preserved by speed. It hides in the tax archives behind a red-brick arched window of the Port History Museum, in the open floors of the banana sheds originally designed for 23,300 baskets of bananas, and in Hamasen, a colloquial place name derived from the coastal line. These surviving objects give Kaohsiung the chance not only to display how powerful a port once was, but to ask: in each era when the port pursued efficiency, whose labor was counted, whose life was displaced, and who today reclaims the right to interpret this shore?

Therefore, walking Kaohsiung Port need not start from "where looks best for photos." One can begin at the red building beside Pier 3, imagining how the sugar consumption tax was recorded. Then look at the banana sheds, understanding how many steps not in the photos agricultural export required. Finally move along Hamasen and the old port area, observing how railways, warehouses, walls, and new cultural spaces overlap. The port is not the city's background; it is a method the city once used to arrange the future, and a place where generations of laborers left bodily memory.

Today's Kaohsiung Port still connects to the world, but it no longer needs every old warehouse to keep loading cargo to qualify for preservation. True preservation is not freezing the port zone into a retro landscape, but letting people know why these buildings appeared, who worked inside them, what institutions made them thrive, and why they lost their original place after the next technological shift. When a port is willing to put its own tax, labor, war, immigration, and industrial decline into history, it does not only send Taiwan to the world — it begins to let the world understand how Taiwan became itself on the shore.

## How the Port's Scale Was Measured

Kaohsiung Port's space cannot be divided simply into "old port area" and "new port area." From channel, quay, railway, warehouse to city streets, the port is actually a set of mutually dependent yet not necessarily simultaneously visible scales. Aerial photos of the First Port and container center show distances between water, stacking yards, roads, and city. But aerial views have blind spots: they see the hinterland, but not how stevedores allocate time on the ground, nor how control lines decide who may enter the quay.

| Observation Scale      | Visible Matters                                                    | Easily Overlooked Matters                                                            |
| ---------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Channel & harbor basin | Vessel access, dredging, breakwater engineering scale              | Maintaining depth requires continuous investment, not a one-time project             |
| Quay & stacking yard   | Container, banana shed, warehouse, transshipment equipment layout  | After cargo standardization, some traditional stevedoring work replaced by equipment |
| Harbor railway         | Port station, signals, spurs, industrial hinterland connection     | Railway's remaining space ≠ original transport function still operating              |
| Urban shoreline        | Old port area, cultural facilities, residents' paths to view coast | Management boundaries remain between open tourism and port security                  |

Among these, signal towers, railways, and port stations are not just relics for railway enthusiasts. They record how cargo was permitted to pass, how trains connected with ship schedules, and how port staff maintained order in invisible procedures. When the harbor line no longer carries its original transport volume, the preservation challenge shifts from "preserve a building" to "how to interpret a system that has ceased to operate." If such interpretation leaves only photo landmarks, labor is erased from port history once again.

## Supplementary Material: How the Port Changed the City's Ways of Working

Breaking Kaohsiung Port's history into a few objects lets one see more clearly how it changed the city. The first object is the railway: it linked sugar mills, agricultural collection points, and quays on a single timetable, making export no longer dependent only on small near-shore boats. The second object is the banana sheds: they are not mere warehouses, but intermediary spaces controlling sun exposure, bruising, packaging, and loading time. The third object is the Port History Museum: it converts the institutional memory left by port authorities into a public building citizens can enter, view, and discuss[^2][^3][^7][^8][^9].

| Data Object                                | Question It Answers                                                                      | Limitations to Retain When Reading                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------------ | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Harbor construction archives               | When did the port change from small harbor to modern engineering system?                 | Official archives emphasize engineering & administration; laborers' voices fewer[^1][^5]             |
| Banana sheds & banana exports              | How were agricultural products graded, packaged, and connected to overseas markets?      | Export peak ≠ all farmers & stevedores benefited equally[^2][^9]                                     |
| Port History Museum & cultural assets data | How were port authorities preserved as urban memory?                                     | Preserving building ≠ fully restoring original functions[^3][^7][^8]                                 |
| Old port area reuse                        | How does the city reuse the shoreline after the port exits part of its freight function? | New cultural & tech uses still face port security, industrial memory, residents' use rights[^6][^11] |

This set of data also shows why a single "Kaohsiung Port is huge" photo cannot substitute for port history. The port's scale is manufactured by regulations, taxation, train schedules, cargo specifications, and labor division together. When one institution changes, other spaces rearrange accordingly. Today's Banana Pier, Port History Museum, and port station remnants are worth preserving not because they maintain original productivity, but because they let people ask: for whose cargo did a city once expand, and whose work was left in history's shadows?
