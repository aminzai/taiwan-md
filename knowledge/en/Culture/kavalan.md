## A Name Returning Along the Coast

> **30-Second Overview**: The history of the Kavalan people is not merely an event that was "officially recognized in 2002." To understand this group, one must simultaneously view the river-sea life of the Lanyang Plain, the dispersal caused by the Battle of Kaliawan in 1878, the tribal language and rituals preserved in the Hualien and Taitung communities, and how banana fiber weaving places cultural memory back into daily life.[^1] [^2] [^4]

In 1987, members from Xinshe, Hualien performed dances and rituals in Taipei. During the performance, the community spoke their tribal name, which also led outsiders to discover that among the Kavalan—who were thought to have been "Sinicized"—there were still people who could sing, perform rituals, and live using the Kavalan language. After this "Fengbin Night," the naming restoration movement moved beyond the community, becoming an action demanding that the nation re-see the group's subjectivity.[^2]

On December 25, 2002, the government announced the Kavalan as the eleventh Indigenous group of Taiwan.[^1] Within the same historical period is another fact that cannot be overshadowed by a simple "successful name restoration": some descendants still have not obtained Indigenous status because their ancestors were registered in household records as plainlanders.[^2]

Therefore, the story of the Kavalan cannot be compressed into a timeline from "disappearance" to "recognition." It is closer to a woven thread: some kept the tribal language by the sea, some remembered ancestral spirits through rituals, and some spun fine threads from banana stalks, weaving a name back onto fabric.

> 📝 Curator's Note: The starting point of a group being seen often precedes an official announcement. Only when someone refuses to forget themselves in daily life is there a possibility for a public name to reappear.

## "Plain People" of the Lanyang Plain

The Kavalan people refer to themselves as **kavalan**, and official documents interpret this as "the people of the plain." They lived in Yilan, with settlements near rivers and the sea; their early dwellings were stilt houses, and they also engaged in trade through seafaring.[^3]

A study preserved by the Lanyang Museum points out that the story of the Kavalan ancestors "coming from the sea" was long told from grandfather to grandson, and father to son. It was only recorded in writing by Japanese scholar Inō Kaku in 1896.[^4]

This oral history mentions a place called **Mariyan** and refers to the ancestors passing around Sandias Cape to Yilan, which was then known as Hozainan. This is part of the community's memory, not a single route fully proven by archaeology; the same museum study also presents different hypotheses from archaeology and linguistics.[^4]

The ancestral origin route still awaits more material dialogue. When a group lacks its own written history, external records easily become the only voice, but the history of the Kavalan is also preserved in the coast, estuaries, dugout canoes, and place names that are still spoken.

Archaeological data shows that sites related to the Kavalan are mostly distributed in coastal dunes, estuaries, or swampy areas in the inland plains. These locations indicate that water directly constituted the living conditions for settlement, food, transportation, and exchange.[^4]

## From Lanyang to Hualien and Taitung: Dispersal Was Not the End

In the late eighteenth century, Han Chinese began entering the Lanyang Plain, and in the early nineteenth century, the Qing government established the Kavalan Prefecture, leading to changes in land and administrative order. Starting from the mid-nineteenth century, large numbers of Kavalan people left their original homes and migrated to the coasts of Hualien and Taitung.[^1]

One group settled in Kaliawan, Hualien. In 1878, the Kavalan resisted Qing forces together with the Sakiyaiya people during the Battle of Kaliawan. After the battle, the community was forced to disperse along the East Coast and into the Taitung Valley; some lived among the Amis tribe using the identity of the Kaliawan people.[^5]

Academic research frames this event within the context of "how traditional territories changed due to state intervention," pointing out that after the Kaliawan incident, the activity space of the Kavalan in the Qilai Plain and East Coast changed.[^6]

External records often write this history as a disappearance of the group, but community memory suggests another narrative: the people changed their names and hid their language in order to survive, searching along the coast for places to settle.

The Kavalan in Xinshe, Hualien, were sometimes called **Kaliawan** by surrounding tribes. Before the naming restoration movement, this name made it difficult for outsiders to link them with "Kavalan," but for the community members, a break in the name did not mean a break in memory.[^3]

Xinshe borders the Pacific Ocean to the east and the mountain range to the west. After the Kavalan arrived, they cultivated rice using limited terraces and also engaged in fishing, gathering, and maritime activities. Official documents list rivers, coasts, and sea exchanges as important aspects of Kavalan life.[^3]

> 📝 Curator's Note: Migration changed the map, but it did not take the sea out of the Kavalan people's lives.

## How a Banana Tree Became Fabric

One of the most visible cultural objects of the Kavalan is banana fiber weaving. The Lanyang Museum records that the community selected mature but unripened banana trees, scraped the outer layer of the stalks to extract pale purple fibers, which were then dried, twisted into strands, warped, and woven.[^7]

One banana tree can weave a piece of banana cloth measuring 70 cm long and 50 cm wide. This figure first shows the fragility of the material: the fibers do not last long and must be processed quickly.[^7]

Before weaving, there was a simple **Spaw** ritual to pray for the ancestors' blessing for a smooth process. The waist belt, loom, shuttle, carding board, and spindle collectively formed the working posture; the weaver sat on the ground, fixing the tools with their body as the warp and weft interlocked one by one.[^7]

Nineteenth-century artifacts provide another clue to this craft. When Father Macchi visited Yilan and Hualien between 1870 and 1880, he collected Kavalan clothing, banana fiber weaving, and ritual items. About 40 of these collections are preserved in the Royal Ontario Museum in Canada.[^8]

These artifacts preserve precious clues but have also detached from their original community context, becoming specimens in a museum. When contemporary communities re-engage with them, the work is not just about replicating ancient patterns; it is about the community deciding how these objects should be understood today.[^8]

After 2002, banana fiber has become a medium for cultural revitalization and contemporary creation. Reports from the original perspective describe it as a historical carrier, documenting how artisans retrieve woven patterns from early artifacts and community memory and place them back into clothing and daily life.[^2]

The Lanyang Museum also preserves an elder's words: "In the past, outsiders would come to purchase them; now they do not, so the young people don't do it anymore."[^7] The weight of this statement is that it does not package traditional crafts as a never-ending landscape; it speaks to who will sustain the skill after the market declines.

## Rituals Bring Time Back to the Community

_Extended Viewing: [Scraping banana stalk fibers is a preliminary process for banana fiber weaving. Images from Lanyang Museum Special Issue 073.](https://www.lym.gov.tw/export/sites/lym/ch/collection/epaper/current-enews-list/images/enews-2021/202101-06/20210611-e-st-001-073-03.jpg)_

The revitalization of Kavalan culture is not limited to weaving. Communities on the East Coast of Hualien still record sea rituals (**sbaw tu lazin**), harvest festivals (**gataban**), year-end ancestral worship (**palilin**), and rituals related to female shamans (**metiyu**).[^2]

The harvest festival in Xinshe community is held in mid-August each year to commemorate the earth spirits and ancestors, while sea rituals are performed when the cassia flower blooms, praying for safety and a good catch before men go out to sea.[^2]

Some rituals have been discontinued or transformed into public cultural performances. This change reminds us that cultural revitalization does not mean bringing the past back exactly as it was; rather, it is about re-negotiating which content should be continued in what context after transportation, education, marriage, state systems, and demographic changes within the community.

The society of the Kavalan has also undergone inter-tribal marriage and identity changes. The migration and cohabitation of communities in Hualien and Taitung have made tribal names, language, and places of life part of contemporary identity discussions.[^2]

Therefore, reducing group identity to a ratio of bloodline misses the most crucial layer of Kavalan history: identity is created together by family, language, rituals, place of life, and shared memory.[^9]

## Three "Name Restorations" That Cannot Be Mixed

The restoration of the Kavalan name has at least three scales. The first is state recognition: the 2002 announcement placed the tribal name on the official Indigenous group list. The second is identity registration: not all descendants who self-identify as Kavalan can obtain Indigenous status through the same household registration process. The third is cultural practice: the language, rituals, weaving, sea rituals, and family memory must continue to be used in community life; otherwise, they will only remain explanatory text in a museum.[^1] [^2] [^8]

These three scales are interconnected but cannot replace each other. An announcement can change legal classification, but it cannot substitute for an elder teaching a child the tribal language. A piece of banana fiber clothing can preserve clues to the craft, but it cannot substitute for the community deciding how it should be worn today. A public performance can allow society to see the culture, but it does not mean that all private rituals should be open to the outside world.

## Who Has the Right to Name a Group?

The name "Kavalan" has different spellings in different eras. Seventeenth-century Spanish and Dutch documents have appeared as Cabalan or Kavalan, while Chinese records show Hozainan, Jiazilan, and Hazilan. These names are not fixed translations of the same administrative unit but traces left by different regimes, travelers, officials, and researchers when encountering the group.[^8]

In the late nineteenth century, anthropologists began building a knowledge system of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples through classification, surveys, and photography. These materials preserved clothing, ancient documents, houseboards, recordings, and images, allowing contemporary researchers to trace the lives of the Kavalan. However, what investigators chose to record, what museums collected, and which category household registration placed people in all influenced how later generations understood this group.[^8]

Therefore, a tribal name is not just an entry in a dictionary. It relates to who can be clearly named in schools, household registers, museums, land discussions, and public policy. When the community demands the use of their own name, they are demanding not just phonetic correction, but recognition from the state and society of a history that was not interrupted by administrative classification.[^2] [^9]

## Who Can Be Called After "Name Restoration"?

The official recognition in 2002 has institutional significance, but it did not automatically repair all identity ruptures. Reports from _Yuan Shi Jie_ pointed out that some members who completed registration around 1956 and 1963 were included in the recognition, yet descendants recorded as familiar subjects or classified as Pailou groups in Japanese colonial household registers still had not obtained Indigenous status.[^2]

This is why "name restoration" cannot be understood merely as a successful political event. It also involves household registration, land, educational resources, language inheritance, and who has the right to define the "true Indigenous person."

In 2018, the Kavalan and Sakiyaiya people held the 140th Anniversary Commemoration of the Kaliawan/Dagubu Bay Battle, re-narrating the battle and dispersal through exhibitions, documentaries, academic seminars, and community forums. The event also proposed that the purpose of commemorating the battle is to understand equality, mutual respect, and historical justice among groups, rather than creating new conflicts.[^5]

Pan Chaocheng stated in the event records: "The Kavalan migrated south from Lanyang after Han people entered, living a life of hardship in the Hualien and Taitung areas for over two hundred years."[^5] This sentence places migration on the map back into human time: two hundred years lived through generations, and also in the process of establishing a home in new places.

> 📝 Curator's Note: Restoration does not mean returning to some unchanged past. It empowers descendants to decide which things left by their ancestors should live on today.

## Keeping the Name in Daily Life

Today, the Kavalan are distributed across Yilan, Hualien, and Taitung. Official English documents list major distribution areas such as Zhuangwei and Wujie in Yilan, Xincheng and Fengbin in Hualien, and Changbin in Taitung, and record that the community has promoted respect for their name and subjectivity since the 1980s.[^3]

On the map, these places seem far apart. However, according to the Kavalan's own history, they are connected by sea routes, marriage, rituals, language, and family memory.

The importance of banana fiber weaving lies in its ability to transform grand history into a manual action: peeling a layer of banana stalk, extracting fibers, using ten fingers to connect short strands into a long line, and then weaving the long lines into cloth. Every step answers the same question: is there still someone willing to make this name with their own body?

The answer from the Kavalan does not stop in 2002. It resides in the sea rituals of Xinshe, the language re-sung in Yilan, the artifacts that travel between museums and communities, and in those who still demand fair recognition for Pailou descendants. The name has returned; what needs to be done next is to ensure it does not have to leave daily life again.
