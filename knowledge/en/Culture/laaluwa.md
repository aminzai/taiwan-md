> **30-second overview:** On June 26, 2014, the Hla'alua and Kanakanavu were officially recognized, becoming Taiwan's 15th and 16th Indigenous peoples. The Hla'alua story is not merely an administrative "splitting of groups": it involves the four Taoyuan communities, the Miatungusu (Holy Shell Festival) centered on shells, and a heritage language that gradually lost its daily living space amid the pressures of Bunun, Mandarin, and the education system. What rectification of names truly seeks to reclaim is the right for the people to tell their own story in their own name.

## A Name First Collected by Others

On June 26, 2014, the Executive Yuan approved the name-rectification proposals of the Hla'alua and Kanakanavu. After that day, Taiwan's official roster of Indigenous peoples grew from 14 to 16; the Hla'alua became the 15th. This date is often written as an institutional change, but for the people, it is more like a name long placed in someone else's classification system finally returning to their own hands.[^1] [^2]

The Hla'alua were previously subsumed under the Tsou and called "Southern Tsou." This label did not appear from nowhere: in the late Qing, Han settlers called them the "Upper Four Communities" (頂四社); during the Japanese colonial period, this continued as "Upper Four Communities" (上四社) or simply "Four Communities." The classification left historical traces, but also compressed groups with different languages, rituals, and social organizations into a single larger name.[^1] [^3]

The Hla'alua's own name is Hla'alua. The National Cultural Memory Bank records approximately 413 people as of January 2020. This is data from a specific point in time and should not be misread as a real-time population statistic for 2026.[^8] Council of Indigenous Peoples data indicates this group primarily resides in Gaojhong Village (高中里) and Taoyuan Village (桃源里) in Taoyuan District, Kaohsiung, and Maya Village (瑪雅里) in Namasia District, composed of the four communities of Paiciana (排剪), Vilian (美壠), Talicia (塔蠟), and Hlihlara (雁爾). The Cultural Park introduction also uses the four communities and the Miatungusu as the entry point for understanding the Hla'alua.[^9] The four communities are not merely four markers on a map, but the actual operating units of ritual, kinship, and community politics.[^1]

📝 **Curator's note:** Name rectification is not swapping one label for another. It is putting the question of "who has the right to name" back into the people's own language and life.

## The Holy Shell Is Not an Ornament

The Hla'alua's most distinctive ritual is the _miatungusu_, the Miatungusu (Holy Shell Festival). The holy shell _takiarʉ_ is regarded as the dwelling place of the shell deity or the ancestral spirit; the shell is therefore not a decoration on clothing, but a medium connecting ancestral spirits, agriculture, the community, and the future. The Council of Indigenous Peoples' ethnic data places it in the context of agricultural rituals: the ceremony prays for peace, bountiful harvests and hunts, and the flourishing of the people.[^1]

The ritual contains an action difficult to replace with modern objects: the holy shell libation. The officiant immerses the holy shell in wine, observing the change in the shell's color. In the people's ritual understanding, the shell turning red symbolizes that the shell deity has drunk deeply, and the ritual has received a response. This is not turning a natural object into a mystical spectacle, but letting a community confirm through shared action that "we are still together."[^1] [^4]

Different sources do not entirely agree on the ritual cycle and its contemporary form. Council of Indigenous Peoples data records that the Miatungusu is held approximately every two to three years. Local cultural publications state that modern public events are often condensed into a single day, divided into segments: welcoming the gods initial rite, pig-sticking rite, inviting out the shell deity, comforting rite, exorcism rite, and communal hunt rite. These differences remind us: rituals have their traditional norms, but also adjust among community conditions, event planning, and public presentation — one cannot treat a single public event as the only version.[^1] [^4]

In March 2025, National Kaohsiung Normal University led a cultural learning journey, placing activities in Hlihlara Community, the Taoyuan Village Four Communities ritual grounds, the Gaojhong Village Milan Community ritual grounds, and Singjhong Elementary School. The activity description calls the Miatungusu a moment of dialogue with ancestral spirits and cohesion of ethnic consciousness. It also lets heritage language learning, community spaces, and school education meet on the same path.[^5]

📝 **Curator's note:** The most moving part of the Miatungusu is not the shell "magically turning red," but that the people must together bring it back to the ritual grounds, together remember how to perform it — only then does culture appear again at the next ritual.

## "Separating Households" Is Not Estrangement

What is most easily overlooked in the name-rectification process is that the Hla'alua and the Tsou are not strangers. Indigenous literature records that on September 8, 2012, the northern and southern Tsou groups held a public hearing at the Alishan Township Office. Present were affections formed through intermarriage, kinship, and long-term exchange, as well as differing judgments on ethnic classification, language loss, and future resources.[^2]

This document preserves a concrete metaphor: the Hla'alua and Kanakanavu hope to be understood as "brothers who have grown into adults and need to separate households," not as separating meaning forgetting each other. This framing did not eliminate conflict, but pulled name rectification back from "contending for more benefits" to a question of cultural lifelines.[^2]

A 2014 PTS News report placed this history in a clear institutional scene: the Executive Yuan meeting approved name rectification, and the Hla'alua and Kanakanavu officially became the 15th and 16th peoples. The report simultaneously noted that the two groups' struggle for name rectification had already lasted six years, and that rectification was expected to become a starting point for preserving culture and language, not a certificate of completion.[^3]

For the Hla'alua, the sharpest tension lies here: kinship relations can continue, but ethnic name, heritage language, and ritual cannot therefore be treated as the same thing. A loving family need not share only one ethnic name. Shared history need not flatten mutual differences.

## How a Language Quietly Disappears in the Community

A 2014 research article notes that the Hla'alua language and Tsou are not mutually intelligible as a single language. The three languages differ in vocabulary, morphology, and syntax. The article also records that in Gaojhong Village, Bunun has long been the dominant community language, while Mandarin expanded its reach through schools and media.[^6]

What researchers heard in the field was not the simple answer "the people don't love their own language," but a series of daily choices: parents speak to children in the language they themselves are more familiar with; children encounter Mandarin at school and on television; cross-ethnic marriage and labor migration bring Bunun or Taiwanese Hokkien. Language thus did not die suddenly one day, but retreated from the household bit by bit in decisions of "which language is more effective today."

Interviewee Amahlu once said: "There are hardly any people left who can speak Hla'alua; if we don't quickly find a way to save it, it will be too late." He proposed starting from nursery school, letting children encounter the heritage language in an immersive environment. The weight of these words lies in their simultaneous acknowledgment of the language's crisis and that a few weekly classes at school may not suffice to bring the language back into life.[^6]

Another interviewee, Aluvai, offers something more like a door: "Very few people here speak Hla'alua kai; even if they do, no one understands.... If it were all Hla'alua people chatting together, maybe they would speak it." The heritage language has not vanished into an abstract "break in transmission"; it still hides in certain people, certain relationships, certain moments that only open when the people gather together.[^6]

📝 **Curator's note:** Language revitalization is not pasting a few words back into textbooks, but letting people believe again — that when I speak in this language, someone will actually receive it.

## Cultural Revitalization Is Not Sealing the Past in a Glass Case

The contemporary form of the Miatungusu shows that cultural preservation is not completed by storing artifacts away. When the Kaohsiung City Cultural Affairs Bureau introduces the Miatungusu, it particularly mentions people returning from elsewhere to the community, cleaning and arranging the meeting hall and ritual grounds. Local publications concretely write the "holy shell libation" and Vilian Community's "shell-tossing for blessings" as understandable ritual segments.[^4]

In 2024, Indigenous Television reported on the National Kaohsiung Normal University Center for Indigenous Knowledge Research holding a two-peoples knowledge academic symposium, covering Hla'alua language syllables and grammar, as well as the process of elders, community members, and experts jointly organizing knowledge. The report mentioned that relevant teams are working toward a Hla'alua knowledge dictionary and ethnic education.[^7]

Here lies an important counter-intuition: name rectification is not the endpoint of cultural preservation; rather, it makes the responsibility of preservation fall more clearly on the people and their partner teams. Museums, schools, research centers, and government can provide resources, but cannot decide for the people which word should stay, which ritual segment can be public, which story should be told by whom.

The Hla'alua name therefore appears not only on the ethnic roster. It is in the place names of the four Taoyuan communities, in a preserved shell, in the moment when elders hesitate over which language to use when speaking to children, and in the actions of returning to the community again and again, relearning the ritual.

## Questions Left for the Next Ritual

If one only looks at June 26, 2014, the Hla'alua story resembles a completed tense: official recognition, ethnic status confirmed, name written into the system. But if the gaze shifts to the ritual grounds and households, the story continues. Language users have decreased, the population is not large, and the ritual must find balance between traditional norms and contemporary public events.[^2] [^3] [^6]

So the significance of the Hla'alua lies not in the number "Taiwan has a 15th people," but in letting us see the true measure of ethnic recognition: whether a group can retain its own linguistic difference, whether it can let its ritual be not only watched but also learned by the next generation, whether it can still say Hla'alua while sharing kinship history with the Tsou.

At the next Miatungusu, the officiant will still face the shell, the wine, and the people. That action looks small, yet answers a large question: when a name was once collected by others, who is still willing to bring it back?
