> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's full moon customs are undergoing a quiet revolution "from ritualistic weight to emotional connection." In the past, elders would shout at the sky to ward off eagles and protect the child through mystical means; today, parents use exquisite celebration gift boxes to transform the joy of new life into a display of cultural taste within their social circles. This article takes you through the evolution of childrearing rituals from an agricultural society to the digital age.

"The owl (eagle) flies to the mountain, may the baby soon become an official; the owl flies high, may the baby be the top scholar."

In the rural Taiwan of the 1950s, on a baby's full month anniversary, elders would carry the child outdoors, tapping the ground with bamboo poles while shouting loudly toward the sky. This ritual, known as _hǎn lì-yú_ (喊利<0xE9><0xB7><0x82>) or _hǎn chī-xiāo_ (喊鴟<0xE9><0xB4><0x9E>), sounds like a boisterous celebration, but for parents of that era, it was more of a "survival negotiation" with nature. In an age of underdeveloped medicine, surviving the first month was considered the baby's first major hurdle in life.

📝 Curator's Note: Every ritual in full moon customs acts like an ancient "life insurance policy," contending against unknown perils.

## Snatching Babies from Eagles: The Mysterious "Calling the Eagle"

In both Hakka and Southern Min traditions, _hǎn lì-yú_ (Hakka: _hamˇ li iquˇ_) is a highly visual custom. Early Taiwanese farming households often raised chickens, and their greatest fear was an eagle (_chī-xiāo_) diving down to snatch chicks. Folk tradition transformed this fear into protection for infants: by shouting loudly to drive away eagles, they symbolically drove away evil spirits and diseases that might harm the child [^1].

This ritual was typically performed by older siblings or elders, who would strike the ground with bamboo poles with loud, vigorous movements. Interestingly, as the incantations evolved, the fear of driving away predators gradually shifted toward blessings. The chants, beyond hoping for the eagle to fly far away, prayed that the child might "become an official," "be a top scholar," or "become a father quickly" [^2]. This shift from "warding off evil" to "praying for blessings" represents one of the warmest qualitative changes in Taiwanese folklore.

## Head Shaving and Strengthening Courage: Building Life's "Defense"

The most significant physical ritual on the full month day is "shaving the fetal hair." This was not merely to encourage thicker hair growth in the future, but also carried a religious significance of "removing the old to welcome the new."

In the shaving basin, elders would place "ten items of tribute," each with its own symbolic meaning:

- **Stone**: To ensure the child's head is "firm" (_tóu-khak tēng_) and that they grow up with courage [^3].
- **Red Eggs and Red Duck Eggs**: To pray for a complexion as fair and rosy as an egg, and a body as strong as a duck egg [^3].
- **Green Onions and Celery**: Homophones for "intelligence" (_cōng-míng_) and "diligence" (_qín-láo_) [^3].
- **Rice**: Symbolizing abundance and sufficient food [^3].
- **Copper Coins**: Symbolizing great wealth and a future free from want [^4].

Particularly in the _zuò dǎn_ (strengthening the courage) ritual, elders would gently roll eggs around the baby and place a round stone in the basin while chanting, "May this stone give you courage, may your head be firm," praying that the baby would be brave and easy to raise [^9]. This reflects the early society's fear of "fright"; only a courageous child could survive in an unpredictable environment.

## The Flow of Celebration Gifts: From Braised Pork Rice to French Cakes

Looking at the modern era, the most striking change in full moon customs lies in "food."

Traditionally, a boy's birth was celebrated with _yóu-fàn_ (braised pork rice) and chicken legs, while a girl's birth was marked by _hóng-guī-guò_ (red tortoise cakes). The braised pork rice symbolized abundance, while the chicken leg represented robust vitality [^5]. However, with the prevalence of refrigerated delivery and changing lifestyles, modern parents' choices are no longer confined to gendered frameworks.

In recent years, more and more new parents have opted for "room-temperature celebration gift boxes" or "charitable cookies" [^6]. Beyond the convenience of delivery, this reflects a modern parental value of linking their child's birth to "social good." French Madeleines and exquisite pound cakes are gradually replacing traditional braised pork rice as the most prominent features on social media.

Modern celebration gifts have also become more diverse; in addition to traditional rice and cakes, practical items like diapers, clothing, wet wipes, and gold jewelry have become popular options. The "red envelope" (monetary gift) market among relatives varies by closeness: for general friends and relatives, it ranges from approximately 600 to 1,200 TWD, while for close friends or relatives, it may reach 1,200 to 3,600 TWD [^10].

📝 Curator's Note: The evolution of celebration gifts is a microcosm of Taiwan's social shift from "family-oriented" to "social-oriented": the box of snacks sent by parents is both an announcement of joy and a display of lifestyle taste.

## Modern Challenges: Negotiating Customs Between Generations

Despite the evolution of customs, generational clashes persist. Many elders insist on the grandeur of "three-day courage rituals" or "full month banquets," while modern parents prefer small-scale gatherings or simple online announcements [^7].

This conflict often occurs in the details of "return gifts." In traditional customs, the bride's natal family prepares the _tóu-wěi-lǐ_ (head-to-toe gift)—clothing and accessories from head to toe, such as hats, socks, baby carriers, gold locks, and gold bracelets—embroidered with the "卍" (swastika) symbol for peace, to be given to the grandchild [^9]. However, modern parents might prefer receiving practical childrearing funds directly [^8]. This shift from "physical gifts" to "practical gifts" is a true reflection of Taiwan's childrearing culture in the digital age.

Furthermore, some traditional taboos are worth noting. For example, because infants have low immunity before their full month, they should not meet guests too early; traditionally, head shaving is avoided during the seventh lunar month; there is even a saying that "one does not send baby carriers for girls" (as _bēi-jīn_ sounds like "precious daughter") to avoid having another girl in the next pregnancy [^9]. These details all reflect the differences and negotiations between generations regarding how to bless a new life.

## Conclusion: The Unchanging Heart of Protection

From shouting at eagles in the sky to sharing photos of celebration cakes on smartphone screens, Taiwan's full moon customs have undergone massive changes in form, but the core remains unchanged—it is the purest form of protection for a new life.

Whether it is the ancient "firm head" or the modern "charitable gift box," these rituals all tell the child: this world is ready to welcome you.
