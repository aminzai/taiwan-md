# Fireworks, Soy Sauce, and the Full Moon: The Evolution and Collective Memory of Taiwan's Mid-Autumn BBQ Tradition

> **30-Second Overview:** In its early stages, the traditional Mid-Autumn Festival in Taiwan focused primarily on moon gazing and eating mooncakes; large-scale outdoor barbecues began appearing in the 1980s. While the most popular theory credits the "One family grills, ten thousand families enjoy" soy sauce commercial, newspaper archives show that barbecuing appeared even earlier—the direct driver of the widespread charcoal fires in 1982 was a surge in domestic sales by Hsinchu-based grill manufacturers facing a slump in exports. The advertisement was responsible for etching this practice into collective memory, but it was not the one to light the fire.

In the traditional landscape of the Sinophone world, the Mid-Autumn Festival on the 15th day of the eighth lunar month has always symbolized reunion, autumn harvests, and longing. Since the Tang and Song dynasties, literati have raised glasses to recite poetry, eat mooncakes, and admire osmanthus under the moonlight, while folklore tells of Chang'e flying to the moon, Wu Gang chopping the laurel tree, and the Jade Rabbit pounding medicine. However, as time progressed into modern Taiwan, the scent of Mid-Autumn no longer consists solely of faint osmanthus or sweet date paste; instead, the air is permeated with the charred aroma of meat sizzling over charcoal, brushed with rich soy sauce. This phenomenon—the tight binding of the Mid-Autumn Festival to outdoor barbecuing—is a cultural mutation woven from industrial circumstances, commercial marketing, and grassroots life.

## I. From Cold Moonlight to Streetside Charcoal: The Modern Turn of a Traditional Festival

The early manifestations of the traditional Mid-Autumn Festival in Taiwan primarily continued the customs of Min-Yue (Hokkien/Cantonese) immigrants, including ancestor worship, the worship of the Earth God (_Tudi Gong_), and eating mooncakes and pomelos. In early Taiwanese society, Mid-Autumn was a relatively quiet festival centered on the family unit. People would set up small tables in courtyards or under arcade walkways (_qilou_), placing pomelos, sliced Cantonese mooncakes, or Taiwanese flaky pastries, as generations sat together gazing at the bright moon and discussing autumn harvests and domestic affairs.

This tranquil festive atmosphere underwent a dramatic structural shift in the 1980s. As Taiwan's economy took off and per capita income rose significantly, leisure activities began to move from inward-looking to outward-facing. The proliferation of private cars and the rise of outdoor recreation provided fertile ground for new customs. In 1981, the _Min Sheng Bao_ reported on camping, barbecuing, and water activities arranged by the Mingde Yule Park on Mid-Autumn nights, demonstrating that the act of lighting fires on Mid-Autumn night was already part of Taiwan's leisure landscape long before any barbecue sauce advertisement became famous [^1].

## II. The Year the Grills Wouldn't Sell: Hsinchu's Industrial Situation and the Advertising Aftermath

Regarding the origins of Taiwan's Mid-Autumn BBQ custom, the most widely circulated theory is that "it was brought about by that Wan Jia Xiang advertisement." This version reverses the actual order of events.

The first true written record dates to October 1982, in a _Min Sheng Bao_ report titled "Barbecuing and Moon Gazing 'Popular' This Year." The report directly explained why streets were filled with charcoal fires that year: "The reason for the popularity of barbecuing is said to be: this Mid-登 Autumn, barbecuing is prevalent because the export of major grills is in a slump, causing manufacturers to shift heavily toward domestic sales, and the Hsinchu area is the headquarters for grill manufacturing."[^1] In other words, what pushed the barbecue grills to Taiwanese doorsteps was a batch of inventory that could not be sold abroad. As a major hub for grill manufacturing, when Hsinchu-based orders dwindled, manufacturers pivoted to domestic markets with massive promotions, causing prices to drop accordingly [^2].

As for the famous slogan everyone can recite, it appeared later than most people think, but also earlier than many realize. It was "late" in terms of Wan Jia Xiang: it was the 1986 television commercial featuring the popular actress Chang Yung-yung that made "One family grills, ten thousand families enjoy" (_Yijia kaorou wanjia xiang_) famous across Taiwan; Jin Lan later joined the fray with intensive barbecue sauce advertising in 1989 [^2]. It was "early" in terms of the slogan's prototype—as early as 1959, newspapers showed that Wei-Chuan soy sauce had already published an ad saying "One family grills, three families enjoy" [^3]. This means the phrase circulated in Taiwan's print media for nearly thirty years before it finally waited for a Mid-Autumn Festival where everyone was barbecuing to become a shared memory for an entire generation.

The advertising war between these two major soy sauce brands should therefore be placed in its true context: it did not invent the custom, but it firmly welded the act of barbecuing—which originally belonged to camping enthusiasts and cheap surplus inventory—to the imagery of Mid-Autumn reunion. At that time, Taiwan's manufacturing and retail sectors also introduced convenient disposable iron meshes, charcoal, and lightweight grills. Before this, barbecuing required complex brick stacking and fire preparation; these convenient, ready-made tools allowed any family to raise a charcoal fire in front of their home, on a rooftop, or even in an alleyway within minutes. The tools, the price, the advertising, and a full moon night that happened to be a holiday worked together to push the Mid-Autumn BBQ into a nationwide cultural ritual.

## III. Collective Memory and Spatial Politics: From Private Domains to Public Corridors

When people across Taiwan light charcoal simultaneously on Mid-Autumn night, the custom transcends mere eating behavior, evolving into a unique form of spatial politics and social ritual.

During the nights around the 15th day of the eighth lunar month each year, Taiwan's urban landscape undergoes a wonderful transformation. Apartment rooftops, residential arcades, and even riverside parks or green spaces opened by local governments—usually quiet—become filled with dancing lights from countless grills. Neighbors who are usually strangers or mere acquaintances find themselves visiting one another amidst thick charcoal smoke and meat aromas, sharing grilled food. This social gathering, which breaks the daily sense of alienation, aligns with the modern urbanite's desire for warm community connections.

However, this nationwide celebration also brings challenges to modern urban management. Monitoring by the Taipei City Environmental Protection Bureau showed that on the night of the Mid-Autumn Festival in 2012 and the night before, carbon monoxide concentrations in the air surged by 1.3 times, and particulate matter increased by fifty to seventy percent. Incomplete combustion of charcoal produces carbon monoxide, fine particulate matter (PM2.5), and polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons; inhalation levels can be significant during long barbecues or in poorly ventilated areas [^4]. As environmental awareness rises, many county and city governments have begun reviewing the impact of large-scale charcoal burning in urban parks or streets, with some urban areas designating specific BBQ zones or advocating for the use of eco-friendly stoves. Nevertheless, the public's passion for Mid-Autumn barbecuing remains undiminished; the custom is now deeply embedded in the DNA of contemporary Taiwanese culture.

## IV. Conclusion: The Continuation of Taiwanese Daily Life Amidst Smoke and Moonlight

From the cold, desolate Palace of Moonlit Frost in ancient poetry to the rising cheers and barbecue aromas in Taiwan's streets and alleys, the Mid-Autumn Festival has achieved a successful cultural localization in Taiwan. It proves that traditional festivals possess an organic quality, capable of adapting to their environment and growing new vitality.

Whenever the autumn wind rises, and the fresh scent of pomelo intertwines with the white smoke of charcoal in the night sky, the autumn full moon celebrated by the people of Taiwan links together precious moments of reunion and human warmth within a busy modern life.
