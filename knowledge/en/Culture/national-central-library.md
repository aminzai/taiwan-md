# National Central Library: How a Library Preserves an Island's Publications into National Memory

> **30-second overview:** The National Central Library's most important work is not fitting the most books into a building, but ensuring that what Taiwan has published, forgotten, and rediscovered all leave verifiable trails. Legal deposit connects publishers' issuance to national documentary preservation; Taiwan Memory turns local gazetteers, old photographs, postcards, ancient land deeds, and biographical materials into searchable digital resources; e-books and theses push preservation challenges into the realms of format, licensing, and long-term management. [^1] [^2] [^3]

In Taiwan, a book is usually treated as a commodity first, reading material second, and only possibly as historical evidence last. A local periodical records a street that has since been renamed; a thesis preserves a debate that has yet to form consensus; a government statistic lets later generations know how a policy described itself at the time. These publications may never become bestsellers, yet they may be the only threads that can reconnect a fragment of social experience in the future.

The National Central Library's mission therefore cannot be measured merely by "how many books were borrowed." It functions more like an adapter that converts private publishing acts into public memory. Authors, publishers, government agencies, universities, and researchers produce texts daily; legal deposit and bibliographic control feed a portion of them into the national preservation system. Preservation is not locking things in a warehouse—it is giving future people the chance to know something existed, to know how it was described, and to know under what conditions they may read it.

## From Publication to National Documentary Heritage

The National Central Library's explanation of legal deposit calls this system an important means for the comprehensive collection of national documentary heritage. Its logic is direct: if preservation relied entirely on librarians' active selection, what the nation ultimately retains might only be works deemed important by the people of that time. Legal deposit shifts responsibility to the publishing end, requiring publishers to provide a copy upon publication; the National Central Library then incorporates it into the public knowledge infrastructure through cataloging, preservation, and service. [^1]

Taiwan's legal deposit system was not stable from the start. The Library's system explanation notes that early regulations were placed in the _Publication Act_. After the _Publication Act_ was abolished in January 1999 (ROC year 88), domestic library information collection was interrupted. Relevant provisions were later incorporated into the _Library Act_, which was promulgated and took effect in January 2001 (ROC year 90). This turning point matters because it shows documentary preservation is not an automatic goodwill of the cultural sector—it requires law to connect publishers, the National Central Library, and the public users. [^1]

The _Library Act_'s deposit scope extends beyond traditional books. The National Central Library explains that books, periodicals, newspapers, audiovisual materials, and electronic media publications all fall within the relevant legal deposit regulations, requiring publishers to deposit one copy each with the National Central Library and the Legislative Yuan Parliamentary Library. After the 2015 (ROC year 104) amendment, the central competent authority was also required to encourage publishers to deposit copies with each national library. [^1] This "one copy" is not a symbolic memento; it is the minimum institutional unit that enables the nation to establish stable bibliographic records and preservation operations.

Publishers may feel deposit is just mailing an extra book. For the national documentary system, the difference lies in whether that book receives a formal bibliographic record, whether researchers can find it, and whether it remains after the publisher closes, the website shuts down, or the market forgets. The value of legal deposit is not that every book will be read, but that it gives "once published" the chance to become a traceable public fact.

## A Library Is Not a Bookshelf

The services listed on the National Central Library's collection resources page already far exceed traditional bookshelves. The Collection Discovery System integrates the Library's own catalog, special collections, and various electronic resources. The National Bibliographic Information Network integrates bibliographic and holding data from the National Central Library and partner libraries. The Periodical Literature Information Network, Taiwan Humanities and Social Sciences Citation Index, National Newspaper Image Database, and Taiwan Doctoral and Master's Theses Knowledge Value-Added System place different forms of research materials into searchable portals. [^3]

The common work of these systems is to establish relationships for data. A book has title, author, publisher, publication date, subject headings, and classification. A thesis has institution, degree, advisor, and keywords. A newspaper image has date and page position. Without these relationships, a collection may exist but resemble paper piled in a warehouse. Cataloging appears to be back-end technology, but it actually determines whether readers can reach the data.

The National Central Library also collects statistical yearbooks, monographs, survey reports, and gazettes published by central and local governments. Such materials rarely enter the market as bestsellers, yet they are primary sources for researching how policies described population, industry, education, transportation, and local governance. They leave behind not just a set of figures, but also how the government in a given era chose to name problems. [^3]

Therefore, the National Central Library's public nature lies not only in "anyone can come read books." It also lies in whether different resources can be connected. A researcher can trace from a local gazetteer to a government statistic, then from newspaper images find the social response of the time. When data are placed in the same knowledge environment, history does not reduce to only the few names that have been repeatedly told.

## Taiwan Memory: Turning Local Experience into Searchable History

"Taiwan Memory" pushes the National Central Library's preservation mission into another kind of space. The system explanation states that Taiwan Memory collects precious materials including books, historical documents, images, and historical figures; after digitization and standardized metadata, it presents Taiwan's history through geographic location, timeline, and thematic facets. [^2]

This design changes how readers enter the data. Traditional collections usually start from title or author; Taiwan Memory also allows entry from place, time, or person. Someone wanting to know how a harbor changed can cross-read old photographs, local documents, and biographical materials. Someone researching a place's families, industries, or streets need not first know a book's complete title. Digital preservation partially transforms entry points that once depended on professional search experience into exploration paths for general users.

The materials collected by Taiwan Memory also illustrate that "Taiwan history" is not constituted solely by central government archives. Japanese-era postcards, old photographs from various places, old land registers, local gazetteers, ancient land deeds, family genealogies, and rubbings of steles and tablets respectively preserve how different people lived, traded, moved, and remembered on this land. They do not necessarily provide a complete story, but they allow local experience to exist without waiting to be written into a grand narrative. [^2]

Digitization does not end with putting paper into a scanner. Every object requires image specifications, file management, metadata, rights determination, and long-term preservation strategy. An old photograph on a page without time, place, source, and subject information shows users only a drifting image. What digital preservation truly adds is not file quantity, but the possibility for objects to be understood, compared, and re-situated in historical context.

The digital object count displayed on the Taiwan Memory homepage continues to fluctuate because the system is still adding, adjusting, and maintaining. This dynamism itself deserves attention. National digital memory is not a finished monument; it is an ongoing task of selecting partners, organizing data, correcting descriptions, and handling rights issues. Today's dataset does not mean tomorrow will not add new local memories, nor does it mean all undigitized materials do not exist.

## Rare Books, Manuscripts, and E-books: Different Times

The National Central Library's special collections include the Rare Book Image Retrieval System, Stone and Bronze Rubbing Database, Union Catalog of Chinese Rare Books and Taiwan Family Genealogies, Special Collections Online Exhibition Hall, Contemporary Celebrity Manuscript Collection System, and General-Purpose Chinese Rare Book Digital Humanities Research Platform. These resources extend preservation from "protecting an ancient book" to "enabling materials to be read by different methods." [^3]

Rare book images allow researchers to compare editions without frequently handling originals. Manuscripts preserve not only the final published text but also deletions, rewrites, marginalia, and the temporal traces left by paper. The union catalog of family genealogies places data scattered across different collecting institutions into a common retrieval framework, giving family history and local history the chance to transcend a single collector's gaze.

E-books make the problem more complex. The National Central Library's English legal deposit FAQ states that formally published e-books also fall within Taiwan's legal deposit policy. This means the object of publication preservation is no longer just fixed paper, but includes file formats, reading software, version changes, digital rights management, and future compatibility. [^4]

Digital publications may lack paper wear, but that does not mean they are inherently permanent. File formats may cease to be supported; platforms may close; licensing terms may restrict reader use; digital rights management may let preservers hold files yet be unable to freely provide access. Research on national digital publications notes that digital technology changes knowledge acquisition and management, also tasking the National Central Library with international coding for digital publications, permanent preservation, and increasing the digital reading population. [^5]

Thus, e-book deposit is not a simple replica of print deposit. Print book preservation centers on space, temperature/humidity, and conservation. E-books must additionally handle verification, format migration, backup, licensing, and reading environments. The real difficulty is not ingesting files into a server, but letting future researchers know how this file was once published, which version is the true issued edition, and under what rights conditions it may be used.

## Preservation and Access Are Not the Same Thing

"Preservation" and "access" are often conflated. The National Central Library preserving a publication means it enters the national documentary system—not that everyone can immediately download the full text. The collection resources page simultaneously lists public query, on-site use, database licensing, e-book service, and thesis full text as different modes. [^3]

This distinction matters to researchers. A bibliographic record tells you a book exists; a holding location tells you where to find it; a digital image may let you read directly; copyright terms determine whether you may download, reproduce, republish, or publicly display. Database search results do not equal full open access to the original. The National Central Library must maintain a boundary between cultural preservation and copyright protection that cannot be replaced by a single slogan.

The legal deposit system delivers publications to the nation for preservation, but does not automatically extinguish the rights of authors, publishers, photographers, translators, and other rights holders. For digital preservation, rights clearance may be slower than scanning. A local periodical may have multiple authors; photographs may come from different photographers; the publisher may have dissolved; heirs may not be easily found. These obstacles ensure a persistent gap between "we have digitized" and "we can freely provide access."

## After a Book Leaves the Market

The moment the National Central Library most deserves to be seen may not be a new book launch, but after a book leaves the market. When a publisher stops printing, a publishing house reorganizes, a website closes, or a local group dissolves, a piece of data may disappear from commercial systems. As long as it was once legally deposited, future researchers may still find an entry point through bibliographic records, holdings, images, or related data.

This does not mean the National Central Library can preserve all voices, nor that preservation equals correct understanding. Cataloging classification may use terminology of a certain era; digital metadata may be constrained by existing fields; local communities may disagree with official names or historical descriptions. Preservation lets materials remain, but cannot decide a single interpretation for society. After preservation, materials still need researchers, local groups, and readers to re-read.

Here lies a politics easily overlooked: what gets published, what gets deposited, what gets digitized, what gets tagged as searchable—these are all choices of the knowledge system. Legal deposit reduces loss risk, but cannot guarantee all small publishers have the capacity to complete deposit. Digital archives expand entry points, but cannot guarantee all local languages, private archives, and unpublished oral memories are included. The National Central Library's public nature lies not in claiming completeness, but in continuously revealing where it remains incomplete.

## A Building, and the Network Beyond It

The National Central Library main building is a photographable structure, but its most important parts are often not in its exterior walls. A Wikimedia Commons photo of the main building records the architecture on Zhongshan South Road in Zhongzheng District, Taipei, released by Xuan Shisheng under CC BY-SA 3.0 Unported. The photo lets readers locate this institution, but cannot alone explain how it works. [^6]

What truly supports the National Central Library is the publisher deposit system, ISBN and CIP, bibliographic databases, periodical indexes, thesis systems, newspaper images, government publications, Taiwan Memory, rare book digitization, thesis alliances, and overseas Sinology resource centers. The building is the entrance; data standards and cooperation networks are the skeleton that enables long-term operation. [^3] [^7]

The National Central Library also connects Taiwan's publications and Chinese-language documents beyond a single Taipei building through the Sinology Research Center, Overseas Taiwan Sinology Resource Centers, and Chinese-language documentary resource cooperation. Such international cooperation is not packaging Taiwan culture as an export commodity, but enabling different collecting institutions to share bibliographic records, images, and research portals. The scale of national-level collection is therefore determined not only by holding volume, but by whether it can mutually discover and mutually calibrate with other institutions.

## Reader Services Are the Exit of Preservation

The preservation system must ultimately return to how readers enter the data. The controlled entrance and card-issuing counter in the National Central Library main building's reading hall appear to be the most mundane places in the architecture, yet they connect the national documentary back-end system to users. Readers first obtain entry qualification, then approach a publication that may have left the market years ago through collection query, bibliographic data, and reading rules. [^3] [^17]

This entrance also reminds us that digitization cannot replace all physical services. Electronic resources allow remote search, but librarians still need to handle version identification, special collections use, copyright restrictions, and data context. The 2025 Library Bulletin's mention of rising digital literacy and dual-track library services does not mean print is about to disappear; it means readers simultaneously need both online discovery and physical verification paths. [^14]

## From Deposit List to Publisher Interface

The latest system change makes legal deposit more like a publisher interface than just shipping a cardboard box. The National Central Library's 2025 Library Bulletin records that the publication deposit system has launched online and integrates with the National New Book Information Network's application service. This means the process publishers face includes data registration, bibliographic information, deposit lists, and subsequent management—deposit is no longer just an administrative supplement after the publishing process ends. [^13] [^14]

The National Central Library's 2025 Library Bulletin also places digital literacy, cross-border Chinese rare book digitization cooperation, and international dissemination of Taiwan academic research results in the same issue. Viewed together, two simultaneous lines emerge in the Library's work: one brings Taiwan's publications into the national system; the other sends already-preserved materials into larger research networks. Preservation and dissemination are not sequential but services that must be designed simultaneously. [^14]

The deposit system's authorization management operation manual, as well as thesis delayed release, replacement, and authorization forms, more clearly expose the institutional seams between preservation and access. Publications can be deposited and cataloged first; full-text access then depends on author, publisher, or thesis authorization terms. This is not system inefficiency, but the result of preservers having to respect different rights states. [^1]

The Taiwan Memory County-City Co-construction and Sharing Symposium brought the issue from the central building to the localities. The National Central Library once invited the Ministry of Culture, National Museum of Taiwan History, twenty-two county/city cultural bureaus, and local libraries to discuss cooperation, explicitly addressing audiovisual resources, original context, and Creative Commons licensing terms. For local digital archives to enter a common platform, they cannot just move files over—they must also explain who provided them, how they are described, and what can be reused. [^15]

## The Next Question for Taiwan's Publishing Memory

Print publishing continues, while e-books, audiovisual, websites, social media posts, and interactive data keep increasing. Traditional legal deposit can answer "who published what when," but needs new tools for rapid version changes, platform dependence, and multimedia content. A print book has a clear publication date; a web publication may update daily. A book can be placed in a stack; an interactive webpage requires preserving programs, databases, and usage environments.

What the National Central Library must preserve in the future may not be just a file, but a system of how content is generated, modified, disseminated, and read. This will raise preservation costs and make copyright and privacy issues harder to avoid. For publishers, deposit should not be understood as ending once the work is handed over. For the National Central Library, collection should not be understood as complete once items are received. Both must negotiate data formats, rights scope, long-term maintenance, and reader use.

The best national library does not seal the past into a neat answer, but leaves enough mutually verifiable evidence for the future. A local gazetteer, a government statistic, an old photograph, a thesis, an e-book file—they may not agree with each other, yet can jointly prove how an era once lived, how it named problems, how it imagined its own future.

Taiwan publishes a new version of itself every day. What the National Central Library truly preserves is not just books already written, but an island's willingness to write down certain experiences and hand them to those not yet born to read anew.
