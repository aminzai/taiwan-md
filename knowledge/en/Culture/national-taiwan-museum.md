# National Taiwan Museum: From Imperial Display Case to Community Co-creation, Who Has the Right to Present Taiwan?

> **30-Second Overview:** In 1908, when the through-country railway was fully operational, the Taiwan Governor's Office established a museum; in 1915, a building with a 30-meter dome, stained glass, and classical portico was completed in Taipei New Park. It once served as a display case for the colonial government to present "Taiwan," and although it changed names and administrators after the war, it has always remained on its original site. Today, the NTMoM is a complex of four historic buildings, and the question has shifted from "what to collect" to "who can decide how to present." (https://www.ntm.gov.tw/News_Content.aspx?n=5651&s=243375)

![Front view of the main building of the National Taiwan Museum, clearly showing the triangular gable, classical portico, stairs, and dome](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/National_Taiwan_Museum_front_view_20070730.jpg)

In 1915, walking into the main hall of the museum in Taipei New Park, one first sees light. The stained glass divides the sunlight into layers of color, below is the marble floor, and above is the towering dome. This building looks like a temple brought from Europe, yet it placed Taiwan's pineapples, bananas, mahamania trees, minerals, and animal specimens in its display cases. From the beginning, the solemnity of the architecture and the local nature of the collection were not separate matters.[^2]

More accurately, this building did not first have a "Taiwan story" and then find a house to hold it. It was first an modernization project of the colonial government, and only later, through changes in regime, restoration, and community cooperation, did it gradually become a public space that Taiwanese people could re-negotiate. **The history of the NTMoM is not just what it preserved, but who named Taiwan, and who gradually reclaimed the right to that name.**

## A Railway Spurred a Museum

In October 1908, the Taiwan Governor's Office established a museum in Taipei. This timing was not accidental: in the same year, the through-country railway was fully operational, connecting north and south into a new administrative and economic axis. The historical articles of Guanghua (Taiwan) state that the original building initially used the lottery office building, named the "Museum affiliated with the Civil Affairs Bureau, Colonial Industry Bureau of the Taiwan Governor's Office." Its predecessor can be traced back to the commodity exhibition hall established in 1899.[^2]

The commodity exhibition hall displayed local customs, handicrafts, and industrial achievements from various parts of Taiwan. In other words, the museum's first task was not simply to preserve the past, but to organize the colony into data that the Japanese government, investors, and the outside world could understand. Camphor, sugar, minerals, plants, and Indigenous artifacts, when placed in the same classification system, made Taiwan simultaneously an island, a market, and an object of study.

Here is a contrast easily obscured by "the historic site is beautiful": while the museum's classical exterior proclaimed civilization and order, the specimens inside produced a knowledge about the colony. Academic research from 2008 referred to this period as the cultural politics of the museum, pointing out that the architecture, system, display classification, and target audience of the Taiwan Governor's Office Museum were all intertwined with how Japan understood Indigenous peoples, Han society, and "modernity" itself. The research also noted that after the post-war Nationalist government took over, the museum may have continued to be a site for the new rulers to present their own cultural order.[^3]

> **Curator's Note:** A museum is not a transparent glass case. It decides what goes in the case, what label to use, and who stands outside to view it; therefore, while a specimen was preserved, a certain mode of classification might also have been preserved.

## The Dome of 1915 Spans Four Names

Construction began on the museum building, commemorating Er-Yu-source Taro and Goto Shinpei, in 1913, and it was completed two years later. It later became the Taiwan Governor's Office Museum building, located between the current 228 Peace Park and the museum road, forming an urban axis with the old Taipei train station. The historical articles of Guanghua (Taiwan) record its establishment in 1908 and completion in 1915. The NTMoM official announcement states that the main building was completed in 1915 and was among the first designated national historic sites.[^2] [^1]

The main building uses reinforced concrete and brick load-bearing walls, with a classical portico on the front facade, and the central dome is about 30 meters high. The hall is supported by Roman Corinthian stone columns, where stained glass, stonework, and plant patterns place European classical vocabulary and Taiwanese products in one space.[^2]

This mixture is not trivial. The building uses classical vocabulary to declare that a colonial government was capable of constructing modern public institutions, while the decorations incorporate Taiwan's fruits, plants, and local materials. It functions both as a monument demonstrating the Empire's capacity for modernization and as a workplace translating Taiwan's natural resources into knowledge and wealth.

### Treating Architecture as the First Exhibit

When approaching the main building, the front facade is what is seen first; only upon entering does one realize that the building itself has arranged a viewing sequence. The portico leads people into the central hall, the dome draws the gaze upward, and the columns separate the central space from the side exhibition halls. This is not mere decoration, but a spatial grammar of public education: first making people feel order and solemnity, then directing attention to the specimens and explanatory texts.[^2]

Looking back at this grammar today, one cannot conclude with just "classical, elegant, magnificent." The building placed Taiwan's products within an external civilized framework, while also leaving the possibility for later audiences to deconstruct that framework. When exhibitions explain architectural materials, restoration traces, and collection acquisition history together, the dome is no longer just a background but becomes historical evidence open to questioning.[^4]

In 1949, the name was changed to "Taiwan Provincial Museum." In 1999, it was changed again to today's "National Taiwan Museum." The name changed, the administrator changed, and the park was renamed from Taipei New Park to 228 Peace Park, but the building remained on site. Guanghua (Taiwan) views this history within the context of post-war regime changes and urban memory: a public building constructed against the backdrop of colonial rule did not disappear when the rulers left; instead, it was taken over, renamed by the next regime, and handed over to later Taiwanese society.[^2]

This also explains why the NTMoM cannot be written solely as a "Japanese-era building." Its brick walls, dome, and collection indeed retain traces of Japanese colonial modernity. However, its use after 1949, its official renaming in the 1990s, and its current exhibition focus are equally part of Taiwan's own history. **The architecture did not hold permanent interpretive authority for any single regime.**

## The Underground Land Bank, Financial History Within Walls

Today, the NTMoM is not just the main building in 228 Peace Park. The NTMoM official announcement lists the four branch museums as important modern buildings constructed during the Japanese era. The predecessor of the Paleontology Museum was the Taipei Branch of the Japan Commercial Bank, which was taken over by the Taiwan Land Bank after the war and later restored and operated under the NTMoM System Plan in 2005.[^1] [^9]

Therefore, the walls of the Paleontology Museum do not only tell stories of fossils. They also preserve the overlapping history of banking, land, colonization finance, and post-war public financial institutions. When a bank building is repurposed as a natural history museum, the building itself becomes a question: how did finance support modernization? How was land measured, mortgaged, and developed? And how does a museum place these seemingly different stories into one city?

The predecessor of the Nanmen Exhibition Hall was the Taipei South Gate Factory of the Taiwan Governor's Office Monopoly Bureau, established in 1899, which mainly handled camphor and opium industries. The official English website and the Nanmen Hall exhibition page state that the factory closed in 1967, and ruins such as the red mansion, the small palace, and water tanks remain. Today's exhibitions look back at the impact of the camphor industry on socio-economics, ethnic culture, and ecological environment using historical objects and local ruins.[^7] [^8]

Including the Nanmen Hall in the NTMoM system means refusing to write camphor merely as "an early successful export commodity of Taiwan." Camphor was an intersection of industry, monopoly systems, forest collection, and global trade. Opium placed colonial governance's finance and bodily control on the same map. The cracks in the historic walls, the scale of the warehouses, and the local displays allow industrial history to be understood beyond a string of years.

The permanent exhibition at the Nanmen Hall further breaks down this history into eight themes: "Definition, Market, Battlefield, Production, Camphor Monopoly, Opium Monopoly, South Gate Factory, Local Display," making commodity history not just about export figures, but also connected to production space, monopoly systems, and historic site restoration.[^8]

The railway park outside the North Gate draws out another line of modernization. Its predecessor was the Railway Department of the Taiwan Governor's Office, which spanned from the Taipei Machinery Bureau in the late Qing dynasty, the Japanese-era Railway Department, to the post-war Taiwan Railways Administration, serving as a long-term hub for Taiwanese railway administration. The NTMoM official announcement lists the railway park as an important site of modern architecture and railway history, placing it alongside natural history, finance, industry, and urban modernity as a microcosm of modern Taiwanese history.[^1]

### Four Buildings, Four Ways to Enter Modern Taiwan's History

| Building            | Historical Entry Point                                        | Questions That Can Be Asked Today                                               |
| :------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------ | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Main Building       | Natural History, Museum System, Colonial Display              | Who classified Taiwan, and what objects entered public knowledge because of it? |
| Paleontology Museum | Japan Commercial Bank, Land Bank, Natural History             | How did finance, land, and biological evolution meet in one building?           |
| Nanmen Hall         | Camphor, Opium Monopoly, Industrial Ruins                     | How are commodities, monopoly systems, forests, and bodies intertwined?         |
| Railway Park        | Machinery Bureau, Railway Administration, Urban Modernization | How did transportation infrastructure change Taiwan's space and daily life?     |

These four entry points were not arbitrarily designed by the museum for tourism, but rather a cross-organization of each building's predecessor and its current exhibition function. The official building data and NTMoM announcements provide their historical lineage, architectural status, and display direction.[^1] [^7] [^9]

> **Curator's Note:** The four buildings are like four entrances: the Main Building enters through natural history, the Paleontology Museum enters through finance, the Nanmen Hall enters through camphor and monopoly, and the Railway Park enters through transportation and administration. Modernization in Taiwan did not suddenly happen inside a white temple; it grew between banks, factories, railway tracks, and specimen cases.

Research on restoration exhibitions in 2022 broke down the display methods of these buildings into three aspects: the life history of the historic site and its socio-cultural context, architectural features, and the restoration process. After observing the Paleontology Museum, Nanmen Hall, and Railway Park, researchers noted that local displays allowed visitors to see the ruins, construction techniques, and equipment originally present in various parts of the buildings, as well as the issues faced by the restoration itself.[^4]

This approach is important because "restoration" is not about making a building look its prettiest year. Restoration exhibitions reintroduce dismantled components, retained construction methods, and traces left by users back into the narrative, turning a historic site from just a finished photograph into a social life history that is still being read.

## Beyond Specimens, Who Can Tell Their Own Story?

The most difficult legacy for the NTMoM to handle may not be the dome or the marble, but the collection and classification itself. Early museums often classified by ethnicity, object function, or physical characteristics, organizing complex societies into rows of easily viewable cabinets. Specialized literature on Indigenous peoples points out a clear shift between this type of classification display common in early 20th-century anthropology and today's emphasis on public participation, online exhibitions, and co-creation.[^6]

This is not to say that all early research was wrong, nor is it to automatically deem today's curation correct. The question is: when an artifact leaves its originating community and enters a capital museum, who holds the authority to explain it? Museums can provide resources for preservation, research, and public display, but if they only let the institution decide what the artifact represents, the exhibition remains structured as "the big museum speaking for others."

The NTMoM's recent practices have begun to address this issue. The 2016 official cooperation announcement of the NTMoM records that since 2009, the two parties have promoted the return of Indigenous cultural artifacts from "big museums to small museums," expanding from one-to-one repatriation exhibitions to involving multiple small museums with two large museums, artifact re-creation, and joint curation. The announcement also recorded cooperation with 6 local Indigenous cultural museums for 8 special exhibitions.[^5]

The focus of this path is not just returning artifacts to tribes for display, but changing who sits at the curatorial table. The official announcement records that collaborations have invited tribal elders into museum archives to inspect and select exhibits, lending original artifacts from collections to local cultural museums, and having the community lead the planning and interpretation of the exhibition content.[^5] These practices transform "repatriation" from a transportation issue into one of knowledge and power rearrangement.

Research reviews also remind us that the representation of Indigenous culture still has shortcomings; cooperation does not mean the problem is solved. Repatriation, dialogue, and co-creation are not pretty endings, but methods requiring the museum to continuously confront interpretive differences, positional knowledge, and institutional resource allocation.

> **Curator's Note:** "Co-creation" is not just inviting local representatives for a meeting and then bringing the decision-making power back into the museum. True co-creation must leave space for questioning, correction, and rejection when artifact selection, naming, narrative sequence, and outcomes return to the community.

From this perspective, the NTMoM's transformation today is not a straight line from "colonialism to progress." The architecture and collections left by the colonial period form the foundation that current research and display can use; these same buildings and collections also carry the classification methods of the society at that time. What the museum can do is not pretend this history does not exist, but to expose it, letting more people know how an artifact was acquired, how a factory operated, and why an object ended up in a case.

## A Century for One Building

The value of the NTMoM does not lie in the title "oldest." What is truly important is that it places Taiwan's natural history, industrial history, financial history, railway history, and ethnic culture within a single walkable urban system. The NTMoM official announcement also views the four buildings as architectural heritage for understanding modern Taiwanese development.[^1]

However, publicness is not achieved merely by attaching the word "National." It requires how exhibitions label origins, how controversies are handled, how originating communities participate, and how inequalities in the formation of the collection are acknowledged. Historic restoration preserves walls, columns, roofs, and techniques; exhibition transformation must preserve the right of different people to question these objects.

### Four Layers of Viewing for Readers

The first layer looks at the exterior: identifying the dome, portico, stained glass, and urban axis. The second layer looks at function: questioning how the museum is connected to railways, banks, camphor factories, and monopoly systems. The third layer looks at collections: confirming the origin, classification, and original life context of objects. The fourth layer looks at power: noting who writes the labels, who can offer different narratives, and whether local communities can participate in the final display decisions.

These four layers also apply to online reading. Images should not just add atmosphere to an article. They should let the reader know which building, which space, and which viewing angle they are seeing. A good caption guides the eye from the exterior back to the history of the building, allowing the reader to understand why the portico, specimens, and display cases before them are worth looking at.

### Supplementary Article Data

This article uses building data, historical articles, and academic research separately: the official page confirms the architecture, dates, and administration; the historical articles supplement urban memory and political lineage; and academic research is used to address colonial displays, restoration methods, and community cooperation limitations. These three sources cannot replace each other; only when combined can they prevent the museum from being written as a single nostalgic spot.[^1] [^3] [^4]

> **Curator's Note:** A photo can only tell the reader "what it looks like"; a good caption must also tell the reader "why to look here right now." The two photos in this article approach the main building from the perspective of exterior architecture and natural history display, allowing the reader to see how the NTMoM preserves both space and knowledge classification simultaneously.

Therefore, next time you walk before the museum road, do not just take a picture in front of the dome. Ask three small questions first: Who collected this object? In whose life did it originally exist? Who can decide how it should be explained today? These three questions turn the museum from a beautiful historic site into an operating democratic site.

The museum of 1908 organized Taiwan as an object for the Empire to view. The NTMoM of 2026 must allow Taiwanese society to collectively decide how it is viewed. **Preserving this museum is not just preserving a 1915 building, but preserving a place where society continuously rewrites its own story.**
