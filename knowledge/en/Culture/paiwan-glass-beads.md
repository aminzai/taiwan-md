> **30-Second Overview:** _Qata_, the Paiwan glass beads, were once a medium for class, marriage, belief, and family memory; the pattern of a single bead could reveal the wearer's status and who they were responsible to. In 1976, Umas Zingrur in Sandimen, Pingtung, recreated modern _qata_ through five years of experimentation; in 1983, Remereman Taruzaljung (Shi Xiuju) established the Dragonfly Workshop, reintroducing this once-interrupted craft into daily life. This is not a linear story of "ancient techniques being revived," but rather a group of people deciding: which knowledge can be seen, produced, and passed down to the next generation.

At the center of a string of Paiwan glass beads might be a rainbow-patterned **Mulimulitan**. It is translated as a "noble bead," but "nobility" does not mean sitting in a high place receiving service from others. In traditional narratives, the rainbow pattern is associated with the status of tribal leaders. The leader was responsible for caring for the weak and sick within the tribe; under the system of primogeniture, the beads were passed to the next heir, along with the duty of care[^1].

Therefore, what makes Paiwan glass beads most memorable is not how bright or rare they are, nor the price they fetch in tourist shops. They write a person's status on their chest and hang an invisible obligation onto that status. This connection—"Object—System—Responsibility"—is precisely why Taiwan.md has not yet introduced them: they are not merely decorative crafts, but a form of wearable social memory.

## Asking First About One Bead: Who Are You, and Whom Must You Care For?

The Paiwan call glass beads **qata**. Official cultural heritage documentation places them within the context of a traditional hierarchical society: they were used to display the class status of noble families and individuals, and served as important evidence for wealth inheritance. They involve religious belief, worldview, ethics, identity, moral standards, tribal structure, and aesthetics[^2]. Academic research analyzes the cultural imagery of _qata_ through interviews with artisans, tribal residents, and consumers, dividing it into two layers: "implicit" and "explicit." The implicit includes cultural identity and inheritance, class status, religious guardianship, and formal beauty. The explicit relates to shape, color, and motifs[^3].

This division is useful because it reminds us not to treat the patterns as a fixed encyclopedia. What is seen on the outside of the beads are colors and lines; what is connected inside is how a certain tribe understands ability, bloodline, marriage, and the cosmos. Exhibition materials from the National Museum of Taiwan also record that the tribes believed glass beads originated from dragonfly eyes or were offerings from the Sun God. Each bead has its own name, story, and symbolic meaning[^4].

These stories are not necessarily identical across all tribes. In an interview with Paiwan cultural worker Lin Xiuhui (Kedrekedr Maljaljaves) by _Transmitting Culture Online_, it was pointed out that the same pattern might have different meanings in different regions. The importance of the beads lies in the fact that descendants can trace the path of knowledge transmission through legends, songs, and ways of pairing them[^1]. Turning _qata_ into a product manual where "each pattern has only one standard answer" would flatten its most vital aspect.

## Mulimulitan: The Steam from a Pot

The legend of **Mulimulitan**, recorded by _Transmitting Culture Online_, does not stop at the abstract metaphor of "the rainbow is beautiful." The rainbow pattern was interpreted as the colored light band formed by the steam rising from a pot in a traditional leader's house intersecting with the light outside the window. It thus connects heaven and earth, and it connects to the caretaking work that the leader must perform daily: bringing those who need care in the tribe home so they can eat and have a place to live[^1].

This detail changes how we view "noble beads." They are not about painting power beautifully; they are about recording the cost of power. When the beads were passed down under the system of primogeniture, what was handed over was not just a precious item. It was the family's status, the tribe's responsibility, and a notification: "You must take over now." The organization of Paiwan material culture by the Municipal Office of Jinfeng Township, Taitung County, also points out that glass beads, ancient pottery jars, and bronze knives are the Three Treasures of Paiwan, linked to the inheritance rights of the chief's family and marriage dowries[^5].

Another type of pattern writes ability more directly. _Transmitting Culture Online_ records that the butterfly motif can point to agility and quick response in a crisis. Green insect beads are associated with agricultural talent, representing those who can help the tribe avoid famine[^1]. A report by Taiwan Today also preserved Umas Zingrur's explanations of different bead patterns: the warrior bead (_mananigai_) is linked to the centipede guardian image, and the agile bead (_puka_) comes from the butterfly; **Mulimulitan** is an essential bead in noble wedding dowries[^6].

## Where Do Ancient Beads Come From? No Single Answer Can Be Casually Written Down

When discussing Paiwan glass beads today, it is easy to fall into a neat but dangerous narrative: they were made in the tribes since ancient times, disappeared at one point, and are now being revived. Official cultural heritage documentation actually retains a more cautious statement: the ancient beads may not have been self-made by the early Paiwan, but acquired through transmission or trade across different time periods. Today's glass beads include "traditional ancient beads" passed down from ancestors, as well as "modern glass beads" independently developed by the tribes[^2]. The collection guide of the Museum of Prehistoric Culture also points out that glass beads entered Taiwan through various exchange channels and became items exchanged in Paiwan society for inheritance, marriage dowries, and displaying family status[^9].

This distinction is important. The material, origin, and circulation path of ancient beads are issues faced jointly by archaeology, ethnology, and tribal memory. Modern _qata_, on the other hand, is a technique rediscovered by 20th-century artisans who had lost production records, piecing it together from objects, materials, and experimentation. A cultural research paper from National Yunlin University of Science and Technology compiled the uses of ancient glass beads from Paiwan and Rukai tribes: inheritance, marriage dowries, tribal alliances, funerals, and important ceremonies. It also points out that new usage sequences, such as gifting beads at birth, school enrollment, and coming-of-age ceremonies, have begun to appear in modern tribes[^7].

Thus, the history of _qata_ has two threads. One thread asks how ancient beads reached Taiwan, and the other asks how contemporary tribes keep answering "what the beads should carry." The first thread requires archaeological and cross-regional trade evidence and cannot be replaced by legend. The second thread occurs in workshops, weddings, exhibitions, and daily tribal life, and must listen to those who are still making and wearing them.

## In 1972, Umas Saw the Beads Left by His Mother

Umas Zingrur (Omaz Zingrur), born in a crafting family in Sandimen, Pingtung, in 1946. Taiwan Today reported that in 1972, he visited collector Chang Muyang in Taipei. Although he initially intended to make Paiwan knife-related products, his attention shifted to glass beads after seeing a batch of colored ornaments[^6]. Among the Three Treasures of Paiwan, bronze knives, pottery jars, and glass beads each have their own place. For Umas, the most difficult part about the beads was: everyone knew they were important, but no one could tell him completely how to make them.

He first secretly studied the beads treasured by his mother, mistakenly thinking they were ceramics, and tried to make his own ceramic beads. The glaze was wrong, so he went to Hsinchu, famous for glass, tracing from a glass factory to a factory making glass animals, only realizing that glass could come in different colors. Next, with no ready-made textbooks, he could only incrementally experiment with the size of the torch, the intensity of the flame, and how to remove metal wires from the beads after cooling[^6].

The official cultural heritage website lists 1976 as the key year for his development of modern glass bead techniques. After about five years of experimentation and fieldwork, Umas finally made traditional-style glass beads and began promoting sales and job opportunities in Sandimen[^2][^6]. This year has two meanings: it is the year the technique stood up again, and it marked the beginning of an experiment to see if "knowledge without a complete operation manual" could be recovered through objects, memory, and patience.

## In 1983, Shi Xiuju Kept the Fire Burning in the Workshop

Shi Xiuju's story moved revitalization from that of a craft researcher to a workshop capable of accommodating daily life. Her Paiwan name is Remereman Taruzaljung; cultural ministry records state she was born in Pingtung in 1956 and began teaching _qata_ making in 1983, establishing the Dragonfly Workshop[^8]. Exhibition materials from the National Museum of Taiwan also point out that after a long period where glass bead craftsmanship had disappeared, she dedicated herself to researching firing techniques and established her workshop in 1983[^4].

The English profile on the Ministry of Culture provides a very concrete scene: Shi Xiuju was an elementary school teacher, and the workshop later employed over thirty Paiwan women; she said this allowed the women to care for children and families while earning income, making the workshop like a large family[^8]. This statement means that "preserving traditional crafts" is no longer just a museum display case issue. The fire must be kept alive, and there must be a work arrangement so that it can remain close in daily life.

After the 2008 film _Seven Seas_ was released, the plot—which linked beads to the life stories of characters—suddenly brought a flood of orders to the Dragonfly Workshop. The Ministry of Culture records that Shi Xiuju and her team worked late into the night; she attributed this massive experience to the team and reminded people: while fame can promote _qata_ across Taiwan, it cannot automatically solve issues of inheritance, quality, and livelihood for the workshop[^8].

## Cultural Revitalization Is Not Just Copying the Ancient

Lin Xiuhui (Kedrekedr Maljaljaves) followed a different path. She worked as a designer in the city before confronting her Paiwan identity after her father's passing; she returned to the tribe, learning from Umas and Jiang Yalei, documenting the origin and knowledge context of the beads through fieldwork[^1]. In an interview with _Transmitting Culture Online_, she discussed that the inheritance of traditional crafts cannot rely solely on selling finished products; it must also allow the makers to understand the cultural teachings behind the patterns. The Kata Cultural Workshop thus handles training, cultural learning, and income sources simultaneously[^1].

This is where the most contemporary contradiction in _qata_ appears: it must be made into a livelihood object, yet it cannot only consist of easily recognizable, easy-to-buy patterns. If a rainbow bead is detached from the system of leadership, caretaking responsibility, and family inheritance, leaving only the word "noble," the product may still be beautiful, but the knowledge has lost a layer. Conversely, if every contemporary creator is required to copy ancient beads, the craft will lose its ability to respond to contemporary life.

A 2008 study divided the cultural imagery of _qata_ into aspects such as identity, inheritance, class, belief, and form, which perfectly illustrates why it cannot be summarized merely as "Indigenous style." Different tribes, families, and artisans in Paiwan may have different explanations for the patterns; research and exhibition should articulate these differences and leave uncertainty, rather than inventing an overly neat myth for all beads.

## Fire, Ash, and a Name That Can Be Passed On

The National Cultural Heritage website registered _qata_ as a traditional craft in 2018 and 2023, listing Jiang Yalei and the late Lei Ci as preservers. The reasons for registration include the artistic value of the works, the close connection between glass beads and Paiwan culture, and the knowledge and skills required to make _qata_[^2]. This documentation places an item often seen merely as a "beautiful ornament" back into its complete scale as cultural heritage: what is preserved is not a collection of beads, but the ability to identify materials, control the fire, understand the patterns, and use them correctly within social relationships.

Lin Xiuhui experimented with ancient kiln firing methods based on fieldwork learning, using natural fire sources, ash coating, and slow cooling; the significance of these actions is not to prove that all ancient beads were necessarily made by the same method, but to allow the tribes to regain the right to ask questions and practice[^1]. Umas started from his mother's beads, Shi Xiuju kept the fire burning in the workshop, and Lin Xiuhui recorded the elders' sayings, patterns, and production experience: three different paths, yet all addressing the same rupture.

What Paiwan glass beads ultimately leave behind is not a conclusion that "the ancient was beautiful." It leaves a more difficult, and more practical, question: when a bead is passed to the next person, what is being handed over—a product, a status, or the ability to care for another?

If one day we see _qata_ at a wedding or tribal celebration, do not immediately ask how much it is worth. First, imagine that rainbow-patterned bead coming from the steam rising from a pot; imagine it shining on someone's chest, and also imagine the unspoken word beneath the light: **By receiving this bead, you are also receiving the life of the next person.**

## Reading the Image: Don't Just Look at the Color, See How It Is Used

When looking at a _qata_, the first thing one usually notices is the color, transparency, stringing method, and pattern; but the cultural significance is often not limited to the beads themselves. Official cultural heritage documentation places _qata_ at the intersection of hierarchy, belief, ethics, worldview, and wealth inheritance, while research reminds us that shape, color, and motifs are only the "explicit" layer, which must be understood together with the "implicit" layers such as identity, inheritance, class, and guardianship[^2][^3]. Therefore, a single photograph cannot independently answer "what this bead represents"; one also needs to know who made it, who wore it, in what context it appeared, and which tribe and family lineage the explainer belongs to.

This is also the limitation of images in cultural articles. The three photos in this text can help readers distinguish the visual differences between multi-strand ornaments, chest pieces, and the Paiwan Treasures, but they cannot be treated as a pattern dictionary, nor can the wearer's class or family status be inferred from appearance alone. Museum collection data indicates that glass beads have different uses in exchange, weddings, inheritance, and social relationships; images should be an entry point into the context, not a conclusion provided by the tribes[^9].

## The Technique of Revitalization is Also Relational Work

The revitalization of modern _qata_ is not just about finding recipes or copying ancient appearances. Umas Zingrur gradually pieced together fire, materials, and cooling methods starting from the beads left by his mother, glass factories, and repeated experiments; Shi Xiuju put the production techniques into a workshop, allowing women to work, care for families, and pass down crafts in the same living space[^6][^8]. These experiences show that preserving crafts requires not just "knowing how to do it," but also having time, tools, income, learning relationships, and a context for handover.

Lin Xiuhui's work pushed the question further: revitalization must simultaneously address production methods, pattern stories, elder knowledge, tribal differences, and contemporary livelihoods[^1]. This prevents _qata_ from being locked into two extremes: "only reviving the ancient" or "just making it into a product." The truly difficult work is enabling creators to create while respecting the context, and allowing readers to know that what they are seeing is a cultural object with origins, relationships, and responsibilities.

## How Does a Bead Enter Contemporary Life?

Contemporary _qata_ has not stopped changing just because it became cultural heritage. Research data mentions that in addition to inheritance, marriage, alliances, funerals, and important ceremonies, modern tribes have new usage sequences such as gifting beads at birth, school enrollment, and coming-of-age ceremonies[^7]. These new uses do not mean the tradition is diluted; a more accurate way of speaking is that the tribes continuously decide which relationships are worth remembering with beads, and how to let the objects respond to today's life stages.

Therefore, the best conclusion when introducing Paiwan glass beads should not just be "the ancient technique was successfully preserved." A more complete conclusion is: _qata_ is still being made, worn, gifted, researched, and reinterpreted, and every use may reaffirm a family relationship, a tribal position, or a responsibility to others. This continuously unfolding cultural practice is why it is more than just a museum object.
