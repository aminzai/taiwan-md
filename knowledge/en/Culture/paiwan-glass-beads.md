> **30-second overview:** Paiwan glass beads, _qata_, once served as carriers of social hierarchy, marriage, faith, and family memory. The pattern on a single bead can reveal the wearer’s status and the responsibilities they must uphold for others. In 1976, Umas Zingrur in Sandimen, Pingtung, spent five years experimenting to recreate modern glass beads; in 1983, Hsiao Hsiu-chu established Qingting Yazhu, bringing this once-broken craft back into daily life. This is not a linear story of “ancient skills coming back to life,” but rather a group of people redefining which knowledge can be seen, made, and passed on to the next generation.

In the center of a Paiwan glass bead strand, there may be a **Mulimulitan** with a rainbow pattern. Translated as the “Beast of Nobility,” the term “noble” does not mean sitting high above and receiving service from others. In traditional narratives, the rainbow pattern is linked to the status of tribal leaders. Leaders must care for the young, weak, elderly, and sick in the tribe; under the primogeniture system, the beads are passed to the next generation, along with the responsibility of care[^1].

Thus, the most memorable aspect of Paiwan glass beads is not how bright or rare they are, or what price they can fetch in tourist shops. They inscribe a person’s identity on their chest and hang an invisible duty upon that identity. This connection between “object–system–responsibility” is precisely why Taiwan.md has not yet introduced it: it is not merely decorative craft, but a social memory worn on the body.

## A bead first asks: Who are you, and who must you care for?

The Paiwan call glass beads **qata**. Official cultural heritage data places it in the context of a traditional hierarchical society: it was once used to highlight the status of noble families and individuals, and served as an important proof of wealth inheritance. It involves religious faith, views on life, ethics, identity, character standards, ethnic structure, and aesthetics[^2]. Academic research analyzes the cultural imagery of _qata_ through interviews with artisans, tribal residents, and consumers, dividing it into “implicit” and “explicit” layers: the implicit includes cultural identity and inheritance, status, spiritual protection, and formal beauty. The explicit focuses on shape, color, and totems[^3].

This division is useful because it reminds us not to treat patterns as a fixed field guide. What is seen on the outside are colors and lines; what is connected inside is how a specific tribe understands ability, bloodline, marriage, and the cosmos. Exhibition data compiled by the National Taiwan Museum also records that tribe members believe glass beads originated from the eyes of dragonflies emerging from their pupae, or were gifts from the Sun God to the people. Each bead has its own name, story, and symbolic meaning[^4].

These stories may not be exactly the same across all tribes. In an interview with Paiwan cultural worker Lin Hsiu-hui, _Chuangyi Online_ pointed out that the same pattern may have different meanings in different regions. The importance of the beads lies precisely in the fact that descendants can trace the path of knowledge transmission through legends, songs, and wearing methods[^1]. Turning _qata_ into a “product manual” where each pattern has only one standard answer would flatten its most vital aspects.

## Mulimulitan: Behind the rainbow pattern is the steam from a pot of rice

The Mulimulitan legend recorded by _Chuangyi Online_ does not stop at the abstract metaphor of “rainbows are beautiful.” The rainbow pattern is interpreted as the colorful light bands formed by the intersection of steam rising when the lid of a pot is lifted in a traditional leader’s house and the light from the window. It thus connects heaven and earth, as well as the care work the leader must undertake daily: bringing those in the tribe who need care into the home, ensuring they have food and shelter[^1].

This detail changes how we view the “noble bead.” It does not merely paint power beautifully; it records the cost of power. When the beads are handed over under the primogeniture system, what is being handed over is not just a precious object. It is family status, tribal responsibility, and a notice that says, “You must now take over.” The compilation of Paiwan material culture by the Jinfeng Township Office in Taitung County similarly points out that glass beads, ancient pottery jars, and bronze knives are the “Three Treasures of the Paiwan,” linked to the inheritance rights of chieftain families and wedding betrothal gifts[^5].

Another type of pattern writes ability more directly. _Chuangyi Online_ records that the swallowtail butterfly pattern can point to agility and the ability to react quickly in crises. The green insect bead is linked to agricultural talent, representing those who can help the tribe avoid famine[^1]. A report from _Taiwan Today_ also preserves Umas Zingrur’s explanations of different bead patterns: the warrior bead _mananigai_ is linked to the protection of the hundred-step snake, the agility bead _puka_ is derived from the swallowtail butterfly, and _Mulimulitan_ is an indispensable bead in noble wedding betrothal gifts[^6].

## Where ancient beads come from: No answer can be written carelessly

When discussing Paiwan glass beads today, it is easy to fall into a neat but dangerous story: they were made in the tribe since ancient times, disappeared for a while, and have now been restored. Official cultural heritage data actually retains a more cautious statement: ancient beads may not have been made by the Paiwan in early times, but were acquired through dissemination or trade in different spaces and times. The glass beads referred to today include “traditional ancient beads” passed down by ancestors, as well as “modern glass beads” developed by tribe members themselves[^2]. The preface to the collection guide of the National Museum of Prehistory also points out that glass beads entered Taiwan through different exchange channels and became objects of exchange in Paiwan society for passing down families, wedding betrothals, and displaying family status[^9].

This distinction is important. The material, origin, and circulation paths of ancient beads are issues faced jointly by archaeology, ethnology, and tribal memory. Modern _qata_ is a technology that 20th-century artisans摸索 (groped for) from objects, materials, and experiments after losing the records of how to make them. A cultural research article from National Yunlin University of Science and Technology compiled the uses of ancient glass beads for the Paiwan and Rukai tribes: passing down families, marriage betrothals, tribal alliances, funerals, and important rituals. It also points out that modern tribes have begun to see new usage sequences, such as giving beads for births, school entry, and coming-of-age ceremonies[^7].

Therefore, the history of _qata_ has two lines. One line asks how ancient beads reached Taiwan; the other asks how modern tribe members keep the answer to “what beads should carry” alive. The first line requires archaeological and cross-regional trade evidence and cannot be replaced by legends. The second line occurs in studios, weddings, exhibitions, and tribal daily life, requiring us to listen to those who are still making and wearing them.

## In 1972, Umas saw the beads left by his mother

Umas Zingrur (Paiwan name: Omaz Zingrur) was born in 1946 into a craft family in Sandimen, Pingtung. A _Taiwan Today_ report states that in 1972, he visited collector Chang Mu-yang in Taipei. Originally intending to make Paiwan knife-related products, he shifted his focus to glass beads after seeing a batch of colorful bead ornaments[^6]. Among the Three Treasures of the Paiwan, bronze knives, pottery jars, and glass beads each have their place. For Umas, the hardest part of the beads was that everyone knew they were important, but no one could fully tell him how to make them.

He first secretly studied the beads his mother had珍藏 (treasured), mistaking them for pottery products and trying to make his own pottery beads. The glaze color was wrong, so he went to Hsinchu, famous for glass, touching every glass factory and then factories making glass animals, only to realize that glass could have different colors. Next, there were no ready-made textbooks; he could only test out the size of the blowtorch, flame intensity, and how to remove metal wires from the beads after cooling[^6].

The official cultural heritage website lists 1976 as the key year for his research and development of modern glass bead techniques. After about five years of experimentation and field research, Umas finally made glass beads in the traditional style and began promoting sales and job opportunities in Sandimen[^2][^6]. This year thus has two meanings: it is the year the technology stood up again, and the year the experiment began on “whether a knowledge system that left no complete manual could be reconnected through objects, memory, and patience.”

## In 1983, Hsiao Hsiu-chu kept the fire in the studio

Hsiao Hsiu-chu’s story pushes revitalization from that of a single craft researcher to a studio that can accommodate daily life. Her Paiwan name is Remereman Taruzaljung. Ministry of Culture data records that she was born in 1956 in Pingtung, began teaching _qata_ making in 1983, and established the Qingting Yazhu bead art studio[^8]. Exhibition data from the National Taiwan Museum points out that after the glass bead craft had disappeared for a long time, she devoted herself to researching firing techniques and established the studio in 1983[^4].

The Ministry of Culture’s English profile page provides a very specific scene: Hsiao Hsiu-chu was formerly an elementary school teacher; the studio later employed more than thirty Paiwan women. She said this allowed women to care for children and family while earning income, making the studio like a big family[^8]. This sentence makes “traditional craft preservation” no longer just a museum display case issue. For the fire to remain, there must also be an work arrangement that allows people to stay close to it in daily life.

After the 2008 film _Cape No. 7_ was released, the plotline using beads to correspond to characters’ life stories caused Qingting Yazhu to suddenly receive a large influx of orders. The Ministry of Culture records that Hsiao Hsiu-chu and her team worked until late at night; she attributed this surge in experience to the team, while reminding people that fame can push _qata_ across Taiwan, but will not automatically solve the studio’s issues of inheritance, quality, and livelihood[^8].

## Cultural revitalization is not about copying the past back into existence

Lin Hsiu-hui (Paiwan name: Kedrekedr Maljaljaves) took a different path. She worked in design in the city, but after her father passed away, she re-encountered her Paiwan identity; she returned to the tribe, learned from Umas and Jiang Ya-lei, and recorded the sources and cultural context of the beads through field research[^1]. In her interview with _Chuangyi Online_, she discussed that the inheritance of traditional crafts cannot rely solely on selling finished products; it must also allow makers to understand the cultural teachings behind the bead patterns. The Katá Cultural Studio thus simultaneously handles training, cultural learning, and income sources[^1].

Here arises the most contemporary contradiction of _qata_: it must be made into an object that can sustain a livelihood, but cannot be reduced to only quickly recognizable, easy-to-purchase patterns. If a rainbow bead is extracted from the chieftain system, care responsibilities, and family handover, leaving only the word “noble,” the commodity is still beautiful, but the knowledge has lost a layer. Conversely, if every contemporary creator is required to only copy ancient beads, the craft will also lose the ability to respond to contemporary life.

A 2008 study divided the cultural imagery of _qata_ into aspects of identity, inheritance, status, faith, and form, precisely explaining why it cannot be generalized as merely “indigenous style”[^3]. Different Paiwan tribes, different families, and different artisans may have different interpretations of patterns; research and exhibitions should state the differences and leave the uncertainty, rather than weaving an overly neat myth for all the beads.

## Fire, wood ash, and a name that can be handed over

The National Cultural Heritage Network announced the registration of _qata_ as a traditional craft in 2018 and 2023. The preservers listed include Jiang Ya-lei and the late Lei Ci. The reasons for registration include the artistic value of the works, the tight integration of glass beads with Paiwan culture, and the knowledge and skills required to make _qata_[^2]. This data places an object often seen as a “pretty accessory” back into the complete scale of cultural heritage: what is preserved is not a batch of beads, but the ability to identify materials, control fire, understand patterns, and use them correctly in social relationships.

Lin Hsiu-hui once restored ancient kiln firing methods based on field learning, experimenting with natural fire sources, wood ash coverage, and slow cooling methods. The meaning of these actions lies not in proving that all ancient beads were necessarily made by the same method, but in allowing tribe members to repossess the right to ask questions and practice. Umas started from his mother’s beads, Hsiao Hsiu-chu kept the fire in the studio, and Lin Hsiu-hui recorded the words of the elders, patterns, and production experience: three different paths, yet all dealing with the same rupture.

What the Paiwan glass beads finally leave behind is not a conclusion of “tradition is beautiful.” What it leaves behind is a harder, more practical question: when a bead is handed to the next person, what is actually being handed over: a commodity, an identity, or the ability to care for others?

If one day we see a strand of _qata_ at a wedding or tribal celebration, do not rush to ask how much it is worth. First imagine that the rainbow bead comes from the steam rising when the pot lid is lifted, imagine it shining on someone’s chest, and imagine the words below the light that are not written on the label: **You receive this bead, and you also receive the next person’s life.**

## Reading images: Do not just look at colors, but also at how they are used

When looking at a _qata_, the first thing usually noticed is color, transparency, stringing method, and pattern; but cultural meaning often lies not just in the bead itself. Official cultural heritage data places _qata_ at the intersection of hierarchy, faith, ethics, views on life, and wealth inheritance. Research reminds us that shape, color, and totems are only the “explicit” layer; they must be understood together with the “implicit” layers of identity, inheritance, status, and protection[^2][^3]. Therefore, a single photo cannot independently answer “what does this bead represent”; one also needs to know who made it, who wears it, in what context it appears, and which tribal and family context the person explaining it comes from.

This is also the limitation of images in cultural articles. The three photos in this article can help readers distinguish the visual differences between multi-strand bead ornaments, chest ornaments, and the Three Treasures of the Paiwan, but photos cannot be treated as pattern dictionaries, nor can the wearer’s status or family identity be inferred from appearance. Museum collection data points out that glass beads have different uses in exchange, weddings, passing down families, and social relationships; images should be an entry point into context, not a conclusion replacing tribe members in explaining culture[^9].

## The technology of revitalization is also a form of relational work

The revitalization of modern _qata_ is not just about finding formulas or copying the appearance of ancient beads. Umas Zingrur started from the beads left by his mother, glass factories, and repeated experiments, gradually groping for fire, materials, and cooling methods; Hsiao Hsiu-chu placed the production technology into the studio, allowing women’s work, family care, and craft inheritance to occur in the same life field[^6][^8]. These experiences show that craft preservation requires not only “knowing how to do it,” but also having time, tools, income, learning relationships, and a field for handover.

Lin Hsiu-hui’s work pushes the question one step further: revitalization must simultaneously handle production methods, pattern stories, elder knowledge, tribal differences, and contemporary livelihoods[^1]. This prevents _qata_ from being locked in two extremes: “only restoring the ancient” or “just making it a commodity.” The truly difficult work is allowing creators to create while respecting context, and allowing readers to know they are seeing a cultural object with source, relationships, and responsibility.

## How a bead enters contemporary life

Contemporary _qata_ has not stopped changing because it became a cultural heritage. Research data mentions that besides passing down families, marriage, alliances, funerals, and important rituals, modern tribes have also seen new usage sequences, such as giving beads for births, school entry, and coming-of-age ceremonies[^7]. These new uses do not represent the dilution of tradition; a more precise statement is that tribe members continue to decide which relationships are worth remembering with beads, and how to make objects respond to today’s life stages.

Therefore, when introducing Paiwan glass beads, the best ending should not be merely “ancient skills successfully preserved.” A more complete ending is: _qata_ is still being made, worn, gifted, researched, and reinterpreted, and every use may reconfirm a family relationship, a tribal position, or a responsibility to others. This ongoing cultural practice is precisely why it is not just a museum object.
