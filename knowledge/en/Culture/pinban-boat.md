> **30-Second Overview:** The Lanyu Tatara is not merely a vessel made by joining planks. It requires knowledge of which wood species are suitable for the keel, which materials provide buoyancy, how wooden pegs swell in water, when to carve the portholes, wave patterns, and human figures, and which ceremony officially integrates the new boat into the relationship with the tribe and the sea. The significance of the Tatara lies not just in its ability to carry people out to sea, but because it assembles forest resources, ancestral memory, fishing group order, flying fish season, and navigational judgment into a mobile body of knowledge. In June 2026, over 200 people from six communities built the large 20-person vessel "Ovayan Golden Friendship" using traditional methods to cross the Bashi Channel and reach Badang Island in the Philippines, restarting an ancient maritime route that had been interrupted for 300 years. This body of knowledge was brought back into international view not as a display piece, but through actual "voyaging."

## Deconstructing "Plank Boat"

The Chinese name "plank boat" easily leads one to think of the technique: one plank joined to another, forming a slender hull. However, understanding it only by the number of planks fails to capture its role as the core of Yami marine life. The National Cultural Heritage website registers large Amami (Yami) boats in launching ceremonies as folk cultural heritage, illustrating the interconnectedness of large boat construction, fishing group organization, and flying fish season rituals. The Tatara is not an independent craft item; it is an object situated within a system of seasonal, kinship, labor, and religious order[^1].

There is more than one boat type. Cultural heritage data distinguishes between the large _Chinurikuran_ and the small _Tatala_: the large boats were used by fishing group crews and could carry up to 10 people. The smaller _Tatala_ served individuals or families for sea trips, carrying a maximum of 3 people. Collections described in the National Cultural Memory Bank refer to the Tatara, recording differences in plank quantity, hull structure, dyes, and carvings between large and small boats, noting that the ten-person large boat was jointly owned by several families, while the single-person small boat was essential for every family[^2]. The collection descriptions from the National Museum of Indigenous Cultures also state that the large boat is composed of 27 planks, and the small boat of 21 planks, with different woods chosen for each plank based on its position and function in the hull[^13]. Different sources use names like _Tatala_, _Tatara_, or _Chinurikuran_, reminding us not to treat one romanization as the sole answer to an entire culture. These variations may reflect differences in boat type, linguistic transcription, or data organization context.

The hull is composed of a bow, stern, keel, and two side planks; it is not simply finished by fastening flat boards with nails. Boat-building reports from the Marine National Park Office record that large boats can be made of 27 pieces of wood, while small boats may consist of 21 pieces. Joining methods include wooden pegs, mortise joints, lashing, and gluing. Exhibition explanations at the National Museum of Science and Technology also point out that the wooden pegs used to join the planks were made from heartwood of _Morus alba_ (white mulberry), which swells in water, with a single boat using thousands of these pegs. This structure is not an "ancient version of a modern boat," but rather a long-term observation of how materials change after contact with water[^3][^4].

This small island vessel, made from 21 to 27 planks, has its origins foreshadowed in the creation myths of the Yami people. Creation legends recorded in the Taiwan Religious Culture Map by the Ministry of the Interior state that the Heavenly God gave birth to two progenitors—the Stone-born and the Bamboo-born—from rocks and bamboo. In the legend of Red Island, descendants of these ancestors each invented a boat, but all had flaws; they leaked and sank shortly after being launched until one day, while searching for sheep in the mountains, they accidentally found cottonwood (_Mimusops_), which provided a way to plug plank gaps and invent the Tatara. The legend from Dongqing village tells of the ancestors' younger brother visiting the underworld, where he learned the methods of making the Tatara, fishing group organization, and launching ceremonies from the subterranean people. Some scholars argue that Lanyu's boat-building technology preserves the lineage and remnants of South Island cultures[^15]. These legends and myths remind us that the Tatara is not just a craft technique in Yami society; it is an ancestral culture governed by spirits and taboos.

## The Forest Came Before the Boat

The first question in boat building was not "what pattern to draw," but "which tree can support which position." Exhibitions at the Marine Science Museum explain that the keel requires hard, rot-resistant, and wear-resistant _Fanolongyan_ (Taiwanese ironwood), while the side planks could be made from lightweight and buoyant Taiwanese camphor or breadfruit. Since different parts had different requirements, the boat was not made from a single type of wood[^4]. Reports from the Marine National Park Office also list wear resistance, buoyancy, hardness, rot prevention, and insect resistance as criteria for material selection, recording that Lanyu woods were certified with family symbols: _Rambutan_ relatives or Taidong ironwood for the bow/stern, Taidong ironwood for the keel, _Hibiscus_ of Lanyu and Lucky Wood (_Fumu_) of Lanyu for the bottom, and Taiwanese camphor and breadfruit for the side planks[^3].

This distances the Tatara from the general concept of "carpentry." A carpenter can buy standardized lumber from a hardware store and process it according to blueprints. Boat building, however, must confront the island's forests, tree sizes, availability, and seasons. The academic paper _Structural Materials of Lanyu Yami Tataras_ measured 132 existing boats and analyzed plank data from 191 boats for quantitative analysis, noting significant differences in boat length and paddle length between the North Mountain (Coconut Oil, Fishermen, Red Island) and South Mountain (Lang Island, Dongqing) communities. The study also found that planks were concentrated among 6 species, accounting for about 85.45% of all materials—including _Rambutan_ relatives, large-leafed _Rambutan_, breadfruit, Green Island banyan, Taidong ironwood, and _Hibiscus_ of Lanyu—and these species formed the main forest strata of Lanyu under deliberate Yami management[^5].

The significance of these numbers is not to establish a fixed recipe for "tradition," but to show that boat-building knowledge is as detailed as the community, the planks, and the purpose. Research from the American Bowers Museum on its collection _Yami Fishing Canoe_ (_Taratara_) also added material taboos before construction: only living wood was used; deadwood was considered taboo. The process involved carving three keels—the central straight keel and the curved bow/stern keels—before inserting wooden pegs into the sharp angles of the keel, onto which large planks were mortised. The precise measurement of the boat's length and width was completed with wood pieces and reeds, not standardized measuring tools[^12].

The forest, therefore, was not merely the background for boat building. It was the first material database, part of the community's judgment, taboos, and relationship with the land. Academic research quantified the wood, allowing us to see the composition of the planks. But statistics cannot replace the ability of a boat builder to identify a tree in the forest. When forests shrink, roads change, and tourism alters sourcing methods, what is affected is not just a single plank, but an entire chain of learning opportunities—from species identification, felling, transport, drying, to processing.

## Building a Boat First Requires a Fishing Group

The Tatara was never a solo project. The fishing group organization (_kakavay_) in the Yami language was a structure formed by Lanyu men while fishing; each village formed one annually during the flying fish season (March to July) and disbanded afterward. Members were adult males who netted, built boats, held ceremonies, and shared catches together. Young men aged sixteen or seventeen could join the crew as apprentices, and those over twenty became reserve members, with opportunities to become official crew members when positions opened up. Crew sizes were regulated by six (three pairs of paddles, _Atlo so avat_), eight (four pairs of paddles, _Apat so avat_), or ten (five pairs of paddles, _Alima so avat_), with the ten-person boat being the standard. Each position on the boat had a specific duty: the captain steered from the stern, and the vice-captain held the oars from the bow; no positions could be arbitrarily swapped[^17].

The fishing group and the large boat were two sides of the same system. Cultural heritage registration data indicates that the large boat carried a maximum of 10 people, making it taboo to build a boat carrying more than 10; thus, the fishing group was also limited to 10 members, with any excess serving as reserves. Members of the fishing group were involved in everything from building the large boat and preparing for the launching ceremony to the four-month flying fish season fishing that required all-night work at sea. The allocation of construction responsibility, division of labor in fishing, and distribution of catches were all arranged according to the crew members' age and status within the group[^15]. Building the large boat was a crucial link in maintaining the fishing group structure: from construction to the start of use, a new boat required two launching ceremonies, often taking several years, testing the team capability and patience of the entire crew. Completing the launching ceremony symbolized not only the new boat's entry into service but also the fishing group entering a new phase[^15].

However, this system faces structural changes. A summary of the current state of fishing groups on Chinese Wikipedia notes that with the popularization of motorized boats, Yami society rarely uses large boats to catch flying fish; sweet potatoes and pigs are often purchased from Taiwan proper, and the time needed for ritual preparation is gradually shortening. The operation of the fishing group has become looser and less practical, becoming more of a symbol of consolidating Yami community identity, and in recent years, it has been gradually replaced by net organizations[^17]. Cultural heritage registration data also records that the Dongqing Xie family's fishing group completed a completion ceremony for a large boat in 2008 (Year 97 of ROC), and the Yein Zhang Shunyong's fishing group did so in 2009 (Year 98 of ROC)—these examples of adherence to tradition still exist, but overall, the number of fishing groups is less than before[^15]. The 2026 plan for a 20-person large boat built by over 200 people can be seen as re-mobilizing this ancient fishing group logic on a new collective scale[^9].

## Plank Joining is Also Bodily Memory

The Tatara is most easily photographed: white hull, red and black patterns, neatly arranged planks. But the difficulty in boat building lies not in the final silhouette, but in the series of corrections before joining. The thickness of the planks, the direction of curvature, the location of joints, and the weight of the hull all constrain each other. If a joint is not tight, it can become a source of leakage and structural problems after going to sea. Boat builder Hsieh Zhenxiong stated in an interview for the 2026 first voyage that "if the surface is uneven, this spot sticks out a bit, and that spot sticks out a bit, the water resistance will be much greater"—this statement was not aimed at tourism display, but at a large boat about to cross the Bashi Channel[^10].

The Marine National Park Office divides the construction process into material selection, planing, joining, carving, painting, and related rituals. The Science Museum places plank joining, carving, painting, and wood division within the same exhibition narrative. Both official narratives remind us that boat building is not filling in an abstract blueprint with wood; it is a mutual correction between materials, gestures, and the hull during the process[^3][^4]. The preparation period for large boats is particularly long: park reports record that constructing a large boat requires three years of preparation, including climbing mountains to find usable timber and cultivating sweet potato fields for the completion ceremony[^3].

The interview with Syaman Misrako provides another scale. The report records him building a boat with his father, involving long periods for sourcing materials, rough shaping, carving, and painting with shell ash and red earth. He stated that completing one boat alone could take half a year to a year. This is not the standard timeline for all builders, nor does it replace institutional data, but it shows that "skill inheritance" is not an action completed in a classroom, but a life arrangement where family, agriculture, fishing group, and individual time yield to each other[^6].

The job of a boat builder is not just about making a boat. To be able to teach someone also requires knowing when to stop, which plank cannot be planed further, which pattern can be carved, and which stage requires waiting. Such judgments are often difficult to write into standard operating procedures, so inheritance requires long-term collaboration. The report mentions that there was an inheritance gap in the 1980s, with young people migrating to Taiwan proper, leaving a generation who said, "My friends are all elders; I have no young friends." When traditional crafts were reduced to tourism experiences, what was most easily lost was this part of waiting, observing, and taking responsibility[^6].

## Patterns Are Not Stickers on the Boat

The painting and carving of the Tatara are often detached from tourist imagery as "Lanyu style." However, official records—the porthole patterns, human figures, wave patterns, sun patterns, and the configuration of red, white, and black—are not generic patterns that anyone can arbitrarily copy onto merchandise. The National Cultural Memory Bank states that the gear-like eye patterns are the boat's eyes, serving a protective function. Exhibition displays at the Science Museum explain that human figures commemorate the ancestors who taught boat building, wave patterns represent ocean waves, portholes guide the community to fishing grounds, and the rule of painting the recessed areas white, raised areas black, and the waterline with vermilion red, using traditional pigments from red earth, luminous shells, and charcoal[^2][^4][^6]. Research on museum collections at the Bowers Museum also points out that the concentric circles at the bow and stern represent the boat's eyes, and the carvings commemorate Magamaog, the hero who taught the Yami people agriculture and boat building according to tradition. The three-dimensional carvings on the bows are decorated with real feathers[^12]. These patterns appear alongside the boat's identity, launching ceremony, kinship, and marine relationship; they cannot be cut out in isolation from the hull and social context.

The online exhibition _Path of the Hunter...Way of the Sea_, launched by the National Museum of Taiwan in 2025, places material selection, mortise joint techniques, porthole patterns, wave patterns, human figures, launching ceremonies, Black Tide, flying fish season, and celestial navigation into a single "Way of the Sea" section[^7]. This mode of display is noteworthy: it does not treat the Tatara merely as an object for viewing, but presents boat building, navigation, oral tradition, prayer, and environmental knowledge together.

However, the museum's integrated narrative cannot replace internal tribal differences. Different communities, families, boat types, and rituals may have different terminology, norms, and practices. For a knowledge base, the correct way to write is not to translate all patterns into one beautiful symbol, but to clearly state "which source describes it in what context." If data only says that porthole patterns appear on display boats, do not extrapolate that all boats on the island must have them. If an interview mentions a certain practice, do not rewrite a personal life experience as a consensus of the entire tribe.

## The Launching Ceremony Makes the Boat a Social Member

The description of the Yami large boat launching ceremony in the National Cultural Heritage website includes ritual processes such as _mapabosbos_, _Manwaway_, _Mapatotalaw_, and _Mangavang_, stipulating that any boat with carvings must undergo a completion ceremony. The registered preservation groups include six community associations from Yein, Lang Island, Coconut Oil, Fishermen, Red Island, and Dongqing[^1]. These names and processes should not be simplified into a tourist program schedule. They explain that the new boat must enter the order of the tribe and the sea through collective action.

Activity reports from the Marine National Park Office and the Taiwan Religious Culture Map detail more specific ritual structures: the day before the completion ceremony (_mapabosbos_), sweet potatoes were harvested, and relatives were invited; on the first day, sweet potatoes were piled up for a welcoming banquet and an all-night celebratory song session; on the second day, sweet potatoes and pork were distributed, _Manwaway_ (power accumulation) and _Mapatotalaw_ (boat launching ceremony), and _Mangavang_ (speed trial) took place; on the third day, there was a feast, and the boat was paddled to a designated point and returned by the captain's wife with lucky millet. The launching process also included piling sweet potatoes for the boat, distributing ritual meat (pork belly, blood, lungs, abdomen, intestines, etc.), _Manawai_, boat launch, and trial paddling. What is less known to outsiders is the distinction between a regular launching ceremony and a completion ceremony: a regular launching ceremony was held for large boats without carvings (_ioiroaun_); only after carving (_ipanitika_) would the special and grand completion ceremony be held. After carving, three preparations—harvesting sweet potatoes (_Mangap so soli/Mangap so ora_), inviting guests (_Mapatoyon/Manayid_), and piling sweet potatoes (_Mapadpon_)—were required before the completion ceremony[^3][^15]. The trial voyage was also symbolic: crew members slowly pushed the new boat onto the sea to test its stability, while shore members slaughtered ritual chickens and plucked them. The speed of plucking symbolized the ship's speed. After reaching its destination, the bowman would catch fish and crabs; catching a crab or octopus signified good luck for the new boat[^15]. Reports also detail crew positions and fishing practices: the stern captain (_Panavilaken_) needed to be tall and capable of reading currents and weather; the species caught during the first trial fishing was interpreted as the ship's future fortune—grouper meant prosperity, and sea bream meant wealth[^1][^3]. Here, the ritual is not an after-the-fact performance after construction, but a procedure that reorders the boat, crew, food, family, and marine action. For a boat to sail, it naturally needs physical structure. For a boat to be recognized by the tribe, it requires social structure.

The social life of this vessel is closely linked to the flying fish season rituals. _Indigenous Cultural Archives_, in its article "Flying Fish Legends, Fishing Stories, and the Religious and Social Life of the Yami People," points out that flying fish are not just an important food source but are revered as friends and gods—a cosmic entity: in the creation myth, the Heavenly God was saddened by the people discarding flying fish, so he revealed fishing, processing, and consumption norms to the Stone-born progenitor in a dream via the Black-winged Flying Fish God (_mavaeng so panid_), requiring the community to hold a catching ceremony (_mivanoa_) every year to welcome the schools of flying fish. The festival runs from late January until the final feast in early October, during which strict taboos govern social order: men must sleep indoors the night before going out to sea; relatives are forbidden from visiting the home; women are forbidden from cooking; and no one is allowed to step on another's property while walking[^16]. The multi-month flying fish festival each year is a shared annual memory of all six Lanyu communities, and the Tatara is an indispensable protagonist in this ritual—without boats, there is no night fishing for flying fish. Without a fishing group, there is no boat. This also explains why "putting a Tatara in a museum" and "preserving Tatara culture" are not the same thing. A museum can preserve wood, carvings, paintings, and images, but it cannot preserve how a boat was used within the context of launching ceremonies, the flying fish season, the fishing group, and the tribe. The best preservation strategy must record the object, technology, language, people, seasons, and usage norms simultaneously. Otherwise, what remains is just the appearance of the boat, not the complete knowledge of the boat.

## _avavang_ Reminds Us Not to Mix Boats

The traditional wooden dugout canoe (_avavang_) of Lanyu is an important case study. The Indigenous Traditional Wisdom Protection Information Network records that the _avavang_ was carved from a single piece of wood, possibly leftover material after making a Tatara. Oral histories also mention it being used as a children's toy, a carving practice model, or a souvenir[^8]. In documents regarding this case, Shamam Lanboan noted that making the _avavang_ "besides giving children toys, was also intended to inspire their longing for the Tatara and spark their interest in boat building in the future"[^8]. This case received intellectual property protection in June 2023, with the Yami Tao (the entire community collectively owned, represented by Zhang Maqun) as the applicant, compiling oral histories from six communities and collective ownership claims[^8].

The difference between the _avavang_ and the Tatara cannot be erased by saying "both are small boats." The former is a traditional wooden dugout canoe carved from a single piece of wood; the latter is a plank boat made from multiple planks. The uses and traditions of the former include children's toys, carving practice, and souvenirs; the latter is deeply connected to fishing groups, the flying fish season, sea trips, rituals, and crew order. They can be linked through leftover materials, father-son learning, and children's imagination, but they are not the same boat.

This case also brings cultural preservation into the realm of rights issues. When porthole patterns, designs, proportions of the Tatara, or the _avavang_ shape are mass-replicated into products made by outsiders, external viewers may only see "a very Lanyu style" without seeing the community, oral history, and usage norms behind it. Traditional wisdom protection is not against all exchange; it demands that any exchange first acknowledge who holds the right to explain, produce, and authorize. This point is particularly important for designers, museums, schools, and tourism operators.

## 2026: Restarting a Three-Hundred-Year Interrupted Route

The public potential of the Tatara reached a new scale in June 2026. The "Lanyu–Badan Three Hundred Year First Voyage Project" was organized by the Executive Yuan's Indigenous Peoples Affairs Council and managed by the Indigenous Cultural Foundation, supported by the Ministry of Culture, Marine Affairs Council, and Ministry of Foreign Affairs. After three years of preparation involving six communities and over 200 participants, the modern largest Tatara, "Ovayan Golden Friendship" (_chinedkeran_), was built using traditional methods; it is about 12 meters long, and the name Ovayan means golden chest ornament in the Yami language[^9][^11]. The boat was completed in March, tested on April 28, and inaugurated with a traditional large boat launching ceremony on June 9. It set sail from Lanyu Longmen Port on June 15, crewed by 60 paddlers rotating shifts from the six communities, arriving at Badang Island, Philippines, on the evening of June 16, restarting an ancient maritime route interrupted for nearly 300 years[^9][^11]. English reports from CNN further noted that this was a voyage of about 111 nautical miles, navigated by celestial observation against the strong currents of the Bashi Channel. The hull suffered severe leakage during the journey and was ultimately completed with the support of crew rotation and escort vessels[^10].

The historical context of this route is deeper than what general tourism narratives suggest. The Yami people of Lanyu and the Ivatan people of the Badang Islands both belong to the Austronesian language family, showing high similarities in language, boat-building techniques, marine knowledge, and culture over many centuries. Both groups frequently traveled back and forth with traditional boats, maintaining trade, marriage, and cultural interaction. Travelogues that compiled historical documents mention that missionary Thomas Sanchez recorded oral history in 1801 where the residents of Itayat Island attacked a visiting vessel, resulting in only one survivor. After this, direct contact between the two places ceased, leaving the connection between Lanyu and Badang sustained only by oral tradition, ancestral memory, and scattered documents[^11]. Therefore, when the "Ovayan Golden Friendship" docked, the Yami paddlers' chant of "Kakteh do cinai" (We are brothers and sisters from the same gut) was not just a ceremonial slogan, but a public response to a specific history of interruption. Hundreds of Ivatan residents welcomed them with drums and dance, collectively helping to carry the Tatara ashore and parade it to the welcoming site[^10][^11].

This event also changed how "the Tatara is recognized by the world." Past international visibility came from museum collections and research papers—for example, the Bowers Museum used its collection of Tataras to show that Yami and Philippine _balangay_ shared the "lashed lug" construction method. International media coverage of the Yami was often focused on static culture[^12]. The 2026 voyage is the first time this body of knowledge has appeared as a "technology in use": the boat builder's engineering judgment on surface flatness, the paddlers' practice of navigation by Black Tide and celestial bodies, the organization of 60 people rotating shifts, and the decision to gift the boat to Badang Island upon arrival all propelled the Tatara from an "object to be viewed" into a "system that is still solving problems"[^10][^9].

## Tourism Makes the Boat More Visible, But It Can Also Make It Thinner

After entering exhibitions, images, online panoramas, and cultural experiences, the Tatara is indeed more visible to other parts of Taiwan and international audiences. The National Museum of Taiwan's exhibition places Shamam Lanboan's interview, tribal prayers, real-life Tataras, Black Tide, and celestial navigation into a multilingual panoramic exhibit. This allows content that originally required being on Lanyu to be accessed by more people through digital means[^7]. Visibility is not the issue; the problem is whether the display turns culture into a decontextualized background image.

Syaman Misrako's interview also presents another form of publicness: the boat builder brings Tatara technology into broader public discussion through sea travel, cultural promotion, and tourism activities. At the same time, the report maintains the tension between traditional taboos, tribal life, and tourist experiences—he mentioned that flying fish season was not open for experience because it was traditionally forbidden for women to board (due to concerns from the community about affecting fishing), but the cultural experience allowed women to interact with the Tatara, which he sought as a balance between traditional norms and tourism demands. The experiential boats on the island have grown from 5 or 6 to nearly 20[^6]. Academic studies on the commercialization of the Tatara raised warnings earlier: Tsai Wei-hsuan's 2009 master's thesis observed the phenomenon of Lanyu Tatara tourism, pointing out that when taboos and traditions gradually dissolve, and the Tatara experience is pulled into a tourist field as another form of economic production, the Tatara culture undergoes extreme transformation. The study suggested that experiential activities should be enriched with cultural context[^14].

This tension cannot be summarized in one phrase: "conflict between tradition and modernity." For the boat builders, tourism can bring income, apprenticeships, and display opportunities. For the community, if tourists only demand quick photos, short experiences, or purchase replicas, the boat may be reduced to a consumption symbol. Therefore, cultural tours must clarify at least three things. First, who built, used, or commissioned the current boat. Second, which content is for public display and which involves rituals, taboos, or tribal rights. Third, whether the audience's actions of photographing, touching, imitating, or purchasing are permitted. This is not making tourism inconvenient; it is ensuring that "understanding culture" does not stop at owning a photograph.

## The Object of Preservation Is Not a Perfect Boat

The most common misunderstanding in preserving the Tatara is believing that saving one complete boat means saving the entire boat-building culture. A complete boat is certainly important, but it is only the result of a specific moment in time. To help later generations understand this boat, one must also know where the wood came from, who was responsible for selecting it, how long the building took, which tasks were shared by family and fishing group, which ceremony launched it, and how it was maintained after use.

The evidence comes from different levels: material statistics in academic research, ritual descriptions in cultural heritage registration, structural explanations in museums, and life stories of boat builders. Research tells us that there are comparable differences between planks and wood species. Cultural heritage data tells us about rituals and preservers. Museums translate the structure into a publicly understandable display. Personal interviews make the construction time, learning process, family dynamics, and gaps concrete. No single source can replace another.

This is the depth that Taiwan.md should record: not to write again that "the Tatara is a symbol of Yami traditional culture," but to deconstruct the "symbol" into verifiable relationships. Which wood supports which structure? Which source records what boat type? Which ritual is supported by which cultural heritage page? Which claims are merely the life narrative of one builder? What rights were compiled in the intellectual property protection application? Only when these layers are separated does the article not turn indigenous culture into a beautiful adjective without an author, time, or tribal difference. This writing style also allows readers to see how knowledge is produced: researchers measure, curators archive, communities apply, and builders practice—each layer has its own evidence.

## The Tatara Still Answers a Modern Question

The modern significance of the Tatara is not that it is "purer" than fiberglass boats, nor is it automatically worthy of preservation because it is old. It is important because it demonstrates a way of binding environment, technology, and society together: the forest is not just a warehouse of materials; the sea is not an empty highway; the boat is not a neutral tool; and the ritual is not merely an after-the-fact performance. Every construction reconfirms how humans acquire wood, how labor is distributed, how one becomes a crew member, how to enter the flying fish season, and who has the right to describe this boat.

The 2026 voyage pushed this question into a more open space. The boat was gifted to Badang Island, the route was reopened, and Lanyu builders began discussing language revitalization, craft exchange, and youth visits with the Ivatan people of the Philippines[^9]. If only the appearance of the boat is preserved, there may be more and more things "like Tataras" in the future, but fewer people will know why planks must be joined this way, which wood to choose, when a pattern can be carved, or when a boat can be launched. Conversely, if preservation efforts support long-term cooperation between builders, tribal organizations, apprentices, researchers, and museums, the Tatara does not have to be fixed in an imagined past. It can be documented digitally, enter new exhibitions, engage in environmental education, and continue to be built, paddled, and described within the boundaries set by the community.

What is most worth preserving about the Lanyu Tatara is not the conclusion of "a very special boat," but a more difficult and practical question: when a society joins forest knowledge, marine experience, kinship relations, ritual norms, and aesthetic judgment into one vessel, do we have the capacity to view it without mistaking any part for mere decoration?
