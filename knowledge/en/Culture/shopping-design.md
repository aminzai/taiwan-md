> **30-Second Overview:** Launched in 2006, Shopping Design (Design Curation Magazine) has spent two decades turning "design" from a specialized term confined to exhibition halls into a daily practice for browsing stores and selecting objects. What it truly taught Taiwan is that "discernment" can be cultivated as a skill: recognizing the difference between this shade of white and that, and articulating why. It expanded the act of "discerning for you" from a single magazine into an annual Top 100 list, a quarterly publication, and a design carnival; it also expanded it onto its parent company's single "magazine publishing + advertising services" license. Thus, this magazine that teaches you to discern ultimately asks you to discern itself.

## Two White Covers, Eleven Years Apart

In December 2006, a magazine called Shopping Design launched on Taiwan's bookstore shelves. Its first issue focused on white design. Eleven years later, in March 2017, it reached its 100th issue. The cover returned to white, but this time with two additional characters: "Procuring White Design" [^1].

The same white, separated by eleven years, now carries the word "procuring." These two words may appear like marketing jargon, but they conceal the magazine's true core. Whiteness itself carries no inherent value. A white wall, a blank sheet of paper, a white shirt—the difference does not lie in the color itself, but in whether you can see where one shade diverges from another, and why one deserves to be taken home. "Procuring" transforms an abstract concept—taste—into a concrete action: discernment.

This is precisely what the magazine has been doing for two decades, yet rarely articulated outright. Its name, "Shopping Design," sounds as though it will teach you how to buy things. But what it truly sells is never a specific chair or a particular café; it sells the ability to discern: to perceive distinctions and articulate their reasons. Once you have trained yourself alongside it for twenty years, you will realize that the very object requiring the sharpest discernment is, in fact, the magazine itself.

Hold that thought. First, let's trace where this craft of "discernment" originally took root, and in whose hands it grew.

## Two Ad-Industry Veterans, Teaching You How to Look

The man who founded it is Huang Wei-jung. He was not yet forty. He was not formally trained in design; his background was advertising. "My first job was as a copywriter at an ad agency. That was the golden age of Taiwan's advertising industry," he recalled in an interview [^2]. The agency was Ideology Advertising, and his direct supervisor was creative director Hsu Shun-ying. "My first direct supervisor was Ms. Hsu Shun-ying, creative director at Ideology Advertising," he said years later, noting that this experience continues to shape him profoundly [^2]. Ideology Advertising was the premier narrative-driven agency of 1990s Taiwan. A copywriter there honed a specific skill: articulating why the ordinary is extraordinary.

In autumn 1998, he and four close friends—Ma Shih-fang, Yao Rui-zhong, Hsu Yun-pin, and Chen Kuang-da—co-authored _One Hundred Reasons to Survive in Taipei_, published by Big Block Culture. The book re-examined and re-described the most mundane urban corners: alleyways, signage, street food, buses. "Romantic endeavors like this only happen once in a lifetime," he later wrote about the collaboration [^3]. This book introduced him to many, transforming him from an ad copywriter into an editor who organizes images and text to help readers see the world anew. He divides his thirty-year creative career into three phases: first, ad copywriting and publishing; second, magazine editing and visual-text integration; third, hybrid creative services [^2]. Shopping Design sits precisely at the starting point of the second phase.

Thus, his decision to launch a design magazine in 2006 followed a natural trajectory. Yet the magazine's birth required more than ideology; it required commerce. Years later, he revealed that the true catalyst that summer came from a convenience store channel: "Hard magazines are finding it increasingly difficult to push sales upward. Why not try a 'soft' topic?" [^4] "Soft" refers to light, aesthetically pleasing subjects like lifestyle, travel, and design. He cited _La Vie_ and PPAPER, launched around 2004, as the "vanguard of soft magazines," with Shopping Design arriving in their wake [^4]. A magazine teaching discernment in design was, from day one, both an ideological pursuit and a commercially viable product. This dual lineage would eventually crystallize into the article's central question.

To understand how it earned the right to teach discernment, we must examine what an editor actually does daily. Editing boils down to three actions: placing a cluster of items side by side for comparison, naming their differences, and arranging them on the page so the reader can comprehend them. For a café issue, it juxtaposes pour-over vs. espresso, heritage buildings vs. minimalist white, solitary drinking vs. communal conversation. It points out how each type of quiet or bustle serves different people, deconstructing the word "café" into multiple distinct ways of living. An inn issue, a white issue, a "filter" issue—each selects a theme, lays out an entire category, points and says, "This is worth it, because...", and arranges it page by page.

As you flip through, you think you are admiring beautiful images, but you are actually being trained, page by page, to see. Over time, you will notice that you begin to apply this same gaze while shopping: facing two seemingly identical items, you pause, perceive their differences, and articulate why one moves you. This capacity for discernment is cultivated and transmitted page by page. This is what the magazine truly sells you, yet charges nothing for.

The clearest articulation of this comes from Li Hui-chen, who succeeded Huang Wei-jung as editor-in-chief. She took over in 2011 and stepped down in 2017 [^5]. Notably, she also transitioned from advertising: "I am deeply grateful to Editor-in-Chief Hsu Li-wen for helping me grow from a deserter of the ad world into a confident planner" [^6]. She translated Huang's implicit editorial craft into a direct, audible principle: magazines should remind readers to live "consciously"—to choose consciously, to consume consciously. "Conscious" in plain terms means discernment: perceiving distinctions and questioning their causes. She even quoted Chan Hung-chih: "When you read a certain book, you may become a lonely person..." [^6]—the more you know, the less you can return to the self that accepts everything without question. In 2016, she and Shopping Design jointly received the Editor Award for the Magazine Category at the 40th Golden Pinwheel Awards [^7]. The following year, for the 100th issue, she chose the theme of returning to that opening white, adding the two characters "procuring."

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The two editors-in-chief who successively helmed this magazine actually intersect at the same deep point in their resumes: both fled the advertising industry, and both worked under the same Big Block Culture editor-in-chief, Liao Li-wen [^6]. This need not be over-interpreted as a direct mentorship, but it reveals one thing: Taiwan's wave of "soft magazines" teaching people to look at design originated with a group trained in advertising, skilled at finding narratives for the ordinary. They took that craft away from selling things and redirected it toward teaching people to discern things. The craft of discernment inherently shares the same muscle memory as the craft of salesmanship.

An editor's eye, cultivated page by page, could originally only hold a single magazine. Over the next two decades, what Shopping Design did was to expand this "discerning for you" action, circle by circle.

## One Magazine Is Not Enough: Lists, Quarters, Carnivals

A monthly can only discern one theme at a time. So it began to stretch that same action into other forms.

First, the print rhythm changed. In late 2019, Shopping Design shifted from monthly to quarterly. The first quarterly issue after the revamp was Vol. 134, published in March 2020 [^8][^10]. The shift to quarterly was a deliberate division of labor: the website handles speed, tracking daily news and exhibitions in the design world; the print magazine handles slowness, spending three months per quarter to fully think through a single theme. The editorial department explained this pivot: "The environment is changing, and so are readers. If it is to remain necessary, we hope to endow it with more value," making it "useful, interesting, and meaningful" [^9].

The slowed-down print edition means each quarter becomes a complete thematic proposition. In 2023, it published _The Ideal Dweller_, questioning the relationship between a person and their living space [^11]. In 2024, it published _We Collect Design Like This_, asking why people want to keep certain objects close [^11]. Each issue takes an unspoken question of daily life for an entire demographic, discerns it thoroughly, and names it clearly. This is an enlarged version of the founding craft, merely shifting the subject from an object to a way of living.

Discerning for a demographic is not enough; it next discerns for an entire year. Starting in 2012, Shopping Design annually publishes the Taiwan Design Top 100, selecting one hundred people, events, and objects from that year's Taiwanese design to form an annual list [^12]. This list serves to name and archive a year of Taiwanese design: what deserves remembrance, what defines the year. The 2019 edition, themed "Keywords of Design Power," divided the one hundred entries into nine categories: Annual Figures, Annual Design Events, Hundred Scenic Humanities, etc. [^13]. It performed for an entire industry what the founding issue did for a white cover: compare, select, and name. Over a decade of accumulation, this stack of selected items became Taiwan's design closest to a "public history" index—without government endorsement, yet silently treated by the entire circle as an annual coordinate.

Ultimately, it even moved the act of discernment off the page. Starting in 2013, it hosted "Design Gatherings," transforming page-based comparisons and naming into live lectures and dialogues; in 2016, it founded the Style Economy Academy, beginning to translate aesthetics into teachable commercial language [^8]. In 2022, this lineage grew into the first DesignBIZ Fest, a carnival centered on "New Design Business" [^14]. The official definition for the event is a string of parallel clauses: "Design is creativity, design is marketing power, design is cultural power, design is planning power, design is integration power—design is, vitality." [^14] The stage hosts a lineup of brand executives, from co-working spaces and electric scooters to chain restaurants [^15]. Its reach also extends to audio, using podcasts to bring designers' careers, brands, and local revitalization into the ear [^16]. The same "seeing for you, selecting for you" action has now grown from a single page into a complete, ticketed, audiable, and sponsorable commercial language.

One magazine, one list, one carnival: on the surface, three different things; in essence, the same action scaled across three levels: discerning for you, discerning for a year, discerning for an industry. Once the scale expands this far, a question originally hidden beneath the editorial layout surfaces: when "discerning for you" becomes such a large business, can the hand that discerns for you still be separated from the hand that sells for brands?

## The Hand That Selects for You Also Sells for Brands

First, examine the least romantic document: the company registry. Shopping Design's parent company is Business Next Media Corp. (巨思文化股份有限公司), Unified Number 16780474, Chairman Chen Su-lan [^16b]. Flipping through its government-registered "Business Scope" reveals two parallel lines: one reads "Magazine (Periodical) Publishing Industry," and nearby, another reads "General Advertising Services Industry" [^15b]. These words on a registry document, in black and white, constitute its legal business scope. The same company, the same license, one side publishing content that discerns design for you, the other operating advertising sales; its official site also publicly lists ad placement rates, with single placements ranging from 100,000 to 280,000 NTD, and a 10% discount for annual packages [^17]. No polished brand manifesto is as hard as these two registered lines: content and advertising inherently share the same corporate foundation.

This fact is stated more bluntly by insiders. In 2017, Shopping Design's deputy editor-in-chief Pao Shu-ping engaged in a public dialogue with Eric, editor-in-chief of another lifestyle media outlet, discussing "advertorials"—content that reads like journalism but is actually advertising. "Advertorials mean that whether you like it or not, you have to make it compelling to read," Pao stated [^18]. This remark lacks any veil: sponsored content is an industry reality; the media's skill lies in writing it so readers are willing to consume it. He added a remark that serves as a footnote to the entire print era: "Magazines have been hard to sell these past two years. Many media outlets will not publish print editions this year, yet SD still exists." [^18]

In the same dialogue, Eric explained how they navigate this: "In the first paragraph or headline, we will bluntly tell you this is an advertisement," pursuing a "win-win-win" for readers, clients, and writers [^18]. This is a posture of honesty, clearly marking what is advertising and returning the choice to the reader. But a honest posture does not erase the boundary. An advertorial marked "advertisement" lets readers know it is sponsored; yet a magazine's overall taste, the "good design" it curates for you, often lacks that very line of disclosure. The blurriest boundaries often hide within content that appears most pure, yet shares the same license as the advertising department.

In that same era, Shopping Design's official site used a whiskey sponsored post to praise "Editor-in-Chief Huang Wei-jung at the founding" as a "discerning patron of life," packaging his editorial image into a brand story for a bottle of liquor [^19]. To be precise, those phrases about "opening the five senses, knowing how to appreciate" were not spoken by Huang himself, but were third-person introductions written by the editorial team for the sponsored post. A magazine that teaches you to discern uses the image it itself constructed of its founder to endorse a consumer brand. This is precisely the closest manifestation of "discerning for you" and "selling for brands" converging.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> It is important not to frame this as a problem unique to Shopping Design. The same model of "media covering an industry while also hosting awards for it" applies to another soft magazine, _La Vie_, which has hosted the "Taiwan Creativity Power 100" since 2014 [^20]. Tracing one layer further is even more revealing: _La Vie_'s parent group, CityMedia, was convened by Chan Hung-chih in 1996; Shopping Design's parent company, Business Next Media, was also co-founded with Chan's participation in 1999 [^21]. Taiwan's two major lifestyle media families intersect at the same media figure at their source. This is not a conspiracy, but a structural portrait of Taiwan's highly concentrated publishing industry. "Hosting a list for an industry" is a common practice across this sector; each uses it to accumulate brand authority and extend commercial collaboration space. No one is an exception.

It is important to clarify that no public report explicitly criticizes Shopping Design's sponsored content or awards. What follows are not yet "established controversies," but rather structural questions that have always been present, waiting to be asked. Yet this question circulates in social discourse, often tagged as "artsy magazines," implying a certain performance of consumption and superiority of taste. Interestingly, even Huang Wei-jung himself publicly rejected this label: "I truly believe there are no such things as artsy magazines in Taiwan." [^4] His act of rejection precisely proves that the suspicion has always existed. Since the suspicion persists, someone must ultimately discern. And that someone can only be the reader.

## The Final Lesson: Discerning Itself

In December 2025, Shopping Design published Vol. 155, titled _Design Wanted_ [^22]. It borrows the most vernacular visual language: the "Wanted" recruitment posters pasted on utility poles and shop doors. Blunt, direct, posted openly. A magazine that spent twenty years teaching Taiwan how to choose, how to look, how to discern, ultimately pasted itself as a recruitment poster: Design, Wanted.

![Shopping Design Vol.155 *Design Wanted* cover, the magazine pastes itself as a recruitment poster](/article-images/culture/shopping-design-vol155-cover.webp)
_Vol.155 Design Wanted. The magazine that teaches you to discern ultimately pastes itself as an exam paper. (credit: Business Next Media Group, Fair use editorial commentary)_

This ending actually aligns with a society's long preparation. Taiwan published the _Media Literacy Education Policy White Paper_ as early as 2002, and high schools incorporated media literacy into the curriculum starting in the 2017 academic year [^23]. "Readers must have the ability to distinguish sponsored content from editorial content" has long been a consensus at the level of educational policy. In other words, a media outlet ultimately returning the responsibility of discernment to the reader is precisely what this society already decided to teach everyone. Nothing particularly novel. The difference lies only in that the object to be discerned this time has shifted to the very person who spent twenty years teaching you how to discern.

So where does Shopping Design stand now? It has not been replaced by the internet. As of April 2026, the print edition is still publishing Vol. 156 [^11]. Placed within the same wave of soft magazines, this is already remarkable: some changed hands, some stopped at their latest issue in late 2025 with no new issues since, while Shopping Design remains one of the few cases to maintain the same brand and trajectory to the present. Yet it is quietly narrowing. The "About Us" page on its official site was taken down in recent years; the title from 2006 to 2017 was "Editor-in-Chief," but the highest title visible in 2024 public reports has already been downgraded to "Editor Yixin, Deputy Editor Yajun" [^24]. A magazine that teaches you to discern, and teaches you to追问 "who selected this for me," has ironically made its own information about editorial structure and lineage harder to trace than before. You must first learn the very discernment it taught you in order to go back and discern who it actually is, and who it works for. And it is precisely shrinking those latter clues, year by year. This itself is an exercise it never explicitly states, yet leaves to the reader.

This is precisely the final lesson it returns to the reader. From the white of the founding issue to the recruitment poster of Vol. 155, lies a complete craft of "discerning for you," expanded circle by circle into lists, quarters, and carnivals, and step by step closer to the business of selling for brands. What this magazine taught Taiwan runs far deeper than which chair looks good or which inn is worth staying in. It taught that "discernment" itself can be cultivated as a skill. And the wider and larger it expands this skill, the more the object you ultimately need to discern is itself.

Thus, the _Design Wanted_ cover is, in fact, also an exam paper. What it seeks to recruit is actually a reader who can understand, and can ask, "who selected this for me." Twenty years ago, it taught you to procure white; twenty years later, it returns to your hands the power of discernment, along with the necessity of discerning itself. Knowing how to appreciate and knowing how to doubt have always coexisted in the same pair of eyes. And those eyes were precisely what it spent twenty years, page by page, training for you.
