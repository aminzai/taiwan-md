# Sì-siù-á: A Sweet Festival and Blessings That Needn't All Taste Good

> **30-second overview:** _Shēng rén táng_ reads "shēng rén / shēng rén" to turn peanuts into family wishes; _cùn zhāo_ borrows the character "zhāo" to imagine early blessings; _má luǎ_ puts "eating until old, old, old" into malt sugar and sesame[^3]. The Ministry of Education dictionary defines "sì-siù-á" as snacks and nibbles[^2]. But festival table sweets aren't just about auspiciousness—they're also about flavor. _Má luǎ_ and _cùn zhāo_ each pack about 500 to 600 calories per 100 grams, and food inspections now include peanut pieces in aflatoxin testing[^6][^7]. What this article wants to see is how a plate of sweets can simultaneously become blessing, habit, and something that needs to be re-chosen in daily life.

## Let the "Person" Be Said First with Shēng rén táng

_Shēng rén táng_ often sits between festival candies and offerings: it can be given to guests as a sweet treat, or placed on the altar. This position isn't like supermarket shelf categories—it's more like someone opening a candy box on the living room table, pointing at each square and asking: "Have you tried this one?"[^1]

The common look of _shēng rén táng_ is peanut kernels wrapped in layers of red and white sugar frosting. An old Zhongnan article calls it "shēng rén táng" and uses the homophonic link between "shēng rén" and "shēng rén" to connect to family prosperity. The article also records another name, "tiān gōng dòu" (Heavenly Great), linking it to the folk custom of worshipping the Jade Emperor on the ninth day of the first lunar month[^3]. These stories belong to folklore and brand culture—they're not explanations every household uses. But they show something: festival candies are kept around not just because of sweetness.

_Taiwanese sweets. Photo: pelican, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/deed.en). [Image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwanese_sweets_(1088069273).jpg).\_[^8]

This photo brings _shēng rén_, candies, and other sweets together in one frame. Festival snacks rarely serve just one meaning—they sit side by side in the same box, letting the blessings on the altar, the hospitality in the living room, and children picking at sweets all appear at once[^8].

"Shēng rén" can be re-read through blessing. Peanuts are food; strangers are wishes. A sugar-coated peanut thus stands between the dining table and the altar. Some people even forget the taste of certain candies—the uncertainty makes the festival believable: tradition isn't something that has to be memorized every year, but something people are still willing to ask about again.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Standard festival lists always translate each snack into a sentence of good wishes, but this translation hides choices of dislike, picky eating, and buying just for hospitality. What's left in the candy box isn't proof that "everyone loves it," but how families negotiate between blessing and taste.

## Cùn zhāo Has No Jujube, Má luǎ Can Eat "Old"

_Cùn zhāo_ often appears in this set of festival vocabulary. An old Zhongnan article calls it "zhāo zhī" or "cùn zhāo táng" and records a saying: in earlier times, red jujubes were scarce, so glutinous rice was shaped like jujubes, coated in white sugar to substitute for real ones. So the name has jujube, but the ingredients may not[^3]. This story is best understood as "legend"—it has explanatory power, but shouldn't be written as the sole origin theory.

The interesting thing about _cùn zhāo_ is that its name completes the blessing before its ingredients do. When a food is called "zhāo," people can put the wish for early blessings into the festival context. Even if what's eaten is actually fried glutinous rice. Rather than mystifying the food, it's more like compressing family expectations into a sentence spoken at the dinner table.

An old Zhongnan article describes _má luǎ_ as a puff pastry made from taro and glutinous rice, fried and coated in malt sugar with added toppings. It records the blessing "eating má luǎ can eat until old, old, old" and notes its role in the Heavenly Emperor festival, engagement ceremonies, and homecoming rituals[^3]. Here, "old" doesn't mean aging—it means putting longevity, lifelong companionship, and living a long, peaceful life into the crispy and sticky texture.

Buying a whole bag of _má luǎ_ before the New Year turns it from abstract meaning back into daily life: it's not a museum artifact that can only be viewed behind glass, but food that can be bought in bulk and opened when relatives visit. The Tourism and Travel Bureau of Taoyuan City Government also lists _má luǎ_, _shēng rén_, _cùn zhāo_, _dòng guā tiáo_ (winter melon strips), and _niú xiá táng_ (milk candy) as seasonal festival items sold before the New Year[^4].

![Dihua Street Lunar New Year candy stall, 2010](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b7/2010-02-13_Lunar_New_Year_sweets_vendor_at_Dihua_Street%2C_Taipei.jpg)

_2010 Dihua Street Lunar New Year candy stall. Photo: eazytraveler, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0/deed.en). [Image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:2010-02-13_Lunar_New_Year_sweets_vendor_at_Dihua_Street,_Taipei.jpg)._[^9]

The 2010 Dihua Street photo puts _má luǎ_, _shēng rén_, and other sweets back into the shopping scene. People don't reach out after understanding the meaning—they decide whether to take a pack home after seeing colors, prices, and familiar items. Festival culture thus includes a very practical action: walking into the market before the New Year and buying blessings home[^9].

## "Sì-siù-á" Isn't Four Kinds, It's a Whole Set of Snack Expressions

The Ministry of Education dictionary lists "sì-siù-á" pronounced as `sì-siù-á`, meaning "snacks and nibbles," with the example sentence "You go buy some sì-siù-á to snack on"[^\2]. The importance of this word isn't in drawing strict boundaries around snacks, but in preserving how Taiwan families talk about food: not as nutritional snacks, nor as items in a brand catalog, but as the everyday tone of "buy some first and try eating."

_Má huā xuán_ (sesame rolls), _kāi xīn guǒ_ (happy nuts), _guā zi_ (melon seeds), _jīn bì qiǎo kè lì_ (gold coin chocolates), _niú xiá táng_ (milk candy), _xuě huā míng_ (snow flower cake), _nán zhāo hé táo gāo_ (southern jujube and walnut cake), and _gān nà dòu_ (candied lotus seeds) can all be placed in the same festival story, without pretending they share the same origin. Gold coin chocolates carry the imagination of imported goods; melon seeds retain the family habit of eating; candied lotus seeds pull focus to sugar-soaked legumes. These differences together show the fact that "why everything is on the New Year's table"—itself[^1].

This mix is very Taiwanese: traditional offerings, local snacks, imported chocolates, and wedding candies can all appear in the same box. TaiwanPlus's English report also describes Taiwan's Lunar New Year candies as symbols of joy and good fortune, pointing the camera at Dihua Street's Lunar New Year market. Candies here aren't isolated foods, but festival objects composed of shopping, gifting, hosting, and blessing[^5].

![Dihua Street Lunar New Year market, 2007](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b3/Dihua_Street_market%2C_Chinese_New_Year_2007.jpg)

_2007 Dihua Street Lunar New Year market. Photo: Allentchang, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY-SA 3.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/deed.en). [Image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dihua_Street_market,_Chinese_New_Year_2007.jpg)._[^10]

The 2007 market photo pulls the view back. Dihua Street isn't a shop selling one kind of candy—it's a festival street scene with offerings, dried goods, snacks, and decorations lined up. Candy boxes can mix in chocolates, peanuts, and preserved fruits precisely because the Lunar New Year market allows sweets from different eras to be taken home at the same time[^10].

## Festival Etiquette Allows a "Not Tasty"

Gold coin chocolates, haricot beans, and three-color soft candies don't match the festive tone commonly seen in traditional New Year snacks. Appearing during the New Year doesn't have to mean they're worth liking. Just because a candy has an auspicious name doesn't mean everyone has to accept its taste. This contrast is important: candies provide blessings, but people still keep the freedom to refuse the carrier of those blessings.

This freedom has always existed in festival foods. Preparing a plate of sì-siù-á is to give guests something to hold and conversations something to pair with. Who eats only peanuts, who picks out chocolates, who leaves disliked candies until last—those are often the details that truly stay in family memory. If tradition can only be praised, it quickly becomes a slogan. If tradition can be criticized, it might continue into the next generation's mouth.

![Dihua Street Lunar New Year vendor, 2007](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/4/4b/2007-02-13_vendor_at_the_Chinese_New_Year_Fair_of_Dihua_Street.jpg)

_2007 Dihua Street Lunar New Year vendor. Photo: Michael Rehfeldt, Wikimedia Commons, [CC BY 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0/deed.en). [Image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:2007-02-13_vendor_at_the_Chinese_New_Year_Fair_of_Dihua_Street.jpg)._[^11]

The vendor's figure adds a role often overlooked to "festival snacks": the person selling them. Family memory only remembers which candy was sweetest, but market memory also includes who was welcoming guests among lanterns, red couplets, and stacks of goods. Sì-siù-á is therefore not just food at home, but also festival work that repeatedly appears on the street[^11].

Still, honest taste needs to connect with the modern body. A 2013 Mazu Daily News summary of festival snacks pointed out that _má luǎ_, melon seeds, and _cùn zhāo_ each have about 500 to 600 calories per 100 grams, and sugar-coated peanuts are close to 500 calories. The article also quoted the National Health Bureau's advice: recreational snacks and desserts shouldn't exceed 10% of daily calories, and sodium content should be noted[^6]. This isn't about turning the New Year's table into a nutrition lesson, but reminding us: the cultural weight of a plate of sweets can't cover the fact that it will actually be eaten.

Food safety is on the same table. The Keelung City Health Bureau's 2023 Spring Festival and Lantern Festival food inspection list includes peanut pieces tested for aflatoxin in November 2022, with results "in compliance with regulations"[^7]. Festival snacks thus gain another modern path: they're both the language of blessing and products that need labeling, testing, and responsible production.

## What's Left in the End Is Whose Memory

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Food inspection and calorie labeling seem to pull candies away from folklore, but actually the opposite is true. When festival snacks become purchasable, labelable, and testable commodities, blessings have a chance to keep circulating in today's families.

Festival candies don't need a neat conclusion. Putting one name, allusion, and taste sensation together reveals a less tidy culture: _shēng rén táng_ can wish people prosperity, _cùn zhāo_ can borrow a "zhāo" to ask for early blessings, _má luǎ_ can wish people to live long. At the same time, some candies might be disliked like plastic, or even forgotten entirely.

This is exactly where sì-siù-á is warmer than "traditional festival foods." The former isn't a completed list, but a verb: take, share with others, eat while chatting, ask for names, make homophones, and finally admit you don't like it. Next time you see that candy box during the festival, maybe don't rush to ask what each square's auspicious words mean. First ask the person beside you, which square they really want to eat.

The value of sì-siù-á isn't in awarding Taiwan's festival candies, but in turning a seemingly familiar plate of food back into a question: what do we leave behind—food, stories, or the afternoon we all disliked a certain candy?
