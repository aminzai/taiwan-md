> **30-second overview:** In Taiwan, a single "sorry" doesn't necessarily mean you've done something wrong — it might just be a way of getting by, opening a conversation, or drawing someone closer. A 2022 study of 90 NTU students found it appears more often in everyday or mildly offensive situations than "excuse me."[^1] A single "eh?" isn't unique to Taiwan — it's a human repair mechanism triggered when we don't hear clearly. Taiwan also calls @ "little mouse," and pronounces D as "pig/Zhu" when exchanging exam papers.[^2] [^3] [^4] These four expressions all point to one thing: language doesn't just convey content — it manages distance, identifies companions, and shortens the time needed for explanation.

In 2022, NTU Mandarin teaching researcher Chiu Chi-er combined 90 questionnaires to ask participants which of "excuse me," "sorry," or "sorry" they would choose in different situations. She arrived at a result that felt very much like everyday life, yet wasn't quite intuitive: "sorry" usually wasn't the strongest apology — instead, it appeared more often in everyday or mildly offensive situations.[^1]

You say "sorry" in front of the MRT doors, reply with an "eh?" on the phone, write "little mouse" in an email, and pronounce D as "pig" when exchanging exam papers. These phrases seem to have no shared history. One sounds like an apology, one like a mishearing, one is a symbolic nickname, and one sounds like a mispronunciation. But when you look at them together, you realize that Taiwanese colloquial speech is often doing the same thing: using the shortest sounds to handle distance, misunderstanding, and identity between people.

## "Sorry" Manages Distance Before Apologizing

"Excuse me" usually places responsibility on the speaker. "Sorry" acknowledges that something has caused inconvenience. "Sorry" is more like setting aside a small buffer zone for both parties. It can appear when you actually bump into someone, or before "excuse me," "excuse me," or "could you please help me?"

Chiu Chi-er's study targeted 18 to 25-year-old NTU students, collecting 90 questionnaires to observe how gender, intimacy level, social status, offense severity, and offense type influence the choice of apology language. The results showed that the use of these three words wasn't just synonym substitution. Intimacy level and offense severity changed how people judged the situation: "excuse me" appeared more often in the highest severity, "sorry" leaned toward everyday or higher severity, and "sorry" leaned toward everyday or lower severity.[^1]

This difference explains why "sorry" can shift from an apology to an opening line. The speaker may not have done anything wrong, but they first acknowledge that their presence might interrupt the other person's time. This phrase wraps a request in politeness and slightly disperses the pressure of refusal. If the other person agrees, things move forward. If they're not available, they don't have to deal with a heavy apology first.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Taiwanese people don't just apologize more often — they turn apology words into a revolving door. It can bring people into a conversation, or let them exit gracefully from an awkward moment.

This is also why translating "sorry" into a single "sorry" loses part of its meaning. Research shows that Taiwanese participants placed it in lower or everyday severity situations, and this doesn't mean the phrase lacks politeness. On the contrary, its power lies in not having to turn every small disturbance into a major event.[^1]

When a society places "sorry" at the boundaries of passing by, asking for directions, making requests, refusing, and thanking, the word is no longer just describing emotions. It begins to manage the distance of relationships. The person who says it lowers themselves slightly, and the listener gets an entry point to respond.

## "Eh?" Isn't Taiwan's Exclusive Repair Button — But It's the Most Familiar One

The Ministry of Education's Revised Mandarin Dictionary records the pronunciation of "eh" as ㄍㄜˊ and ㄏㄚˊ, with meanings primarily pointing to clams, geoduck, and frogs. Writing it as "eh?", stretching the tone, or saying "eh!" with surprise is another living path in spoken interaction.[^2]

In 2013, a cross-linguistic study put English "huh?" into conversation analysis. Researchers called these short sounds "repair initiators" — that is, "repair trigger words." When a listener doesn't hear clearly, doesn't understand, or needs the speaker to reorganize the previous sentence, they don't need to ask a full question. A short "huh?" can bounce the ball back, asking the speaker to repair the dialogue. The study observed similar forms and functions across many languages.[^3]

This discovery makes "eh?" even more interesting. It's not a mysterious password unique to Taiwanese people. As long as humans speak together, we need a low-cost sound to say "the previous sentence didn't reach its destination." Taiwanese daily life writes this sound into a fixed character, allowing it to appear in chat messages, memes, subtitles, and spoken language teaching.

"Eh?" can mean you really didn't hear clearly. "Eh!" can express surprise. "Eh……" might turn not understanding into a bit of helplessness. The character doesn't change — tone, length, and punctuation refocus the relationship. It's not like a complete "please say that again," nor is it a direct contradiction. It first suspends understanding, letting the other person decide whether to repeat, explain, or admit they spoke unclearly.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** What makes "eh?" most Taiwanese isn't that it belongs only to Taiwan — it's that Taiwanese people polish a sound all humans need for repair into a character everyone knows how to respond to.

"Eh?" also reminds us that spoken culture can't be understood by looking only at dictionaries. Dictionaries record standard pronunciations and word meanings, while interaction lets a single word grow new functions. When "eh?" appears at the beginning of a sentence, what it does might be closer to a button than a dictionary entry: press it, and the other person needs to reassemble what was just said.

## @ Becomes "Little Mouse" — Only Then Does the Technical Symbol Enter the Classroom

![SVG of the @ symbol in the public domain, symbolizing how technical symbols grow a "little mouse" name in Taiwan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/88/At_sign.svg)

_Image source: [Wikimedia Commons: At sign.svg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:At_sign.svg), Public domain. Images are embedded via external hotlinks only._

In the RFC 5322 email format, @ has a very precise job. It separates the local-part from the Internet domain — before it is part of the account, after it is part of the domain. The standard document calls it the at-sign and defines it as ASCII value 64.[^4]

Engineers need a separator. Children in school need something they can remember. BBC Chinese compiled different languages' names for @, listing Taiwan's "little mouse," mainland China's "circle A," Italy's snail, Greece's little duck, and so on. The technical function of the symbol is the same, but cultural memory gives it a different shape everywhere.[^5]

Ray Tomlinson put @ into the format of network email addresses in 1971, making this symbol — which rarely appeared in ordinary text — the bridge between accounts and domains.[^5] [^6] Later, Taiwanese people called it "little mouse" — not by modifying RFC, nor by adding an official standard to the symbol. This is a way of translating an abstract tool into a nearby animal for teaching.

![Taiwanese keyboard layout with Zhuyin and English letters](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Taiwan_keyboard.png)

_Image source: [Wikimedia Commons: Taiwan keyboard.png](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwan_keyboard.png), by Zhongzheng Road 711, CC BY-SA 3.0. Images are embedded via external hotlinks only, with attribution and license links retained._

The interesting thing about "little mouse" is that it preserves the feel of early computer education. Children don't need to understand domains, accounts, and network protocols first — just remembering that circle that looks like a mouse is enough to find it on the keyboard. The first step for technology to enter life is often not understanding the whole system, but finding a recognizable name for a strange symbol.

This name also leaves generational differences. Those familiar with early email education can say "please send it to little mouse" and let classmates find @ immediately. Those who encountered the internet later might more often say "at," or just read out the entire address. Both ways can complete the task, but the former carries a memory of Taiwanese campus and family computer classes.

## Why D Becomes "Pig" or "Zhu"

The Ministry of Education's Dictionary of Commonly Used Taiwanese Hokkien words lists "pig" as ti/tu. This Taiwanese pronunciation ti allows the sound of the English letter D to find a close Chinese equivalent in some Taiwanese campus colloquial speech.[^7]

A 2020 Sanlii News report compiled PTT discussions, mentioning that after high school exams, when exchanging answer sheets, some people pronounce D as "pig" to make B and D easier to distinguish. The same report also listed the formal English pronunciation Delta, as well as other ways people distinguish B and D. This shows it's a recognition technique spread in some campuses and online discussions — not the standard English pronunciation taught in schools, and it can't be generalized to mean all Taiwanese people speak this way.[^8]

"Zhu" is more like a listener using Mandarin characters to record this sound for convenience. To be more precise, it should be said that in Taiwanese colloquial speech, some people pronounce D close to the Taiwanese Hokkien "pig," and then write it down with "pig" or "Zhu." This difference is small, but it perfectly shows that spoken language and text don't always synchronize. The sound circulates in the classroom first, and Chinese characters find a readable shell for it afterward.

This should also be separated from "ABC Dog Bites Pig." The Republic of China's Overseas Chinese Affairs Commission's Global Chinese Network collects this Taiwanese nursery rhyme, with the full text from "ABC Dog Bites Pig" to "Grandpa Takes a Plane" and "Call a Doctor."[^9] Taiwan.md also has an article compiling cross-generational oral transmission of campus rhymes.[^10] D pronounced as "pig" can share phonetics and campus memory with this nursery rhyme, but this article discusses the recognition function when exchanging exam papers — not reintroducing the nursery rhyme.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** "D pronounced as pig" is not a joke about mispronunciation — it's a small workflow. When a group of people need to quickly exchange answers and avoid mixing up B and D, accents temporarily become tools.

Therefore, this expression doesn't need to be packaged as a myth of single origin. What the existing data can support is that it spread in some Taiwanese campuses and online discussions, borrowing the phonetic similarity of Taiwanese Hokkien "pig." As for which school started it first, which generation of teachers taught it earliest — without school history, interviews, or more complete corpus, the article should stop at the verifiable range.

## The Common Thread of These Four Words: Turning Uncertainty into Shared Reflexes

"Sorry" draws interpersonal distance closer. "Eh?" sends failed understanding back into the dialogue. "Little mouse" turns internet technology into an animal a child can see. "D pronounced as pig" turns phonetic differences into an immediate recognition code between classmates.

They're all small. No single word changes any system, and no single sound represents all Taiwanese people. They're worth writing about precisely because culture is often left behind not in ceremonies or declarations, but in the next second — whether to disturb others, whether to ask someone to repeat, how to pronounce a symbol, how to hand an answer to a classmate — becoming the body's first choice.

The sense of identity in Taiwanese spoken language doesn't come only from unique vocabulary. It comes from a group of people knowing when to say "sorry," knowing how long the tail of "eh?" can be, knowing @ isn't a mouse but can be called "little mouse," and knowing D's "pig" shouldn't be used as the standard answer in English class. These shared boundaries make language the lightest — and most solid — password in life.
