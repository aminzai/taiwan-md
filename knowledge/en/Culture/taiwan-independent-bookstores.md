> **30-Second Overview:** Independent bookstores in Taiwan are not a fixed store format but an operating style that ties together book curation, spatial scale, and public relations. They might be located in basements of university towns like Taipei or hidden in old streets such as Guanshan, alongside Changbin Highway, or within alleys of Penghu. While these stores sell books, they more often facilitate encounters between strangers and preserve a language that can continue to be spoken for the locality. The challenges—discounts, logistics, rent, and demographics—vary across book streets in Chongqing South Road, humanities bookstores in Wenluo Ting, and micro-spaces in local towns and islands; yet, they all answer the same question: how does a place enable books and readers who are hard to see to meet? This value cannot replace financial viability, but it explains why, after online discounts and digital reading, some people are still willing to stake their rent, time, and lives on a set of bookshelves.

## Don't Just Photograph the Bookstore as a Postcard

When Taiwanese discuss independent bookstores, they often start with an aesthetically pleasing image: wooden shelves in an old house, a table by the window, or the owner recommending a hard-to-find book on social media. These images are not wrong, but they quickly turn the bookstore into a tourist backdrop. The real difficulty lies in how such a space pays rent daily, acquires books, arranges labor, and maintains a trust in reading during an afternoon when no one enters.

When _Reporter_ introduced ten independent bookstores in 2019, it called them "guides." The article documented not the same decor, but how each bookstore guided readers into issues such as disability employment, migrant reading, women's issues, wrongful conviction advocacy, gender, agriculture, and local culture. Chanlan Times brought Southeast Asian languages and migrant reading needs into bookstores; Yuli placed books, food, and social support in one space; and Huwei-suo salons maintained a living room for discussing local and public issues in Yunlin.[^1]

The commonality among these cases is not that every store makes much money, nor that every store meets a single standard of "independence." They are more like answers from the owners to their locality: what reading is missing here, which people are unseen by existing bookstores, and what problems require a room where one can sit down and talk.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> **If independent bookstores are only understood as small and beautiful storefronts, their real work will be missed. The curation the owner does every day is actually deciding for a place which issues are worth putting in front of people and which unfamiliar experiences can be taken home.**

## Ask "What is Independent?" First, Not How Many There Are

The definition of an independent bookstore is not simple. In 2017, when Openbook interviewed Chen Lung-hao (陳隆昊) and Liao Ying-liang (廖英良) from the Taiwan Independent Bookstore Culture Association, the association was preparing a tour of bookstores across Taiwan. The interviewees noted discrepancies in the number of stores known to the government because some shops' names did not suggest they sold books, some bookshops primarily sold stationery, and some stores were not fully registered but still served readers. Consequently, the association divided Taiwan into ten regions, hoping to understand the types, scale, and operational difficulties of bookstores through store visits.[^2]

"Independent" does not simply mean having no government subsidies. Commentary published by _Reporter_ pointed out that bookstores have relationships with publishers, distributors, agencies, NGOs, media, authors, and readers; what truly matters is whether they can maintain their own curation and operational judgment through interaction. A store that has never received a subsidy is not necessarily more independent. Conversely, accepting public resources does not mean the owner has abandoned autonomy.[^3]

This difference is crucial because "independent bookstore" is sometimes treated as a moral title—as if receiving subsidies, selling coffee, or taking local government contracts means one is no longer pure. However, getting a book to a small town often requires the logistics of cooperatives, support from public policy, and even income supplements from coffee, stationery, classes, or commissions to subsidize book sales. Purity does not automatically guarantee survival, nor does survival equal cultural betrayal.

## This Story Is Much Older Than "Literary Youth Trends"

The modern bookstore history of Taiwan has a long precursor. Historical records compiled by Openbook point out that Ruicheng Bookstore (瑞成書局) was established in Taichung in 1912; Wenshui Cultural Bookshop (文化書局) was founded by Chiang Wei-shui (蔣渭水) in 1926; Central Bookstore (中央書局) appeared in 1927; and International Bookstore (國際書局) opened by Hsieh Hsueh-hung (謝雪紅) in 1929. These bookstores did not just sell paper; they put religion, knowledge, social movements, and political ideologies into accessible publications. Bookstores of the 1920s already functioned as centers for enlightenment, gathering, and transmitting new knowledge.[^4]

After 1949, Chongqing South Road gradually formed a new book street. Publishing was restricted during martial law, yet bookstores remained places where readers sought textbooks, classical novels, foreign classics, humanities anthologies, and limited new knowledge. Used book stalls, periodical stands, and specialized bookstores offered different levels of entry; these spaces cannot be simplified as a free market nor as political resistance, but they did allow certain texts to circulate in an environment of censorship and resource scarcity.[^4]

Tangshan Bookstore (唐山書店), which opened in 1982, is another important thread. Before the term "independent bookstore" was common in Taiwan, it focused on humanities and social sciences, leftist ideologies, and niche publications. After Eslite (誠品) opened in 1989, large bookstores redefined reading spaces through events, lectures, curation, and spatial design. These two types are not the same, but they collectively show that a bookstore is not just a place where books are shelved waiting for checkout; it can be an arena that intervenes in thought and life.[^4] [^5]

Wenluo Ting connected bookstores with university towns, social movements, and dense alleys. Tangshan, Donghai Bookstore (東海書苑), and other shops formed a walkable knowledge network. The importance of this network is not the number of stores itself, but that different owners' judgments on book selection, issues, and readers are adjacent, allowing a reader to traverse several different intellectual scales in one afternoon.[^2]

## After 2000, Ideals Must Learn to Account

The digital age has made acquiring books faster, but it has not eliminated the costs of bookstores. An industry analysis by Openbook in 2023 used bookstore gross profit margin, procurement discounts, and monthly revenue estimates to point out that small bookstores selling books near retail price might still lack enough profit margin to cover rent, labor, and logistics. The article mentioned that when large chains can procure at lower discounts, independent bookstores struggle to survive solely on new book sales. This is the industry analysis of the article and should not be rewritten as a government census, but it clearly illustrates how price wars become daily mathematics inside the store.[^6]

Therefore, many shops began adding work related to books that goes beyond just selling books. They host lectures, publish, take local collections, run cafes, sell stationery, or turn the bookstore into a B&B, exhibition space, or community classroom. These actions do not necessarily make the shop richer, but they transform the bookstore from a single retail point into an intersection of various incomes and relationships.

The "Small Book Vegetable Patch" (小間書菜) recorded by Openbook places used books alongside friendly agricultural products, while Pasela'an Slow Bookstore (緩緩書屋) is closer to an indigenous cultural and historical studio, serving the locality through books, mother tongues, songs, and fieldwork. These cases remind us that composite operation is not turning a bookstore into a general store; it requires the owner to find a relationship between the book and the place that can genuinely support each other.[^6]

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> **It is too quick to conclude that treating coffee, classes, or local contracts as "unrelated business" for a bookstore. What is more worth asking is whether these incomes allow curation, reading activities, and employee labor to continue, and who bears the extra work of composite operation.**

## From City to Town: Bookstores Change the Entrance

After the rise of regional revitalization discourse, bookstores are often described as tools that "light up a place." This narrative is compelling but still requires grounding in detail. A 2023 report by Smile Taiwan documented Guanshan's Lì-kǎnnei (里壠案內) Organic Bookstore, the Haiyu Village in Taimali, the Watershed Bookstore in Piling, the Practice Bookstore in Hualien Xincheng, and Niannian You Chao in Penghu. The commonality among these spaces is not that they share the same business model, but that they first answer what the locality lacks: an entrance to exchange books, a place for children to read, a room that brings environmental education into daily life, or a space where residents can talk about their home again.[^7]

The Book Porridge (書粥) in Changbin, Taitung, is marked as an independent bookstore in a remote village on the Commons file page. In this image, the bookshelves are not completely separated from the road and the interior; readers near the tables can still see the outside trees and street. It is not a miniature model of a "rural city bookstore," but a concrete example of how a bookstore connects with daily movement.[^10]

Here, "place" cannot be written as a naturally harmonious community. When bookstores enter small towns, they may also encounter contradictions such as aging populations, insufficient reading demographics, rent, transportation, and tourism. Whether a bookstore survives depends on the owner's professionalism, local relationships, public support, and cash flow, not just having a beautiful old house. True locality is often when the owner is willing to listen to the place for a long time and gradually adjust curation and activities based on these relationships. Over time, the event posters, reader comments, local publications, and oral histories inside become a small archive, letting later people know what this place cared about, and allowing the bookstore not just to preserve books published by others, but to gradually preserve memories formed with the locality, leaving clues for future generations to read.

## A Bookshelf is Also an Edit

What makes independent bookstores difficult for e-commerce to replicate is not just having someone fetch your book, but having someone make a judgment for you first. When online platforms push books through classification, rating, and recommendation algorithms, independent bookstores use a slower kind of editing. An owner might place a niche poetry collection at the entrance next to local history, gender studies, or a newly published novel. A reader comes to buy what they originally intended but encounters an issue on the shelves that they hadn't searched for yet.

A 2026 tour of bookstores in Zhongshan District, Taipei by Openbook provided several detailed examples. Shi Sheng Huo (詩生活) curated about 95% literature; Xin Ge France Bookstore (信鴿法國書店) focuses on French books, French literature, and Taiwan-France exchange. One bookstore has staff and reader sticky notes passing messages on the books; Pengding places independent publishing, design, exhibitions, and selection together; while Kuifu Ju reinterprets local memory of Chifeng Street using Taiwanese history, old maps, and antique furniture. These bookstores do not need to be judged as more "truly independent"; they demonstrate how curation forms different public entrances.[^8]

An English report by Taiwan Panorama also turned the lens outside the city, introducing how independent bookstores function as nodes for reading and exchange in local societies. The value of this English source is not in labeling Taiwanese bookstores internationally, but in reminding us that the cultural function of a bookstore can be translated to external readers without erasing its relationship with transportation, demographics, local work, and community organization.[^9]

The image of Zhiyin Bookstore (知音書店) in Penghu Ma-gong also presents another scale. The Commons file page records that this store was established in 2019 on Shude Road, Guangfu Township; the bookshelves and narrow interior space in the image show that independent bookstores do not necessarily require large areas or complete urban displays. They can start from a community-recognizable facade and gradually accumulate reader relationships.[^11]

## Policy Can Stop the Bleeding, But Cannot Replace Bookstore Judgment

After the Ministry of Culture was established in 2012, independent bookstores were more clearly placed into cultural policy discussions. Interview records from Openbook show that the Ministry of Culture once used independent bookstores as a theme for national cultural affairs meetings and subsequently compiled resources to support them. A 2022 review of the publishing industry recorded the Cultural Ministry promoting the "Independent Bookstore Dream Realization Project" in 2013, followed by the establishment of the Friendly Publishing Cooperative (友善書業合作社) in 2014, attempting to address issues like small bookstores having low book procurement volume, high logistics costs, and unstable supply.[^2] [^5]

The role of policy is to spread out some costs that were originally borne solely by the owner, providing a space for new stores to experiment, and viewing bookstores as micro-cultural centers of the community, not just retail businesses on paper. However, policy cannot curate books for the owner, cannot schedule staff, nor can it guarantee local residents will buy books. If subsidies only pursue event frequency, they might turn small shops into short-term contract executors without leaving stable readers or labor conditions.

In 2024, the Ministry of Culture issued a notice regarding the dispute over visiting committee members recommended by the Independent Bookstore Culture Association, stating that the association was responsible for the physical bookstore visit service and immediately halting the visits and review work of the related members. This announcement is not the whole story of independent bookstore culture, but it reminds us that when bookstores enter public policy, governance issues arise regarding who they represent, how they are selected, and who has the right to judge cultural value. Writing the subsidy system as unidirectional cultural goodwill ignores the need for transparency, prudence, and verifiable procedures.[^12]

## The Future of Bookstores Is Not Returning to the Past

Taiwanese bookstore history has several clear turning points. Early bookstores linked publishing, education, and social movements; post-war book streets provided textbooks, used books, and new knowledge; chain stores in the 1980s brought bright and abundant displays; Eslite incorporated events and space into bookstores in the 1990s; online channels rewrote price and logistics after 2000; and more micro-bookstores entered local areas in the 2010s. Each change was not the complete disappearance of the previous type, but a reallocation of its original function.[^4] [^5]

Therefore, the future of independent bookstores does not have to be replicating the golden revenue of the 1990s, nor does it need to pretend to return to before the internet. What is more worth preserving is the bookstore's capacity as a public intermediary. It can ensure that a book is selected, interpreted, and placed within a local life context, allowing readers and strangers to see an issue simultaneously. When collaborating with schools, libraries, or community groups, bookstores can extend their curation judgment beyond the shop door, becoming starting points for reading classes, local walks, and public discussions. These tasks cannot all be measured by sales figures, but they also cannot escape the calculation of wages, rent, logistics, and working hours.

For readers, supporting independent bookstores is not just taking photos in the store, nor does it mean buying expensive hardcovers every time. It can mean purchasing a book carefully selected by the owner, participating in an event that was prepared well without relying on traffic, respecting the store's rules, or asking why a place lost a reading entrance after the bookstore closes. For the government and publishing industry, support is not just subsidies or promotion; it is building a fairer supply chain so that small shops are not forced to work under worse conditions simply due to low procurement volume. For schools and libraries, bookstores can be partners in collaborative purchasing, thematic curation, and reading education, allowing students to see how books are selected, introduced, and placed into life, rather than just appearing in exam scores or borrowing statistics.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> **What is most worth preserving about independent bookstores is not a fixed wooden shelf, but the capacity for a place to still choose, interpret, and exchange knowledge autonomously. Even as storefronts change and books circulate digitally, this problem of public mediation remains.**

Taiwanese independent bookstore culture is therefore not a simple story of "we are happy because there are many bookstores," nor is it a tragic tale of "bookstores must disappear because e-commerce arrived." It is a history of continuous negotiation: owners negotiate between ideals and cash flow; readers negotiate between convenience and relationships; the government negotiates between aid and governance; and the locality negotiates between tourism, human connection, and long-term living.

When a book is placed on a shelf, the owner has already made a choice for the reader. When the reader walks in, they may change the bookstore with their own questions. This back-and-forth makes the bookstore a living room for a place, not because it is always quiet or always romantic, but because it remains willing to leave a chair for strangers.
