> **30-second overview:** The _Guidelines for Indigenous Peoples in Taiwan to Adopt Traditional Names_, published in 2025, summarizes the traditional naming systems of 16 ethnic groups into five structures. The part following the personal name may be a given name, clan name, house name, or a term that changes with the birth of a child. The Saisiyat surnames "Dou," "Feng," "Ri," "Si," and others preserve the history of clan names entering Han-style household registration. When reading these names, don't rush to split them into "surname + given name"; more importantly, recognize which kinship, household, and tribal relationship the name is pointing to.[^1]

Before the birth of his son, Si Nozayin was known as Si Nozayin. After the child was born, he changed his name to Syaman Rapongan, meaning "the father of Lapanwan." Here, names are not fixed labels, but ways in which a person is addressed by their family as they move through different stages of life.[^2]

Looking again at the Saisiyat surname "Feng." It appears to be a rare Han surname, but it may actually connect to the `baba:i` clan, kinship cooperation, ancestral sacrifice groups, and marriage taboos. If one only sees it as a "rare character," an entire history of the intersection between clan systems and household registration would be missed.[^3] [^4]

## Don't Look for Surnames First — First See Who the Name Points To

Taiwan's indigenous peoples do not have a single "surname before given name" rule shared across all ethnic groups. The five naming structures compiled in the current guidelines serve as a map for understanding structure, not as a template to apply to every individual. Practices within the same ethnic group may also differ by region, family, and contemporary choices.[^1] [^2]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Han-style names tend to ask first, "What is your surname?" Indigenous traditional naming systems often answer a different question first: "Whose child are you? Which household do you come from? Which clan are you connected to? And at which stage of life are you now?"

The Council of Indigenous Peoples summarizes the traditional naming systems of the 16 ethnic groups into the following five types. The same two- or three-part arrangement, when placed in different ethnic groups, may carry entirely different social meanings.[^2]

| Naming structure           | Common arrangement                     | Ethnic groups or communities listed by the government | What the latter part typically records                                                                                   |
| -------------------------- | -------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Kinship-linked naming      | Personal name + given name             | Atayal, Sediq, Taroko, Amis, Kavalan, Sakizaya        | Given names of parents or other relatives, placing the individual within the context of direct kinship.[^2]              |
| Kinship-linked clan naming | Personal name + given name + clan name | Some Amis communities, Saisiyat                       | In addition to the given name, there is clan identification and social position.[^2] [^5]                                |
| House-name system          | Personal name + house name             | Paiwan, Rukai, Bunun                                  | Household affiliation, inheritance, and social relationships.[^1] [^2]                                                   |
| Clan-name system           | Personal name + clan name              | Buri, Zou, Shao, Kanakanavu, Lanyu                    | Family history, marriage taboos, and group identification.[^2]                                                           |
| Filial naming              | Title + eldest sibling's name          | Yamabi                                                | Parents or grandparents change their names upon the birth of the eldest child, responding to the stage of life.[^1] [^2] |

## Miru, Kaliting, Pacidal: Every Part of the Name Tells a Different Story

The name of Atayal artist Mi Luo Hahong (Miru Hayung): Miru is the personal name, and Hayung is the given name. Official introductions mention that Miru is associated with painting and studying, and Hayung may refer to pine trees. This is an example of a personal name linked to a given name.[^2]

Amis singer and host Ado Kaliting Bajilat (Ado' Kaliting Pacidal) has three components: Ado' is the personal name, Kaliting is the mother's name, and Pacidal is the clan name. According to the Council of Indigenous Peoples' data, Pacidal means "sun" and is connected to the clan's guardian deity.[^2]

Paiwan person Ljaucu Zingrur: Zingrur is the house name. House names can identify which household a person belongs to and may involve house inheritance and social hierarchy. When leaving one's hometown or marrying across clans, the house names of Paiwan, Rukai, and Bunun may also be adjusted.[^1] [^2]

Buri writer Tulbus Tamapima: Tamapima is the clan name. The Council of Indigenous Peoples records that in daily interaction, people usually address each other by personal name. Only in situations requiring confirmation of marriage taboos, such as first meeting or proposing marriage, is the full clan name spoken. Thus, the name simultaneously preserves the boundaries of who one can and cannot marry.[^2]

## "Dou" and "Feng" Are Not Strange Characters: Clan Clues Hidden in Saisiyat Han Surnames

Saisiyat society is based on patrilineal clans. The Council of Indigenous Peoples points out that there are 18 clans, each with its own name. Naming materials can be drawn from plants, natural phenomena, and physical bodily phenomena. Clans then form mutual aid groups, sacrifice groups, and alliance relationships, and follow specific marriage taboos.[^3]

During the Qing Dynasty, the Saisiyat clan system encountered the concept of Han surnames. Clan names were gradually transformed into Han surnames through phonetic translation or semantic selection, forming surnames such as Dou, Zhao, Zhu, Feng, Gao, Pan, Qian, Gen, Zhang, Xia, Xie, Ri, Si, Qiong, Hu, Chuan, Shi, Xue, Mo, and others. Later, some characters were also rewritten as Jie, Hu, and Zhan. These characters are clues left behind by clan names entering Han-style registration, and should not be used directly as a tool to determine an individual's clan affiliation.[^3]

The Saisiyat traditional name page on the Indigenous Traditional Name Recovery Website states that 14 clans correspond to 18 main Han surnames; when restoring traditional names in modern times, many people append their clan name at the end. Taking Obay Ataw Hayawan as an example: Obay is the personal name, Ataw is the father's name, and Hayawan is the clan name; the "ha" sound in Hayawan is close to "Xia," indicating one of the origins of the Xia surname.[^5]

| Clan name or traditional name context | Han surname clue                         | Simplification to avoid                                                                                                                                                                                                                   |
| ------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `tawtaw-azay`                         | Dou, Zhao                                | In some data, Dou and Zhao belong to the same clan correspondence, so one cannot infer different clans simply based on the Han character.[^6] [^7]                                                                                        |
| `ba:-ba:i’` / `baba:i`                | Feng; also seen as Feng, Kong, Bai, Dong | The official English page lists the Feng surname; academic research analyzes other character branches using "original surname / derived surname." This is a research classification, not a universally applicable identity rule.[^6] [^7] |
| `sawan`, `kas’ames`                   | Pan, Qian, Gen                           | Multiple Han surnames may become historical registration traces of the same clan or related kinship groups.[^6] [^7]                                                                                                                      |
| `tanohila:`                           | Ri, Zhang                                | Academic research uses the semantic meaning of the sun and the branching of Han character transcription to present the historical differences between clan names and registered characters.[^7]                                           |

## Why Would One Clan Leave Behind Several Han Surnames?

A 2019 study based on 2013 household registration data compiled some Saisiyat "clan name — Han surname" correspondences into quantifiable data. The study refers to the initially corresponding character as the "original surname" and subsequent different character forms as "derived surnames." This is an analytical tool of the study, suitable for examining registration history, and cannot replace the recognition of clans, families, or household administration for individual cases.[^7]

| Clan name spelling used in the 2013 study | Corresponding Han surnames and numbers     | Total | What this set of data indicates                                                                                                                             |
| ----------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ----: | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `baba:i`                                  | Feng 905, Feng 87, Kong 35, Bai 1, Dong 23 | 1,051 | In addition to the Feng surname, there are multiple character extensions; the number of Han surnames cannot be directly equated to the number of clans.[^7] |
| `tatawazay`                               | Dou 157, Zhao 789                          |   946 | Dou and Zhao belong to the same clan correspondence in this study.[^7]                                                                                      |
| `sawan (kas’ames)`                        | Pan 408, Qian 243, Gen 233                 |   884 | Multiple Han surnames may become historical registration traces of the same clan or related kinship groups.[^7]                                             |
| `minakes`                                 | Zhang 34, Zhang 341                        |   375 | Similar or different character forms may both originate from the transcription of clan names and administrative registration processes.[^7]                 |
| `tanohila:`                               | Ri 205, Zhang 103                          |   308 | Semantic meaning, phonetic transcription, and subsequent registration choices may all play a role simultaneously.[^7]                                       |
| `titiyon`                                 | Zhu 1,008                                  | 1,008 | Some clans in this study's data show only one main Han surname.[^7]                                                                                         |

> ⚠️ **Data reading reminder:** The year in the table above is **2013**, and the data source is the 2019 study, not today's population statistics. The value of the table lies in presenting the structure of "one clan may correspond to multiple Han surnames," not in using numbers to draw conclusions about anyone's identity.[^7]

The reason "Dou" and "Feng" are worth writing about is not because they are rare, but because they pull a surname that seems to be just a single character back to the long history of clans, marriage norms, ancestral sacrifice groups, and multiple changes in household registration systems.

## From the Qing Dynasty, the "He Surname" Movement to Single Listing of Clan Names: How Naming Systems Evolved to Today

Indigenous names are not redesigned on a blank form. The options on today's identification documents carry the layered traces of different regimes, different household registration systems, and indigenous movements.[^7] [^8]

| Time                   | Naming system events                                                                                                                                                                                                  | Impact on names                                                                                                                                                                            |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Qing Dynasty era       | Saisiyat clan names were combined with Han surname concepts through phonetic translation or semantic selection.[^3]                                                                                                   | Characters such as Dou, Feng, Ri, and Si became visible clan clues.                                                                                                                        |
| 1940                   | Research records indicate that after the Japanese colonial period's surname-changing movement, the Saisiyat people adopted 97 "He" surnames; the movement was terminated within five years due to a change in regime. | The He surname brought new divisions, but the study believes it had relatively little effect on the confusion of old Han surnames and original clans.                                      |
| December 1945          | The "Regulations for the Restoration of Original Names by the People of Taiwan Province" required that within three months, names be "self-determined by reference to Chinese naming conventions."                    | Rapid and comprehensive Han-style name registration became the background for many families' name transitions.                                                                             |
| 1995–2006              | The Ministry of the Interior and the _Enforcement Rules of the Surname Act_ underwent three amendments.                                                                                                               | Indigenous name registration gradually stopped forcing the use of Han names and allowed Romanized clan names to be listed alongside.                                                       |
| May 2024               | Amendments to the _Surname Act_ were passed.                                                                                                                                                                          | Indigenous peoples may use indigenous scripts as standalone surnames; name changes based on cultural customs and not due to personal factors are not counted toward the name-change limit. |
| January and April 2025 | Related operational guidelines were amended, and the _Guidelines for Indigenous Peoples in Taiwan to Adopt Traditional Names_ were published.                                                                         | The reference framework for administrative procedures and cultural customs became more complete, emphasizing the diverse structures of traditional names.                                  |

## Law Handles Registration; Culture Handles Relationships

The current _Surname Act_ clearly stipulates that indigenous name registration shall be conducted according to cultural customs, and indigenous scripts may be used, as well as listed alongside Chinese names or Han names. After the 2024 amendment, indigenous scripts can be listed as standalone surnames. Name changes based on cultural customs and not due to personal factors are also not counted toward the general name-change limit.[^8] [^10]

However, regulations do not reduce clan knowledge to a checklist. The Ministry of the Interior's _Operational Guidelines for Indigenous Peoples in Taiwan to Restore Traditional Names and Correct Names_ stipulate that one may apply to restore Chinese or indigenous script traditional names, list indigenous scripts alongside, and correct surnames and parental names. For cases where kinship surnames are inconsistent due to initial household registration at the time of restoration, applicants may submit documents such as household registration investigation records from the Japanese colonial period or copies of initial household registration. If no household registration investigation records from the Japanese colonial period are available, two or more older related relatives may provide testimony.[^9]

The importance of these procedures lies in this: a rare Han surname or an indigenous language term cannot be taken as proof of naming eligibility on its own. Whether a traditional name conforms to cultural customs still needs to return to the context of ethnicity, family data, tribal knowledge, and household administration verification. When there are doubts, household administration practice may request experts or elders of the respective ethnic group to assist in confirmation.[^1] [^9]

## The Dot Is Presentation; Relationships Are the Rule

The dot "・" is often seen as a marker of indigenous names, but it is only a way of presentation. Household administration practice generally respects the individual's choice of using dots or spaces for word separation. More importantly, the arrangement of the name should conform to the cultural customs of the ethnic group the individual belongs to, rather than catering to outsiders' imagination of "how indigenous names should look."[^9] [^10]

Restoring traditional names is not about reversing history. The Japanese colonial period, post-war household registration, inter-clan marriages, migration, and long-term use of Han names have all meant that each family's path back to traditional naming systems is different. Researchers compiling the _Indigenous Name Genealogy_ do so precisely to provide traceable data for families that have lost their clues. In the end, how to name still needs to respect tribal knowledge and personal choice.[^7] [^11]

The person after the name may be a father, mother, eldest child, clan, or household. Only when readers see "Dou" and "Feng" not merely as rare Han character surnames, but as a compressed map of relationships, does the naming rule of indigenous peoples truly begin to emerge.
