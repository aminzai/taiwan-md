> **30-Second Overview:** The Taiwan Lantern Festival adopts a rotating host city model, reorganizing cultural and tourism resources in different locations each year. Originating from Taipei's Lantern Festival on the Lantern Festival in 1990, it shifted to a rotating hosting system among counties and cities starting in 2001. Within a span of about two weeks, the main lantern, lantern zones, performances, transportation, and city brand are recombined, transforming a traditional festival into a public stage for local creativity, crowd governance, and imagining the future. ## From Taipei's Lantern Festival to Nationwide Rotation

The Lantern Festival has long possessed rich local forms in Taiwan. Temple courtyard lanterns, performance troupes, lantern riddles, night processions, and family reunions are each embedded in the rhythms of different communities. The distinctiveness of the modern Taiwan Lantern Festival lies in its re编排ment of these local customs into a national-level event coordinated by the central government, hosted by local authorities, and participated in by audiences across the island. Historical records from the Tourism Bureau of the Ministry of Transportation and Communications (TOC) and related studies identify 1990 as the critical starting point for the modern Taiwan Lantern Festival system. [^1][^2]

Early activities were centered in Taipei. Historical accounts from the Taipei City Government indicate that in 1990, the Tourism Bureau held the Lantern Festival in Taipei, renaming it the Taipei Lantern Festival the following year. This history reveals that today's nationwide rotating system was gradually established through years of accumulation, institutional adjustment, and local participation, building a national brand. [^3]

2001 marked an institutional turning point. The festival began to be hosted in rotation by different counties and cities, making hosting rights part of local governments' efforts to compete for city visibility and tourism resources. Taipei continues to host its own Taipei Lantern Festival, but the venue for the national Taiwan Lantern Festival began to move away from the fixed capital, entering the railways, high-speed rail, riverbanks, port areas, and industrial parks of different cities. [^2][^3]

The significance of city rotation goes beyond bringing tourists to different places. Each move requires localities to answer a question: What does this city want the outside world to see? Taichung once linked the lantern festival with Houli, Wuri, and city transportation. Tainan integrated ancient capital history, National Science Park industries, and the imagery of the Year of the Dragon into the main lantern and promotional materials. In 2024, Tainan's main visual identity was established around the ancient capital of Fucheng and the Year of the Dragon imagery; in 2025, Taoyuan combined airplane windows with lanterns, transforming the mobility experience of an airport city into festival visuals. [^6][^7][^9][^10] These cases show that the "nationwide" nature of the lantern festival can only be realized through local differences.

## How the Main Lantern Becomes Collective Memory

The main lantern is the most recognizable visual core of the Taiwan Lantern Festival. It is typically massive, requiring the handling of structure, wind resistance, lighting, dynamic mechanics, and nighttime safety. Research on main lanterns indicates that a main lantern appeared in 1990, and from 1991 onwards, designs gradually used the zodiac animal of the year as the basis for their form. Around 2019, main lantern designs began more explicitly combining local elements, city concepts, and contemporary visual vocabularies. [^4]

The zodiac provides an entry point understandable across Taiwan. Regardless of whether the host location is in the north, south, or east, the year's animal allows audiences to quickly identify the festival's timing. However, the zodiac is only a common language and cannot encompass the full content. The differences in each year's main lantern stem from how localities handle materials, proportions, colors, landscapes, and stories. When the main lantern is no longer just an enlarged zodiac sculpture, it begins to serve as a way for the city to introduce itself.

The main lantern also changes the way audiences view. During the day, people might first see steel frames, covering materials, and surrounding circulation paths. At night, lighting programs, music, and crowds transform the object into an event. Research compiling the forms, structures, and covering materials of past main lanterns notes that steel frames, stainless steel perforated plates, grating sheets, LED optical materials, and recyclable materials have all played roles in different editions. These technical choices simultaneously reflect the safety requirements of large-scale public art and the aesthetics of the times. [^4]

## A City Exhibition Curated by Government

The Taiwan Lantern Festival is often described as a tourism event, but its operation is closer to a short-term, government-curated city exhibition. The exhibition area has no permanent walls, yet it features temporary entrances, routes, service desks, stages,警戒 lines, and layered lighting. These facilities switch the daily city into a cultural scene that can be viewed intensively. Audiences see lights and performances; organizers face the problem of reallocating public space in the short term. Researchers analyzing government procurement data from 2016 to 2020 found that each year's lantern festival involves 140 to 234 bidding projects, covering services, engineering, hardware, performances, transportation, security, and environmental creation. These numbers remind us that behind the lantern festival's lighting effects lies a massive administrative and supply chain system. [^2]

The governance of large-scale events involves time, space, and data. The host city must complete venue, lantern zone, and transportation arrangements before the Lunar New Year, coordinating with school winter breaks, return-home waves, and other local festivals. Lantern zones cannot pursue only density; they must also arrange entrances/exits, emergency evacuation, accessible routes, rest areas, restrooms, and family needs. Visitor counts, peak hours, shuttle efficiency, and accident handling data will influence the preparation of the next host city. Placing these tasks within a single governance framework allows us to understand that the lantern festival is not an exhibition that operates naturally once installations are set up.

Research indicates that starting in 2017, the Taiwan Lantern Festival announced host cities in advance, giving localities more time to prepare. This system also changed the role of local governments. Localities no longer only took over execution a few months before the event; they could also incorporate the lantern festival into discussions on urban planning, tourism routes, transportation infrastructure, and local revitalization earlier. [^2]

## Lantern Zones Let Localities Be Seen Again

The main lantern concentrates attention; lantern zones disperse the story. The arrangement of lantern zones also generates a special way of reading the city. Audiences follow signs, walking from station exits to riverbanks or parks, and identifying another landscape through one artwork. Markets, old streets, temples, industrial zones, and newly developed areas, originally scattered on the map, are placed within the same nighttime narrative due to the lantern festival. For first-time visitors, this may be an entry point to understanding the city. For residents, it may be a way to see familiar places anew. Each lantern zone typically places local industries, historical landscapes, folk beliefs, school communities, and artistic creations on the same nighttime map. As audiences walk through the lantern zone, they move from one artwork to another, switching between different scales. One moment they see the national-level main lantern; the next, they may linger beside a local group's work, then enter a commercial district, station, or riverbank.

This spatial arrangement can create local economic value, but it may also oversimplify localities. If a city only enlarges specialties into mascots and compresses history into a few slogans, the lantern festival risks becoming a one-off brand packaging exercise. Conversely, if lantern zones give local creators, schools, communities, and traditional crafts sufficient narrative space, the festival may leave cultural relationships lasting beyond two weeks.

Planning data for the 2020 Taichung Lantern Festival shows that the event integrated the lantern festival with tourist attractions in the Houli area, shuttle services, and guide arrangements. This approach directed the flow of large-scale events toward broader local routes rather than keeping everyone solely within the lantern zone. The lantern festival thus becomes an opportunity for the city to reintroduce itself and a stress test for transportation and tourism systems to share the burden. [^6]

## The Encounter of Traditional Customs and Technological Display

The Taiwan Lantern Festival does not preserve tradition as a static specimen. The prayers, reunions, and nighttime strolling of the Lantern Festival have always changed with urban life. Large-scale lantern festivals accelerate this change, placing handmade lanterns and digital projections side by side in the same venue, and having traditional artisans, designers, engineering teams, and software developers co-produce the festival experience. Tradition and modernity meet on the same night road, allowing different technologies and memories to adjust to each other. Recent lantern exhibitions have added projection, sensors, interactive installations, drones, augmented reality, and mobile guides, transforming audiences from distant viewers into people who can trigger light/shadow, collect information, or participate in tasks. Research from _Frontiers in Psychology_ uses the augmented reality display of the Taiwan Lantern Festival as a case study, analyzing through 200 questionnaires how digital interaction affects knowledge understanding, attitudes, creativity, and usage intention. The results show that if technology integrates with learning content, it may ensure cultural experiences go beyond just taking photos. [^5]

The addition of technology to performances does not automatically mean innovation is achieved. High-brightness screens, dense audio-visual effects, and real-time interaction may make it harder for audiences to feel paper, bamboo, metal, hand-painted details, and the night itself. When evaluating the value of technology, the key lies in whether it helps audiences better understand local stories, whether it provides participation methods for people of different ages and physical conditions, and whether the content can be preserved, researched, and reused after the event ends.

In this sense, the lantern festival is also a field for informal education. For schools, the lantern festival can serve as an outdoor extension of local curriculum. Teachers can have students observe the main lantern's materials, explain city history, or compare how different lantern zones use sound and space. For families, viewing lanterns often simultaneously includes elders explaining customs, children operating mobile phones, and family members searching together for designated lantern groups. These small interactions make cultural transmission a shared walking experience rather than knowledge only experts can explain. Children may learn about a river, a station, or a local craft in the lantern zone. Adults may understand how large cities manage crowd flow, light, sound, and public safety while walking and waiting. Festival learning does not necessarily happen in textbook form; it may also be completed in the moment a family discusses "where to go next."

## Costs Beyond Tourist Numbers

The tourism benefits of the lantern festival cannot be calculated solely at the event site. Hotels, catering, commercial districts, transportation, and local attractions may benefit, but revenue distribution often depends on lantern zone locations, opening hours, shuttle arrangements, and visitor stay length. If the city government can connect official routes, local shops, and community guides, audiences may shift from one-time check-ins to longer city exploration. Conversely, if transportation nodes and main lantern zones are too far apart, or if promotion focuses only on a few landmarks, remote areas may still bear the event's costs without seeing corresponding benefits.

The lantern festival also changes the city's nighttime usage rhythm. Riverbanks, station plazas, school surroundings, and industrial zones, usually used only for commuting or leisure, become high-density public spaces in the short term. This transformation allows residents to see places they usually ignore, but may also cause noise, light pollution, garbage, and traffic control. If event planning includes resident consultation, clear construction schedules, and departure transportation, the festival is more likely to become a shared experience rather than an external event locals must temporarily avoid.

The Taiwan Lantern Festival has strong tourism appeal. Research has compiled the phenomenon of the festival attracting over ten million visitors in stages over a two-week period, noting that local lantern festivals, food, and other concurrent activities drive each other. Visitor numbers thus become the most commonly used success metric for governments, media, and host cities. [^2]

However, visitor counts only explain how many people are counted; they do not necessarily explain whether culture is understood, whether local businesses truly benefit, or whether residents are willing to endure the noise, traffic jams, and garbage brought by the event. If host cities only pursue larger numbers, hardware scale and promotional expenses may increase simultaneously, while residents' lives and environmental capacity may not receive equal attention.

Sustainable governance requires breaking down these issues. Regarding energy, one must consider lighting hours, equipment reuse, and the destination of dismantled components. Regarding transportation, one must handle shuttle buses, walking routes, and peak pressure on railways and highways. Regarding materials, one must track whether main lanterns and lantern zone installations can be repaired, recycled, or transformed into future performances. Regarding publicness, one must ask whether the elderly, people with disabilities, children, foreign residents, and those unfamiliar with the city can participate safely and with dignity.

These questions do not weaken the cultural value of the lantern festival. On the contrary, they bring the festival back from beautiful results to concrete public work. The reason lights are visible is because someone arranged power, transportation, cleaning, ambulance services, volunteers, and order. Incorporating this labor into the narrative prevents the festival from being reduced to just photos of the main lantern.

## An Ever-Rewritten Image of Taiwan

The legacy of the lantern festival exists not only in photos of the main lantern. It also exists in the tacit knowledge accumulated by workers. Which materials suit the humid winter, which routes allow wheelchair users to avoid stairs, which stage direction prevents excessive sound reflection, which entrance needs an added medical station—these are usually not known from a single plan. If these experiences are compiled into public operational records, the next city need not start from zero and can instead use resources for more creative places. Host cities can organize lantern zone design, volunteer training, public art works, guide texts, and transportation data into reusable city archives. This data helps the next host understand which routes cause bottlenecks and allows local creators to retain work context after the event ends. If all results remain only in short-term promotional videos, the city cannot accumulate its own festival knowledge.

For local creators, participating in the lantern festival also involves working conditions and attribution rights. Large-scale events often have clear main visuals and national brands, while community groups may produce works with smaller budgets. If curation and procurement systems clearly explain creation fees, maintenance responsibilities, work preservation, and licensing methods, local culture will not be treated merely as consumable decoration. These institutional details are less eye-catching than the main lantern but directly determine who can leave their name in the festival and sustain their work.

The most distinctive feature of the Taiwan Lantern Festival is that it possesses both fixed and fluid characteristics. Fixed are the sense of time: the Lantern Festival, nighttime lantern viewing, prayers, and reunions. Fluid are the host cities, lantern zone configurations, material technologies, artistic languages, and local stories. It is not like a permanent building, yet it can establish a cultural scene recognizable across the island in a short time.

Starting from Taipei in 1990, to the county-city rotation after 2001, the Taiwan Lantern Festival allows the name "Taiwan" to represent not only the central administrative organ but also different localities introducing themselves to each other in rotation. Tainan can speak through its ancient capital and industries; Taichung through transportation nodes and living circles; Taoyuan through airports, mobility, and new city images. Each edition of the lantern festival asks: When a city is seen by the whole island, does it want to leave a scenic spot, or a relationship that residents can continue to use? [^6][^7][^8]

Therefore, the Taiwan Lantern Festival cannot be summarized merely as "viewing lanterns." It transforms folklore into public culture, cities into exhibition venues, and administrative systems into an annual experiment of audience experience. More worth asking is what happens after the event ends. Whether demolition engineering preserves reusable materials, whether temporary transportation facilities return to their original state, whether volunteer and resident opinions enter the review process, and whether local creators can obtain work records—these will determine whether the lantern festival is a one-time consumption or can accumulate into the city's cultural capacity.

The public nature of large-scale festivals is also manifested in who can participate. Some can walk for long hours on weeknights; others need accessible routes, clear rest points, and predictable shuttles. Some are familiar with the host city; others are visiting Taiwan for the first time and must rely on multilingual information and simple, easy-to-understand guides. If the lantern festival imagines participants as a single type of young tourist, many families, the elderly, people with disabilities, and foreign residents will be excluded. Incorporating accessibility into lantern zone design is a basic condition for public cultural activities, not an added service.

After the lantern festival ends, installations are dismantled, lights go out, but how the host city handles local memory, public space, environmental costs, and cultural participation is the true long-term legacy left by this moving stage.
