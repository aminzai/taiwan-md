> **30-Second Overview:** The Taiwan Lantern Festival utilizes a rotating host city model, reorganizing cultural and tourism resources in different locations each year. It developed from the 1990 Lantern Festival in Taipei and has been hosted by various counties and cities since 2001. The main lantern, lantern zones, performances, transportation, and urban branding are reassembled over about two weeks, transforming a traditional holiday into a public stage for local creativity, crowd management, and imagining the future.[^1][^2][^3]

## From the Taipei Lantern Festival to Nationwide Rotation

The Lantern Festival has rich local forms in Taiwan. Temple grounds lanterns, parade floats, lantern riddles, nighttime processions, and family reunions are embedded in the life rhythms of different communities. What makes the modern Taiwan Lantern Festival unique is that it reconfigures these localized customs into a national event coordinated centrally, executed locally, and participated in by audiences across all of Taiwan. Historical records from the Ministry of Transportation and Tourism Bureau and related studies point to 1990 as an important starting point for the modern Taiwan Lantern Festival system.[^1][^2]

Early activities were centered in Taipei. The history provided by the Taipei City Government indicates that the Ministry of Transportation and Tourism Bureau held a Lantern Festival in Taipei in 1990, which was renamed the Taipei Lantern Festival the following year. This history shows that today's nationwide rotating system was gradually established through years of accumulation, institutional adjustment, and local participation.[^3]

2001 marked an institutional turning point. The festival began to be hosted by different counties and cities in rotation, making hosting rights part of a local government’s effort to gain city visibility and tourism resources. Taipei continued to hold its own Taipei Lantern Festival, but the venue for the national Taiwan Lantern Festival began moving away from the fixed capital, entering railway stations, high-speed rail lines, riverbanks, port areas, and industrial parks in various cities.[^2][^3]

The significance of city rotation is not just about bringing tourists to different places. Each move forces a local area to answer a question: what does this city want the outside world to see? Taichung once linked the festival with Houli, Wuri, and urban transportation. Tainan incorporated the history of an ancient capital, Nanke industry, and the zodiac imagery into its main lantern and promotion. In 2024, Tainan established its identity using the ancient capital and zodiac imagery, while in 2025, Taoyuan combined airplane windows with lanterns, transforming the travel experience of an airport city into a festive visual.[^6][^7][^9][^10] These cases demonstrate that the "national" nature of the Lantern Festival must be established through local differences.

## How the Main Lantern Becomes Shared Memory

The main lantern is the most recognizable visual core of the Taiwan Lantern Festival. It is usually massive and requires handling structure, wind resistance, lighting, dynamic machinery, and nighttime safety. Research on the main lanterns points out that a main lantern appeared in 1990, and from 1991 onward, it gradually used the zodiac animal of that year as its design basis. Around 2019, main lantern designs began to more clearly integrate local elements, city concepts, and contemporary visual vocabulary.[^4]

The zodiac provided an entry point understandable across all of Taiwan. Regardless of whether the host location was in the north, south, or east, that year's animal allowed audiences to quickly recognize the festival period. However, the zodiac is only a common language; it cannot encompass the entire content. The differences between each year's main lantern come from how the locality handles materials, scale, color, landscape, and story. When the main lantern is no longer just an enlarged zodiac sculpture, it begins to function as a way for the city to self-introduce.

The main lantern also changes the audience's viewing experience. During the day, people might first see the steel frame, covering materials, and surrounding circulation paths. At night, lighting programs, music, and crowds collectively transform the object into an event. A review of the design, structure, and covering materials of past main lanterns indicates that steel frames, perforated stainless steel plates, light grids, LED optical materials, and recyclable materials have all played a role in different editions. These technical choices reflect both the safety requirements for large-scale public art and contemporary aesthetics.[^4]

## An Urban Exhibition Curated by Government

The Taiwan Lantern Festival is often described as a tourism activity, but its operation is closer to a short-term erected urban exhibition curated by the government. The venue does not have permanent walls, but it has temporary entrances, routes, service desks, stages, safety lines, and lighting layers. These facilities transform the everyday city into a cultural scene that can be densely viewed. What the audience sees are lights and performances; what the organizers face is the problem of short-term reallocation of public space. Researchers analyzed government procurement data from 2016 to 2020 and found that each year's festival involved 140 to 234 tenders, covering services, engineering, hardware, performance, transportation, security, and environmental creation. These numbers remind us that behind the lantern effects is a massive administrative and supply chain system.[^2]

The governance of large events involves time, space, and data. The host city must complete site, lantern zone, and transportation arrangements before the Lunar New Year, coordinating with school winter breaks, the return migration tide, and other local festivals. Lantern zones cannot only pursue density; they must also arrange entrances/exits, emergency evacuation routes, accessible paths, rest areas, restrooms, and family needs. Visitor numbers, peak times, shuttle efficiency, and accident handling data influence the preparation of the next host city. Only by placing these tasks within a single governance framework can we understand that the festival is not an exhibition that operates naturally after the installation is complete.

Research indicates that the Taiwan Lantern Festival began announcing its host city in advance starting in 2017, giving localities more time to prepare. This system also changed the role of local governments. Localities are no longer just taking over execution a few months before the event; they can incorporate the festival into urban planning, tourism routes, transportation construction, and local revitalization discussions earlier.[^2]

## Lantern Zones Re-presenting Locality

While the main lantern focuses attention, the lantern zones disperse the story. The arrangement of these zones creates a unique way of reading the city. Audiences proceed along signs, moving from a train station exit to a riverbank or park, and then identifying another landscape feature through an artwork. Markets, old streets, temples, industrial areas, and newly developed areas—originally scattered on a map—are brought into one nighttime narrative by the festival. For first-time visitors, this might be an entry point to know the city; for residents, it may be a way to see familiar places anew. Each lantern zone typically places local industry, historical landscapes, folk beliefs, school communities, and artistic creation onto one night map. As audiences move through a lantern zone, they transition from one artwork to another, switching between different scales. One moment they might see the national main lantern; the next, they might stop by a local group's work, then enter a commercial district, train station, or riverbank.

This spatial arrangement can create local economy, but it can also lead to the oversimplification of locality. If a city only magnifies its specialties into mascots and compresses its history into slogans, the festival risks becoming disposable brand packaging. Conversely, if lantern zones provide sufficient narrative space for local creators, schools, communities, and traditional crafts, the celebration can leave behind cultural relationships that last beyond two weeks.

Planning documents from the 2020 Taichung Lantern Festival showed that the event integrated the festival with tourist attractions in Houli, shuttle services, and guidance arrangements. This approach directed the traffic of a large event toward broader routes rather than keeping everyone confined to the lantern zones. The festival thus became an opportunity for the city to reintroduce itself and a stress test that transportation and tourism systems must jointly bear.[^6]

## The Encounter Between Traditional Customs and Technological Display

The Taiwan Lantern Festival is not about preserving tradition as a static specimen. The blessings, reunions, and nighttime strolls of the Lantern Festival naturally change with urban life. Large-scale festivals accelerate this change, placing handmade lanterns and digital projections side-by-side in the same space, leading traditional artists, designers, engineering teams, and software workers to co-produce the festive experience. Tradition and modernity meet on the same night road, causing different technologies and memories to adjust each other. In recent years, lantern exhibitions have incorporated projection, sensors, interactive devices, drones, augmented reality, and mobile guides, turning audiences from remote viewers into participants who can trigger light and shadow, collect information, or complete tasks. A study in _Frontiers in Psychology_ used the Taiwan Lantern Festival's AR display as a case study, analyzing how digital interaction affects knowledge comprehension, attitude, creativity, and willingness to use through 200 questionnaires. The results showed that if technology can be combined with learning content, cultural experience may not stop at taking photos.[^5]

The incorporation of technology into performances does not automatically mean innovation is complete. High-intensity screens, dense sound and light effects, and real-time interaction might make it harder for audiences to feel the paper, bamboo, metal, hand-painting, and the night itself. When evaluating technological value, the key lies in whether it helps the audience better understand the local story, whether it provides ways for people of different ages and physical conditions to participate, and whether the content can be preserved, researched, and reused after the event.

In this sense, the festival is also a space for informal education. For schools, the festival can be an outdoor extension of local curriculum. Teachers take students to observe lantern materials, explain city history, or compare how different zones use sound and space. For families, viewing lanterns often involves elders explaining customs, children using phones, and family members interacting while searching for designated lanterns. These small interactions turn cultural inheritance into a shared walking experience, not just knowledge that experts can explain. Children might learn about a river, a train station, or a local craft in the lantern zones. Adults might understand how a large city manages crowds, light, sound, and public safety during walking and waiting. Festive learning is not always achieved through textbooks; it can also be completed at the moment a family discusses "where to go next."

## Costs Beyond Visitor Numbers

The tourism benefits of the festival cannot only be calculated on-site. Hotels, dining, commercial districts, transportation, and local attractions may benefit, but the distribution of profits often depends on the lantern zone's location, operating hours, shuttle arrangements, and visitor dwell time. If the municipal government can connect official routes with local businesses and community tours, audiences have a chance to move from one-time check-ins to longer urban exploration. Conversely, if transportation nodes and main lantern zones are too far apart, or promotion is concentrated on only a few landmarks, remote areas may bear the cost of the event without seeing corresponding benefits.

The festival also changes the city's nighttime usage rhythm. Riverbanks, train station squares, school peripheries, and industrial parks, which are normally used only for commuting or leisure, become high-density public spaces in a short period. This transformation allows residents to see overlooked areas, but it can also cause noise, light pollution, garbage, and traffic control. Only with resident consultation, clear construction timelines, and departure transportation planning can the festival be a shared experience rather than an external event that locals must temporarily avoid.

The Taiwan Lantern Festival has strong tourism appeal. Studies have documented this as a phase phenomenon attracting over ten million visitors during about two weeks, and they also point out how local lanterns, food, and other concurrent activities drive each other. Therefore, visitor numbers are the success metric most frequently used by governments, media, and host cities.[^2]

However, visitor numbers only indicate how many people were counted; they do not necessarily explain whether the culture was understood, whether local businesses truly benefited, or whether residents were willing to endure the noise, traffic jams, and garbage from the event. If a host city only pursues larger numbers, hardware scale and promotional spending may increase simultaneously, but the living environment and carrying capacity of residents may not receive equal attention.

Sustainable governance requires breaking down the issues. In terms of energy, one must consider lighting hours, equipment reuse, and disposal after dismantling. In transportation, peak pressure from shuttles, walking routes, railways, and highways must be managed. In materials, it is necessary to track whether main lantern and zone installations can be maintained, recycled, or transformed for the next exhibition. In terms of publicness, one must ask if the elderly, people with disabilities, children, foreign residents, and those unfamiliar with the city can all participate safely and with dignity.

These issues do not diminish the cultural value of the festival. On the contrary, they bring the celebration back from a beautiful result to concrete public work. The lights are visible because someone arranged the power, transportation, cleaning, medical aid, volunteers, and order. Including this labor in the narrative prevents the celebration from being just main lantern photos.

## A Rewriting Image of Taiwan

The legacy of the Lantern Festival is not only found in main lantern photos; it also exists in the tacit knowledge accumulated by workers. Which material suits a damp winter, which route allows wheelchair users to avoid stairs, which stage direction prevents excessive sound reflection, and which entrance needs a medical station—these are usually not known from one planning session. If this experience is compiled into public operational records, the next city does not have to start from scratch and can use resources in more creative ways. Host cities can compile lantern zone designs, volunteer training, public art works, guide texts, and traffic data into reusable urban archives. This data helps the next host understand which routes cause bottlenecks and allows local creators to maintain the context of their work even after the event ends. If all results are only kept in short promotional videos, the city struggles to accumulate its own festive knowledge.

For local creators, participation in the festival also involves working conditions and attribution rights. Large events often have clear visual themes and national branding, but community groups may produce works with smaller budgets. If curation and procurement systems clearly explain creation fees, maintenance responsibilities, work preservation, and licensing methods, local culture will not just be treated as consumable decoration. These systemic details are less eye-catching than the main lantern but directly determine who can leave a name and maintain their work in the festival.

The most distinctive feature of the Taiwan Lantern Festival is that it possesses both fixed and fluid characteristics simultaneously. The fixed aspects are the sense of time related to the Lantern Festival, nighttime viewing, blessing, and reunion. The fluid aspects are the host city, lantern zone configuration, material technology, artistic language, and local story. It is not a permanent building, yet it establishes a cultural scene recognizable by audiences across Taiwan in a short period.

From Taipei in 1990 to the county-city rotation after 2001, the Taiwan Lantern Festival has made the name "Taiwan" represent not just central administrative organs, but also different localities introducing themselves to each other. Tainan can speak through its ancient capital and industry; Taichung can speak through transportation nodes and living circles; Taoyuan can speak through airports, movement, and a new urban image. Each edition of the festival asks: when a city is seen by all of Taiwan, what does it want to leave behind—a scenic spot, or a relationship that residents can continue to use?[^6][^7][^8]

Therefore, the Taiwan Lantern Festival cannot be summarized merely as "lantern viewing." It transforms folk customs into public culture, cities into exhibition venues, and administrative systems into annual experiments of audience experience. What is even more worth asking is what happens after the event ends. Are demolition projects retaining reusable materials? Have temporary traffic facilities been restored to normal? Has the opinion of volunteers and residents been reviewed? Can local creators obtain work records? These things determine whether the festival was a one-time consumption or if it accumulated into urban cultural capacity.

The public nature of large festivals is also reflected in who can participate. Some people can walk for long periods on weekdays; some require accessible routes, clear rest areas, and predictable shuttles. Some are familiar with the host city; some come to Taiwan for the first time and must rely on multilingual information and simple, easy-to-understand guidance. If the festival imagines participants as a single group of young tourists, many families, the elderly, people with disabilities, and foreign residents will be excluded. Including accessibility in lantern zone design is a basic condition of public cultural activities, not an added service.

After the festival ends, installations are dismantled and lights are turned off, but how the host city handles local memory, public space, environmental costs, and cultural participation is what truly leaves the long-term result of this mobile stage.
