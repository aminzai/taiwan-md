# Taiwan Sign Language: From a Community Language in Deaf Schools to a Nationally Recognized Language

During the COVID-19 pandemic, the sign language interpreters standing beside officials at daily press conferences became suddenly familiar. Many people noticed for the first time: sign language is not simply moving Chinese sentences into hands; it is a language with its own vocabulary, grammar, history, and community. That moment of recognition is important, but it also easily leads to the misconception that Taiwan Sign Language was recently "invented" as a public service.

Taiwan Sign Language has been operating within the Deaf community for a long time. Its history is intertwined with Deaf education, colonial rule, post-war population movements, and language policy. It was not a tool designed by any single government agency. A description from National Taiwan University states this plainly: sign language communicates through a "gesture—visual" channel and possesses its own vocabulary and grammar, making it a natural human language[^1].

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan Sign Language is a natural language used daily by the Deaf community in Taiwan, not a gesture version of Chinese. It formed a sustainable linguistic community in deaf educational institutions in Tainan and Taipei in the early 20th century, influenced by Japanese sign language, and later absorbed new vocabulary from Chinese Sign Language and educational settings after WWII. The division between North and South educational systems resulted in dialectal differences; for a long time, schools often treated sign language as a communication method needing correction or supplementation. In 2019, the National Language Development Act listed Taiwan Sign Language among national languages, beginning institutional recognition of its status, but early exposure in families, teacher training, materials, interpretation, and public use remain the true tests ahead[^2].

## Let's Put Aside the Misconception That "Sign Language is Just Pantomime"

The first common knowledge that needs to be corrected about Taiwan Sign Language is that it is not a word-for-word translation of spoken language. The explanation from National Taiwan University points out that speech is transmitted through sound and hearing, while sign language is transmitted through movement and vision. Both use finite basic symbols to form vocabulary, which are then used grammar to express infinite concepts[^1]. Therefore, Taiwan Sign Language is not "Chinese plus gestures," nor is it a universal international sign language shared by all nations.

A linguistic introduction published in _Taiwan Insight_ provides an easy-to-understand example: Taiwan Sign Language can use the basic Subject—Object—Verb word order; for instance, the structure of "He likes apples" would be rendered as "He—apples—likes." Negation might also appear after the verb or even at the end of the sentence[^2]. This is not substituting Chinese characters with gestures, but rather another set of linguistic rules. Hand shapes, positions, movements, palm orientation, and facial expressions can all participate in distinguishing meaning or constructing a sentence.

Viewing sign language as a natural language also changes our conception of "mother tongue." Many deaf children are born into hearing families where family members often do not know Taiwan Sign Language. Before attending a school for the Deaf and meeting peers, they might only have simple gestures invented by their family. The description from National Taiwan University points out that over 95% of hearing-impaired children are born into hearing families; while family gestures can aid daily communication, they do not necessarily provide a complete linguistic environment[^1]. The question is therefore not just "whether the child learns sign language," but when the child truly encounters a community that uses a complete language, and whether the family receives support during this waiting period.

## Taiwan Sign Language Is Not Equal to "Sign Language Chinese"

There is another concept in Deaf education that must not be confused: Taiwan Sign Language versus Sign Language Chinese. Taiwan Sign Language is a natural language developed by the Deaf community. Sign Language Chinese primarily presents spoken content using gestures or sign language symbols, following the syntax and word order of Mandarin Chinese. A linguistic chapter by James Tai and Jane Tsay points out that after WWII, Mandarin replaced Japanese as the primary language in education, administration, and mass media, leading educational settings to use Sign Language Chinese based on Mandarin. It shares many vocabulary items with Taiwan Sign Language but differs in morphology and syntax[^9].

This coexistence does not mean users only know one mode. Research indicates that deaf students may be exposed simultaneously to spoken Mandarin, written Chinese, Sign Language Chinese, and Taiwan Sign Language. In professional or technical terminology, they might temporarily borrow from Sign Language Chinese and standardized gestures used in educational settings[^9]. Therefore, the linguistic ecology of Taiwan Sign Language is not as simple as "natural language versus foreign language," but rather different communication systems interacting within schools, families, and communities. The real question is: does this interaction lead to every language being learned completely, or does it only result in fragments sufficient for classroom use?

## Tainan and Taipei: Two Schools Left Behind Two Directions

The history of Taiwan Sign Language must begin with the schools. Language did not appear because a certain place had an official naming chart; rather, it emerged because scattered children had the opportunity to gather long-term, attend classes together, live together, and gradually develop family gestures and foreign sign languages into a community language capable of transmitting knowledge and experience.

Wayne H. Smith from the Institute of Linguistics at Academia Sinica points out in his historical review that the first school for the Deaf in Tainan was established in 1915, and the second school in Taipei was established in 1917. Both schools were founded during Japanese colonial rule and were influenced by the educational systems of Tokyo and Osaka, respectively[^3]. The description from National Taiwan University also notes that Japanese teachers came to Taiwan at the time, bringing Japanese sign language, and the Deaf community in Taiwan gradually developed its own sign language within this educational environment[^1].

The differences between North and South did not appear as later accents; they were inherent because the initial educational networks were not entirely the same. Smith's review argues that the Taipei schools largely adopted the Tokyo system, while the Tainan schools largely adopted the Osaka system, and these two Japanese sign languages already had regional variations, which formed the historical basis for the dialects of Taiwan Sign Language[^3]. Thus, "Taiwan Sign Language" is not a monolithic standard without internal differences. Like spoken Taiwanese, it carries traces left by schools, cities, generations, and community migration.

These two schools were not just places of linguistic input; they were where children first met large numbers of peers, formed friendships, and established communal memories. In other words, the schools brought together language users who were previously scattered across different families. The school buildings, dormitories, playgrounds, and classrooms were thus not merely educational facilities but social spaces through which Taiwan Sign Language could be transmitted across generations. Looking back at the gates of these early deaf schools today, we see not just architecture, but a pathway that brought language from the family into the public sphere.

This history also reminds us not to write "influenced by Japanese sign language" as if it means "Taiwan Sign Language _is_ Japanese sign language." Smith points out that Taiwan Sign Language continued to evolve independently after WWII, later absorbing vocabulary from Mainland Chinese and Hong Kong sign languages, as well as new gestures created in educational settings[^3]. The online dictionary of Taiwan Sign Language from National Taiwan University summarizes this path: the vocabulary and grammatical foundation of Taiwan Sign Language originated from Japanese deaf education during the colonial period, with some influence from Chinese Sign Language added after 1949[^4]. Different sources have varying estimates of the proportion of shared vocabulary; here, no single percentage is presented as proof of identity. A language's identity comes from continuous community use, not a similarity ranking.

## The Post-War Problem: A Language Managed by Education

After 1945, the administrative and teaching languages in schools changed, but Taiwan Sign Language did not disappear because of this. Smith records that Taiwanese teachers who remained in Taiwan taught the existing sign language to new staff members. After 1949, deaf individuals, teachers, and experiences from Mainland China brought new influences to Taiwan Sign Language[^3]. This is a history of linguistic contact, and also a history of educational power: which gestures could remain in the classroom, and which were considered "non-standard," were often not decided solely by users.

The study of Taiwan Sign Language also started late. Smith points out that the earliest known articles specifically discussing Taiwan Sign Language did not appear until 1959. In the 1960s and 1970s, deaf schools across Taiwan sequentially organized sign language vocabulary, but early collections, photography, and publications were incomplete, and much material did not circulate[^3]. His own research began in the mid-1970s, with his first doctoral dissertation on Taiwan Sign Language completed in 1989. This academic history shows that a language can be used daily within a community but remain unrecorded by academia or state machinery for a long time.

"Sign language instruction" and "teaching _with_ sign language" are not the same thing. A seminar reported by the Ministry of Education at National Tainan University School for the Deaf in 2024 specifically distinguished between teaching students to learn Taiwan Sign Language as a language, and using Taiwan Sign Language for instruction. The seminar content also included grammar structure and deaf culture, not just vocabulary matching[^5]. This distinction seems technical, but it involves a very fundamental question: are deaf students learning an auxiliary system translated by hearing people in school, or are they learning a complete language capable of carrying mathematics, literature, friendship, and debate?

The history of Deaf education is not a linear progression from "no education" to "linguistic equality." Educational history research divides Taiwan's Deaf education into several overlapping stages: the influence of late Qing dynasty education, the Japanese educational system during colonial rule, the transition of regimes and staff after 1945, and the modern system after the 1950s. Research indicates that teachers from Tainan and Taipei differed in origin during the colonial period; teachers and experiences from Mainland China entered the schools post-WWII; and in the 1970s onward, spoken/auditory training and Sign Language Chinese played a larger role in classrooms[^10]. These changes were not just replacements of teaching methods; they determined at what age, with what language, and by whom students learned.

In the 1970s, the Ministry of Education organized representatives from three deaf schools to compile standardized gestures for teaching, resulting in about 1,750 basic words and creating new signs for classroom concepts. However, related research also records that early preparations involved little participation from Deaf individuals, leading the Deaf community to feel disappointed by the results not reflecting their own language[^9]. Later versions increased the involvement of Deaf users, while the online dictionary of Taiwan Sign Language adopted an approach of including variations rather than replacing community usage with a single standard[^9]. This history adds another layer of weight to the word "standard": standardization can facilitate textbook exchange, but it cannot erase the linguistic judgment of users in the name of textbook convenience.

## 2019: The Law Changed First; Society Must Change Habits

The National Language Development Act, promulgated on January 9, 2019, legally placed Taiwan Sign Language within the scope of national languages. Policy documents from the Executive Yuan clearly state that the purpose of this law is not to designate one language as the sole representative, but to guarantee the natural languages used by various indigenous groups in Taiwan and the inheritance, revitalization, and development of Taiwan Sign Language[^6]. Between legal recognition and every agency being able to use it, there are resources, teacher training, and institutional implementation. Therefore, the real question is not whether the name changed, but whether linguistic rights can be realized where needed.

Article 3 defines:

> "The national languages referred to in this Act refer to the natural languages used by various indigenous groups in Taiwan and Taiwan Sign Language."[^7]

Article 4 further states that all national languages are equal, and citizens using a national language shall not be discriminated against or restricted[^7]. The "equality" here is not just a beautiful summary but a very concrete policy direction: if Taiwan Sign Language is a national language, it should not only appear at high-visibility events like presidential inaugurations or pandemic press conferences; it must also be used in schools, healthcare, justice, administration, and daily information.

The law also requires that national languages enter education, research, textbooks, online learning, and interpretation services. The articles listed in the legal database include provisions for central educational authorities to designate a national language as part of the curriculum at various stages of basic education, government agencies to provide interpretation services between national languages when necessary, and competent authorities to conduct capability certification[^7]. This transformed Taiwan Sign Language from "a method in special education" into "a language that the nation is responsible for developing."

Having an interpreter at a public event does not mean the entire system is accessible. Interpretation is an important bridge, but both sides of the bridge require stable language services: advance announcements, on-site flow, subtitles, emergency messages, medical explanations, and post-event materials must all be independently obtainable by users. The law writes interpretation into the system; the next step is to ensure that information is not only translated after a major event.

However, legal status does not automatically generate a linguistic environment. A national language survey conducted by the Ministry of Culture in 2020, cited by _Taiwan Insight_, found that only 27.1% of the 571 respondents who took an online questionnaire about Taiwan Sign Language reported learning it as their first language at home[^2]. This is not the proportion of the entire Deaf population, nor can it be used to draw conclusions for all families. It is more like a warning, reminding us that there remains a distance between legal recognition and a family acquiring the language.

## Linguistic Revitalization Is Not Sloganizing; It Is Infrastructure Engineering

National language development policies divide the work into areas such as corpus preservation, education, friendly environments, promotion, and digital resources, positioning linguistic inheritance, socialization, and modernization as core policy drivers[^6]. The Executive Yuan's National Language Development Plan also includes education, corpus preservation, and inter-ministerial division of labor as part of its policy advancement[^8]. Placing Taiwan Sign Language within this framework means that revitalization is not just about holding a few "Sign Language Day" events; it requires building a sustainable infrastructure.

This infrastructure includes searchable corpora, dictionaries for learning, stable teachers and interpreters who are trained, textbooks usable in schools, and media and public agencies willing to view Taiwan Sign Language as an indigenous language rather than merely an annotation next to the screen. The online dictionary of Taiwan Sign Language established by the research team at National Taiwan University is an example of this linguistic infrastructure construction. Related studies organize how the center for sign language linguistics and the online dictionary make Taiwan Sign Language a learnable and researchable language[^4].

Institutional attempts are already appearing in education. Seminars reported by the Ministry of Education train and certify teachers and staff from schools for the Deaf in Taiwan Sign Language. 42 teachers and 50 staff members participated, bringing the language into the public cultural space of a museum through sign language tours[^5]. This number cannot be exaggerated to claim "sufficient teaching staff across Taiwan," but it shows a direction: creating a friendly environment is not about requiring the Deaf community to adapt to the hearing system, but about making those within the system learn to enter the world of Taiwan Sign Language.

The tools listed in national language policies also show that linguistic revitalization is not just about increasing class hours. It requires surveys and databases so researchers and communities know where the language is being used; it requires guaranteeing early exposure to prevent children from spending an early period without complete linguistic stimulation; it requires textbooks, teachers, and curricula so the language is not confined to a single field of special education; and it requires written references, digital input methods, online learning, and interpretation training so that Taiwan Sign Language can enter modern administrative and information environments[^7]. These tasks are interconnected: without corpora, textbooks cannot be updated; without teachers, curricula cannot be implemented; without digital tools, the language is difficult to see in daily messages.

## Who Will Use Taiwan Sign Language Next?

![A sign language performance during the 2007 Taipei 101 warm-up event, showing that Taiwan Sign Language can also be part of performance, sports, and group culture.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/2007Taipei101RunUp_WarmingUp_SignLanguagePerformance.jpg?width=1280)

The significance of this photo is not that it decorates Taiwan Sign Language as an event performance, but to remind us: Deaf culture is not only about "needing assistance." Sign language can be a language class, public interpretation, or it can be a performance, a sporting event, a community gathering, and creation. When cultural activities only place the Deaf in the position of being served, Taiwan Sign Language remains marginalized. Only when the Deaf can become performers, organizers, and knowledge producers in their own language does public space gain an expression beyond mere sound.

If Taiwan Sign Language is used only by the Deaf, with a few interpreters translating at critical moments, its legal status will remain on paper. This is not because every hearing person must learn fluently; it is because linguistic rights require multiple levels of entry: deaf children need early exposure to complete language, parents need affordable learning support, teachers need to teach in Taiwan Sign Language, and healthcare and public services need to provide direct or reliable communication, and the general public must understand that they are facing a person who uses a different language, not someone "who cannot speak."

The most powerful aspect of the story of Taiwan Sign Language is precisely not that it was finally named a national language by the government, but that it had been alive for a long time before it was named. The two early schools gathered scattered children into a community; the North and South dialects preserved the differences in educational networks; post-war linguistic contact introduced new vocabulary; and researchers, teachers, and the Deaf community organized it into a language that could be learned, recorded, and inherited.

Therefore, 2019 was not the starting point of Taiwan Sign Language, but a belated public acknowledgment. The true end will not be having one more interpreter next to the press conference. It will be when Taiwan Sign Language appears in a deaf child's family and school before they are forced to wait until "they grow up to learn a language." It will be when public agencies do not have to wait for major events to think about informational equality. It will be when research and textbooks no longer treat the Deaf as mere recipients of service, but recognize them as users, teachers, and knowledge producers—only then will the "equality" in the law become life.

Taiwan Sign Language is not silent Taiwan. It reaches us with its eyes. And whether a society truly listens depends on its willingness to turn its own systems in that direction.
