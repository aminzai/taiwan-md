> **30-second overview:** In 1888 Liu Ming-chuan established the Taiwan Postal Administration in Taipei; eight years before the Qing Imperial Post was formally founded, Taiwan already had its own postal routes and postal experiments. On the surface a stamp is merely a "postage paid" receipt, yet it places dragons, Sun Yat-sen, Kinmen gun towers, Taiwan fruits, birds, and Indigenous artifacts in a single miniature image archive. To read these images is to discover that stamps are not bystanders of history but the paper sites where each regime arranges how Taiwan is to be seen.

In 1888, Liu Ming-chuan established the Taiwan Postal Administration in Taipei, beginning to plan postal routes, handle mail, and issue stamps. This predated the formal establishment of the Qing Imperial Post by eight years; Taiwan's modern postal system was not a complete transplant from outside, but a working version that grew on the island first.[^1] The Customs First Dragon stamps issued in 1878 when the Qing Customs piloted a postal service are listed by Chunghwa Post as the country's first stamp series; by 1896, when modern postal service officially commenced, the two time points together formed the starting line of Taiwan's postal history.[^2]

Here lies an easily overlooked contradiction: stamps began merely as proof of postage paid, yet quickly became the state's cheapest, most stable image publication. Envelopes get thrown away; stamps are clipped, collected, placed in albums. Their size is too small to require explanation, yet their designs often answer for an era "who deserves commemoration" and "which piece of land deserves to be seen." Stamps are not complete history, but they preserve the history power once wanted people to see.

## Liu Ming-chuan First Drew the Postal Routes on the Island

The story of stamps cannot begin under a collector's magnifying glass; it must begin with a letter that actually traveled a postal route. In 1888 a Taiwan relay-station cover was sent from Taipei and forwarded back to Hwei-tou (Tamsui), bearing stamp, postmark, and route traces all at once. This official cover was sent by Liu Ming-chuan to Vice General Li Wan-yu for military communication; only seven such relay-station covers from that year are reported to survive.[^3]

This detail matters because it reduces "institutional founding" to an action: someone wrote a letter, someone affixed a stamp, someone put it on the road. The postal service was not an abstract modernization noun but a traceable line between Taipei, Tamsui, and military administration. That tiny stamp, for the first time, let Taiwan's administrative power appear in daily life in a form that could be touched, stamped, and forwarded.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** A nation is often written as borders and statutes, but the postal service first made it a road; people did not believe the map and then start mailing letters — rather, through repeated mailing, they slowly learned which places were connected to which.

## A Dragon, First Speaking for the Emperor

The 1878 Customs First Dragon stamp used a coiling dragon amid clouds as its central motif; the dragon symbolized the emperor. Like Britain's 1840 Penny Black bearing Queen Victoria's profile, both placed supreme authority inside a "postage paid" voucher. The difference: one drew power as the monarch's face, the other as the emperor's dragon.[^2] Stamps thus performed two jobs simultaneously: they let letters get on the road, and they made the state show up every time a stamp was affixed.

After Taiwan was ceded to Japan in 1895, postal service was incorporated into the Japanese colonial system; between August and October that year, the Republic of Formosa issued eight locally printed stamp varieties. This brief postal history reminds us that stamps are not tools only victors use; a regime lasting only a short time also tried to use stamps to prove it was governing a place.[^4]

The Government-General of Taiwan era had its own administrative logic. After Japan's defeat in 1945, Taiwan began designing and printing special stamps for local use due to currency and administrative needs. Before new stamps could be supplied, old Japanese stamps were even overprinted with "For Use in Taiwan Province" as an emergency measure.[^5] These overprints were not mere printing flaws but the provisional syntax of regime transition: the same piece of paper re-marked, so the post office could keep working.

## Stamps Paste War onto Daily Life

In 1949, stamp denominations were repeatedly rewritten. A Taiwan Panorama postal history article records that currency conditions were chaotic and hyperinflation severe; the face value of Sun Yat-sen gold yuan stamps ran from ten yuan all the way to one million yuan, and later even the post office filled in the amount at mailing.[^5] Stamps thus became a miniature dashboard of economic disorder: the paper did not grow larger, yet numbers were forced to chase the currency's collapse.

In the 1950s, stamps placed national construction and anti-communist mobilization side by side. Taiwan Panorama's compiled subjects included land reform, agriculture, the Cross-Island Highway, bridges, harbors, and industrial construction, as well as the Kinmen Juguang Tower and the Defend Kinmen, Defend Matsu series. Stamps put "Taiwan under construction" and "Taiwan on defense" into the same national narrative.[^5]

This is not to say every stamp represents everyone's life. It is more like a stage cast by officials: farmers, bridges, baseball youths, Kinmen gun towers may take the stage; certain failures, conflicts, and the voices of the ruled may remain offstage. The 2022 Taipei Post Museum exhibition on women's images juxtaposed postwar stamps with original artworks and women's groups' discussions, also noting that urban professional women and factory women of the industrialization era are harder to find on stamp faces.[^6]

The exhibition also placed Lin Chih-hsin's 1994 woodcuts depicting Taiwan rural life and Chen Chin's 2003 works alongside stamps. When originals are enlarged while stamps remain roughly three by four centimeters, a key difference appears: stamps do not merely shrink the world; they also fix the power to decide which details the state may mass-replicate.[^6]

## From Kinmen's Juguang Tower to Taiwan Fruits

In the 1960s, stamps' gaze began turning more explicitly toward Taiwan itself. Taiwan Panorama records that Taiwan fruits, birds, landscapes, fish, and flowers appeared in succession. These images no longer only explained where a regime came from; they began telling people what on this island was worth recognizing.[^5]

This turn did not mean politics disappeared. Researchers analyzing 1958–2009 Taiwan flora/fauna stamps note that, while they displayed higher artistry and originality compared to doctrinal political propaganda, they could still express national identity through endemic species and habitats. In other words, when a stamp paints a bird or a flower, it is not leaving politics but translating politics into scenery easier to like.[^7]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** "The local" does not necessarily start from a slogan; it may start from a stamp painted with a Taiwan blue magpie, pineapple, or mountain landscape; but being painted into the national image and being truly preserved and spoken for by whom have never been the same thing.

## Indigenous Culture Enters the Square Inch

The 2008 "Taiwan Indigenous Culture Stamps (Continued)" selected four artifacts: a Paiwan pottery jar, an Amis lover's bag, a Rukai men's headdress, and a Bunun men's neck ornament. Chunghwa Post's issue description not only listed artifact names but explained their significance in hierarchy, gift-giving, ceremony, merit, and identity recognition. For example, the Amis lover's bag extended from a daily carrying pouch to an accessory for the harvest festival and important events, becoming a marker of collective identity.[^8]

This set is worth seeing not because it "represents" the sixteen peoples, but because it exposes the difficulty of representation: four stamps can let artifacts be seen by more people, yet cannot compress a people's language, land relations, and historical experience into four frames. Stamps can open an entrance; they cannot substitute for the people finishing the story.

## 1996: The Postal Service Begins Defending Itself

In 1996, Chunghwa Post produced a Postal Service Centenary commemorative set depicting the evolution of four postal implements: pillar boxes, weighing devices, transport equipment, and calculators. The issue description simultaneously acknowledged that telecom technology, private delivery, and financial competition had put traditional postal service under pressure, so the post must promote automation and computerization to rebuild its service image.[^9]

The interest of this commemorative set lies in its refusal to write a hundred years of postal service as mere glorious retrospective. It commemorates pillar boxes and transport equipment while acknowledging that traditional advantages are fading. The post must explain why it still needs to exist even as it memorializes itself. This contradiction holds today: electronic messages mean people no longer mail letters, yet post offices have not lost all work, because they still handle parcels, finance, remote-area services, and part of local public life.

The architectural history of the Taipei Post Office materializes this "still exists." The three-story steel-frame building completed in 1930 was then Taiwan's largest post office. In the 1960s floors were added and the front five arches demolished; in 1992 it was designated a historic monument; from 2010 it underwent restoration; in 2019 the five arches were restored. It is not only a business site but the material witness of the postal service from colonial administration, to postwar agency, to cultural asset.[^1]

## Post Office Architecture Keeps Paper Institutions on the Street Corner

If one only looks at stamp designs, the postal service easily seems a distant national symbol; walk to Taipei's North Gate and you see it also has weight. Ministry of Culture data notes that when the Taipei Post Office was completed in 1930 it occupied over 13,000 square meters, its exterior clad in light-brown air-raid-colored face bricks from the Beitou kiln, its main entrance fronted by five semi-circular arched columns.[^1]

In the 1960s the building was expanded and arches removed for postal vehicle access and space needs; from the 1970s it even faced demolition. Only after 1992 historic designation did preservation gain institutional standing; in 2019 the five arches returned, the building re-entering the city's sight.[^1] The postal service thus not only connected places but left in places objects that needed preserving, debating, and reusing.

The post office preservation controversy happens to be the inverse of stamp function. Stamps compress a complex era into a few square centimeters; a historic monument props that era up into a building not easily moved. The former leaves memory through circulation; the latter through staying. The Taipei Post Office simultaneously holds two time scales: letters must hurry; the building must stay.

## A Taiwan No Single Stamp Finishes Telling

Stamps are most easily misread as "a little Taiwan history." They are more like an edited catalog: the 1878 dragon, the 1888 relay-station cover, the 1895 short-lived regime, the 1949 runaway denominations, the 1950s construction and war, the 1960s fruits and birds, and the 2008 Indigenous artifacts renamed and re-displayed — all explain what different eras wanted to stick on envelopes and send to whose hands.[^2] [^3] [^4] [^5] [^8]

Therefore, what is truly worth collecting is not the overly complete sentence "stamps represent Taiwan," but the choices and absences each stamp leaves behind. When we see a Taiwan blue magpie, we must also ask who named it; when we see Kinmen's Juguang Tower, we must also ask how war was compressed into a scenery; when we see an Indigenous lover's bag, we must also ask whether the people's voices remain inside after the artifact enters the national image.

This also explains why philately can be both hobby and research. A Kinmen national philatelic exhibition report treats philatelic items as combinations of stamps, postmarks, and routes, not just pretty designs; a single relay-station cover lets one trace sender, recipient, transit points, and communication purpose.[^3] When collecting moves from "I like this one" to "where has this one traveled," stamps turn from small objects into a method of reading historical sources.

The method itself carries limits. Official issue descriptions excel at telling us stamp subjects, designers, issue dates, and artifact meanings, but will not automatically tell us who was not selected; museum international commentary can place Taiwan in East Asian postal history, but may also follow larger national frameworks.[^4] So reading stamps cannot only ask "what does it depict"; one must also put the issuing authority, historical context, and omitted subjects into the frame.

Stamps are not only past tense. The 1996 centenary stamps already acknowledged pressure from telecom and private delivery, writing automation and computerization into the postal service's self-renewal;[^9] this means postal history is not a decline from "golden age" but a constant self-change proving public service still necessary.

Fewer people mail letters today, but that does not mean the post office's social position in Taiwan has vanished; rather, its value has shifted from mass private letters to parcels, finance, remote accessibility, and local architectural memory. Stamp size has not changed; the social relations the post must handle have never stopped changing.

For collectors, this change sediments in versions, perforations, denominations, postmarks, and overprint texts. For ordinary mailers, it may be just a paper stuck on the envelope's upper right corner. Both views are true, because the postal service has always simultaneously served professional collecting and the most ordinary daily communication.

Therefore, stamps need not be mystified into artifacts only experts understand. Knowing the 1878 Large Dragon, the 1888 Taiwan relay-station cover, is already enough to start asking how a letter passes through a system; knowing the 1960s flora/fauna stamps and the 2008 Indigenous culture stamps lets one see how the state rewrites its own gaze.

Every stamp has an issue date, but that does not equal its meaning living only that day. It may be clipped and saved years later, taken to an exhibition, placed in a historic post office display case, or travel with a family letter to another city. Circulation and preservation are not opposite fates but the stamp's most special life cycle.

Put a stamp back in its issue year, then put it back on a letter's route, and you gain two more layers of understanding than looking at the design alone: who arranged the images, and who used this system.

The gap between these two layers is precisely what makes Taiwan stamps most worth reading. They are small, yet hold both national imagination and ordinary people's mailing acts.

View stamp face, route, and absence together — only then will the image not be mistaken for all of history.

Stamps have never been mere postage vouchers. They press nation, locality, war, industry, nature, and family correspondence into a size that can be affixed, mailed, and saved. **They have not left Taiwan a complete answer, but have left each era a multiple-choice question: who is painted in, who is still waiting for their own stamp.**
