In the spectrum of Taiwan's commoner culture, no art form perfectly bridges the clanging gongs and drums of temple-stage open-air performances, the black-and-white and color waves of television screens, and the visual spectacles of contemporary digital special effects like glove puppetry does. Looking back at this journey, the name of Huang Hai-dai, the glove puppetry master from Huwei, Yunlin, and his family, is almost synonymous with the entire modern history of Taiwan's glove puppetry evolution [^1] [^2]. This is not merely a transmission of puppetry skills, but a series of cultural struggles that break through and are reborn amidst the great changes of the times.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:**
> Many people assume that glove puppetry is just a folk trick for children. However, in 1970s Taiwan, Huang Chun-hsiung's production of _The Great Confucian Scholar of Yunzhou_ achieved a legendary 92% viewership rating. That was a collective memory of Taiwanese people gathering around black-and-white televisions, with streets empty of people, representing the strongest voice of traditional art bursting forth in the wave of modern media.

## From Temple Stages to Indoor Theaters: The Founding of Wuchou Yuan and the Years of Jianghu

The roots of Taiwan's glove puppetry can be traced back to the palm puppetry introduced from Fujian, China, but through the blending of several generations of local artists, it developed a unique artistic life [^5]. Huang Hai-dai, born in Yunlin in the 11th year of the Republic of China (1922), grew up under the influence of his father Huang Ma's puppet troupe and the music of Beiguan [^3]. In 1931, Huang Hai-dai founded the famous "Wuchou Yuan" in Huwei, officially opening the glorious chapter of the Wuchou School.

Early glove puppetry was mostly performed in the form of "serial dramas" on temple stages or indoor theater stages, with a single play often continuing for dozens of days without rest. Huang Hai-dai was not only proficient in Beiguan, Nanguan, Luantan, and Siping opera, but also used his profound literary achievements to adapt traditional chapter novels (such as _Romance of the Three Kingdoms_, _Investiture of the Gods_, and _Ye Sou Pu Yan_) into scripts with exquisite oral narration and dramatic plots [^4]. The characters he created, such as "Wen Suchen," became popular for a time, laying a solid narrative foundation for later martial arts glove puppetry.

During the heyday of indoor theater performances, Huang Hai-dai's eldest son, Huang Jun-qing, pioneered the "Golden Light" glove puppetry. With gorgeous lighting, mechanical devices, martial arts special effects, and a bold oral narration style, he pushed indoor glove puppetry to the peak of commercial entertainment [^2].

## The TV Phenomenon Shaking the Martial World: From Shi Yen-wen to Su Huanzhen

As social structures transformed and televisions gradually entered ordinary households, traditional indoor glove puppetry faced unprecedented survival crises. However, Huang Hai-dai's second son, Huang Chun-hsiung, boldly seized the opportunity of new media, launching _The Great Confucian Scholar of Yunzhou_ on TTV in 1970. The script was adapted by his father Huang Hai-dai, while Huang Chun-hsiung personally handled the oral narration and music composition [^4] [^2].

The protagonist of the series, "Shi Yen-wen," overnight became a household national hero across Taiwan, joining iconic characters like the Hidden Mirror Man, Liu San, and the Strange Old Man in the streets and alleys [^2]. This series not only changed the daily routines of Taiwanese people but also created a viewership myth in Taiwan's television history. Although it was later banned due to political factors such as the "Mandarin Policy," the resilience of glove puppetry in moving to outdoor stages and the video tape market proved that this art form had already penetrated the flesh and blood of Taiwanese people.

Entering the 1980s and 1990s, Huang Hai-dai's grandsons Huang Qiang-hua and Huang Wen-ze (Pili International Multimedia) sparked another revolution. With _Pili Golden Light_, they launched the "Pili" series universe featuring Su Huanzhen, Yi Ye-shu, and others. By combining traditional glove puppetry with modern cinematic camera movements, computer special effects, rock music, and high-difficulty puppet manipulation techniques, they successfully elevated glove puppetry from a niche local opera to a refined cultural industry [^6] [^7].

From Huang Hai-dai's oral narration inheritance, to Huang Chun-hsiung's TV pioneering, to the Pili brothers' digital innovation, each generation faced the most severe media challenges of their time, yet always managed to carve out new aesthetic boundaries in the cracks.

## Preservation of Skills and Contemporary Challenges: The Sustainable Path of the Universe in the Palm

With the diversification of entertainment media, traditional glove puppetry faces the structural dilemma of aging traditional artists and a lack of successors. Although Huang Hai-dai received numerous honors during his lifetime, such as the National Arts Award and the designation of Important Traditional Arts Conservator, symbolizing the highest recognition of the state for this art, how to preserve the essence of the "universe in the palm" and root it deeply remains a task that contemporary cultural workers cannot relax [^8].

In recent years, many young puppeteers and folk groups have begun to try bringing glove puppetry into schools, combining it with contemporary theater, and even using Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR) technologies, allowing the younger generation to re-recognize this comprehensive aesthetic that integrates carving, embroidery, music, literature, and puppetry. From traditional wooden puppets to heroes on digital screens, the story of Taiwan's glove puppetry has never stopped.

## Conclusion: A Lifetime of Puppetry, an Epic in the Palm

In 2007, Huang Hai-dai, revered as the "Grand Master of Heaven" in Taiwan's glove puppetry, passed away peacefully at the age of 107, ending his legendary life [^2]. In his century-long life, he witnessed Taiwan's drastic transitions from an agricultural society, to industrial takeoff, to the digital information age.

The cloth puppets in his hands continued to演绎 (portray) loyalty, filial piety, integrity, love and hate, and the myriad states of life on the small stage. Taiwan's glove puppetry is not only an intangible cultural heritage but also a clear mirror reflecting the resilience of this island in the winds and waves of the times.
