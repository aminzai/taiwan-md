> **30-Second Overview:** In 2004, the Truku people became the 12th officially recognized Indigenous group in Taiwan. However, this name was not born that year. After the 1914 Truku War, mountain villages were forced down to the foothills, and tribal memory was slowly recovered through elder oral histories, community tracing, and the Naming Movement. Huang Chang-hsing walked from age 65 to 75 with the community, finding over 300 old village sites: reclaiming the name is just a title; walking back into the mountains makes that title a lived reality.

## Huang Chang-hsing Spreads the Map on the Mountain Path

In 2024, Huang Chang-hsing, from Wenlan Village in Xiulin Township, Hualien, published _The Truku People Rising in East Taiwan_. He did not piece together genealogies at a desk; instead, he brought decades of military training into the mountains: starting at age 65, he assisted in tracing traditional territories, walking for as short as 4 days and as long as 12 days. [^1]

Huang Chang-hsing began hiking Qilai Mountain at the age of eight. He later served in special forces units and trained on Xiugulan Mountain and Qilai South Peak. Between 1954 and 2014, he was invited to assist with traditional territory surveys, comparing maps, topography, and elder oral histories alongside youth to record migration routes, anti-Japanese resistance paths, and ecology. [^1]

He stated that the historical records left by Japanese rule regarding past migration and settlement sites are limited; much of the content is contained in stories passed down orally by elders. In the end, he and his team found over 300 old village sites. The weight of this number lies not in it being an archaeological finding, but in each site pulling "we once lived here" from legend back onto the map. [^1]

> **Curator's Note:** A community’s history is sometimes recovered not from archives, but from a mountain path that an elder remembers and a young person is willing to walk again.

## The Name Is Not a New Invention, But a Return to Self

The Indigenous Cultural Knowledge Network of the Taipei City Government records that the traditional migration narrative of the Truku people originated in Truku Truwan (德鹿灣). The community first formed a shared memory around Nian'ai Township in Nantou before crossing Qilai Mountain, Nengao Mountain, and Hehuan Mountain in the 17th and 18th centuries to move east into Hualien, establishing villages along the Litwu River, Truku River, and other streams. [^2]

This history cannot merely be written as "moving from Nantou to Hualien." Names such as Truku, Tgdaya, and Toda point to different living experiences and group identities. After moving east, the community established new homes along the Sanzhan River, Mugua River, and Qingshui River areas. [^2]

Today, main settlements are concentrated in three townships—Xiulin, Wanrong, and Zhuoxi—in Hualien County, as well as three villages (Qingfeng, Nanhua, and Fuxing) in Jian Township. This distribution reminds us that the Truku people do not exist only within the canyon landscapes familiar to tourists; they also exist in lowland villages, households, schools, and daily use of the Tâi-gí language. [^9]

Weaving also connects the mountains, households, and identity. The Indigenous Peoples Council records Truku weaving as **tminun**. The traditional process begins with harvesting hemp, spinning yarn, bleaching, and preparing threads before entering the weaving stage. Tools include looms, hemp beaters, spindles, thread holders, and warp frames. This is not merely garment making: official introductions note that weaving also involves female puberty, marriage, and group identification; tattooing was interrupted during Japanese colonial rule. [^9]

In 2004, Truku was officially recognized as one of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples, with official English records stating the population was approximately 32,333 (January 2020 census). The same page places weaving, tattooing, ancestral worship, and _gaya_—ancestral norms—at the core of the tribal introduction. [^3]

The counter-intuitive aspect of the Naming Movement is that in 2004, a strange name was not created; rather, an already recognized name returned to public documents. In interviews collected by the National Cultural Memory Bank, Teyra Yudaw stated: "The Truku naming began in 2004, but in fact, there was a strong consciousness within the Truku community for the preceding 12 years." [^4]

This also explains why "official recognition" cannot be directly equated with "historical repair from this point forward." Academic articles point out that the Truku people were previously classified under the Atayal tribe. Even after successful naming, how the tribal name is used and how different communities understand each other remains an ongoing discussion. [^5]

## 1914: The Mountain Path Rewritten as a Military Route

The Truku War with Japan, from June 1 to August 13, 1914, lasted for 74 days. Articles in Indigenous literature place this event at the collision of two values: the land understood by the Truku people through the life value of "mutual subjectivity between humans and nature," versus the Japanese colonial government's entry into the mountains to establish governing authority. [^6]

One article records that the Japanese forces, from both east and west routes, mobilized a total of 20,749 personnel, along with 48 field guns and 24 machine guns. The Truku side had approximately 3,000 warriors. The Taiwan Indigenous Peoples Encyclopedia uses another statistical category, recording 6,235 Japanese military police and 11,075 accompanying laborers, and over 9,000 people from 97 villages in the Truku area, with about 3,000 being able-bodied men. [^6] [^7]

These two sets of numbers cannot be crudely added together. They instead remind us: "numbers" in war records depend on the subject of statistics—whether it is military police, laborers, village population, or combatants. What is certain is that this was not an equal border conflict, but a key battle where the state incorporated the mountains into colonial governance through surveying, frontier lines, military police, and weaponry. [^7]

Official documents from the Taipei City Government record that Truku tribal lands were designated as state-owned during Japanese rule. The Xin-cheng Incident in 1896, the Wili Incident in 1906, and the Truku Incident in 1914 occurred sequentially. After the war, mountain people were forced down to the foothills and dispersed into different new villages. [^2]

> **Curator's Note:** For the Truku people, what was lost was never just a piece of land. When a migration route is severed, the name, ancestors, hunting grounds, and dwellings become difficult to prove together.

## From Sweet Potatoes to Shared Food

The tribal data from the Indigenous Peoples Council lists millet, corn, and sweet potatoes as important crops for Truku agricultural production, with fishing and hunting supplementing meat and fish. This food structure places mountain areas, streams, hunting grounds, and household labor within a single system: staple food is not an isolated menu item but is jointly produced by season, land, technology, and division of labor. [^9]

Food also concretizes tribal relationships. Official records show that communal pig slaughter and sharing during weddings, funerals, celebrations, etc., and offerings to ancestors remain important. The "sharing" here is not simply placing food on the table but confirming who belongs to a single network through preparation, ritual sacrifice, consumption, and memory. Contemporary members may participate in church life; ancestral worship and church doctrine are not necessarily understood as two mutually exclusive systems. [^9]

## Ancestral Rites: How Norms Enter the Season

Ancestral worship among the Truku centers on **utux rudan**, with _gaya_ being the norms left by ancestors. Indigenous Peoples Council data indicates that a _gaya_ group can be composed of close relatives, distant relatives, and in-laws, serving kinship, economic, religious, and regional functions. Communal farming, ritual sacrifice, and observing taboos are not just individual faith choices but also maintain how a community lives together. [^9]

The ancestral rite **mgay bari** is usually scheduled by traditional leaders or elders after the millet harvest. Offerings include liquor, millet cakes, crops, fruits, and fish, which are shared among participants after the ceremony concludes. Official records also note that leftover offerings not consumed at the ritual are not taken home but are symbolically separated from ancestors by crossing a bonfire. These actions place the season, the harvest, the ancestors, and the community boundary within one ritual. [^9]

Contemporary Truku life may also incorporate both church faith and ancestral rites. This should not be simplified into a dichotomy of "traditional versus modern." A more precise question is: how do people rearrange responsibilities, taboos, shared food, and memory of ancestors within different systems, family structures, and tribal relationships? [^9]

## Dwellings: The Shape Left by Migration History

The dwelling forms of the Truku are closely related to their migration landscapes. Indigenous Peoples Council data distinguishes between semi-subterranean wooden houses in Nantou's original areas and bamboo houses in Hualien. The former digs deep foundations, builds walls with horizontal logs, and covers them with slate roofs; the latter is constructed upward from ground level using bamboo, with a thatched roof. [^9]

These two building types are not just classifications of "traditional architectural styles"; they also retain material choices and living strategies in different environments. Where wood, bamboo, slate, and thatch were obtained, how the house faced rain, slopes, and temperature, and how households divided labor inside—these speak more to the history of habitation than a single exterior photograph. When villages were forced to move, what changed was not just the address, but also the building materials, household distance, ritual paths, and neighborly relations. [^2] [^9]

## How "Life Value" is Held in a Knife

The thematic website of Taiwan National Park describes that Truku hunting requires learning animal habits, identifying plants and venomous snakes, judging paths, and knowing how to cross rivers, make fire, and set traps. Hunting grounds have rules, and seasons have taboos. Group hunting relies on hunter cooperation. [^8]

These details elevate "hunting culture" beyond mere spectacle. It is a knowledge system that places the body into the mountains, the prey into the season, and individual actions into tribal norms. The concept of humans and nature being mutually subjective in Indigenous literature here is not an abstract slogan but knowing when not to enter a hunting ground, what can be harvested, and how to ensure there will still be mountains to traverse next time. [^6] [^8]

This culture also has a tangible object: the knife. The National Park thematic website records that Truku knives are used for both hunting and agriculture; their shape is crescent-like and is also called rainbow knives. Xu Youxiang, who has been making them for four generations, says: "Without a knife, there are no Truku people." [^8]

The significance of the knife can be understood alongside dwellings and weaving in the same line of life. It is not merely a symbol appearing in rituals or display cases but was once a tool used to process crops, prey, wood, and daily tasks. When the Copper Gate Knife today becomes a gift, collectible, or art piece, change does not mean tradition disappears; it means the object enters a new exchange relationship. The question is whether external observers can still see the technology, norms, and land to which it was originally connected. [^8]

The same official tribal introduction also records the mouth-dulcimer and rattle: the dulcimer can be made from _Shuzhuyu_, mountain salt wood, cypress, oil tung, or maple; striking it can summon relatives to share food, and it can serve as dance accompaniment. The rattle is often used to express emotion and affection. These sounds are not accessories to cultural performances but ways of placing invitation, labor, dance, and intimacy into daily life. [^9]

Today's Copper Gate Knives are often treated as gifts, collectibles, and art pieces. Their evolution is not as simple as moving from "tradition" to "modern." The same knife was once a hunting tool, agricultural tool, and symbol of growth; today, it is an object through which the tribe explains its culture externally. [^8]

## How Culture Enters Systems After Naming

After the Naming Movement achieved administrative recognition, the tribal name entered household registers, official documents, schools, museums, and public policy. But visibility within systems does not equal autonomy in daily life. Schools may use the Truku name, yet the mountains are managed separately by National Parks, Forestry Management, Wildlife Conservation, and local administration; cultural activities may display weaving and knives, but the right to use traditional territories still requires negotiation through law and policy. [^1] [^2] [^8]

Therefore, contemporary cultural work of the Truku people is not just about preserving the past; it also involves deciding what content can be made public, what knowledge must be transmitted within the tribe, and how tourism, research, and media can obtain appropriate consent. Weaving, hunting, dwellings, and ancestral rites are not arbitrarily used materials. Each has its own technical holders, family lineages, tribal norms, and boundaries of external explanation.

From this perspective, traditional territory tracing and the Naming Movement are actually two types of work on the same line. Naming answers "how we should be called"; tracing answers "where this name lived"; and institutional reform must further answer "can this name participate in deciding the future of land and culture today."

## Who Can Speak for the Truku People?

The easiest mistake in writing about a tribe is to present the Truku people as a set of cultural characteristics for external viewing: white clothing, diamond patterns, hunting knives, canyons, and ancient legends. These contents each have their historical basis but cannot substitute for the community's own description of its life. Contemporary young Truku people may live simultaneously in the village, the city, schools, churches, and cyberspace; identity does not disappear just because they left their ancestral home, nor does it automatically revert to some fixed "tradition" just by returning to the village.

Huang Chang-hsing guides youth on tracing, offering a direction more concrete than "preserving culture": knowledge is not collected by an external expert but confirmed collaboratively in discussions among elders, youth, maps, mountain paths, and the tribe. If researchers, media, or tourists wish to use tribal names, images, stories, or crafts, the first thing they must address is not how to make the content look better, but who consents to its publication, who can correct the narrative, and whether the results return to the people. [^1] [^4]

## From Administrative Classification to Lived Scale

Official recognition solves a classification problem, but what the community faces is a lived problem. Who can introduce themselves with which tribal name, how schools and official documents refer to this group, and how cultural activities present tradition—these are all extensions of the Naming Movement entering daily life. "Disputes over name usage," as noted by academic research institutes, are therefore not just lexical choices but also involve who has the right to describe the community and which histories can be seen. [^5]

The case of the Truku people also strips the administrative number of its cold appearance; the number cannot explain why the community spent over a decade organizing, interviewing, and compiling oral history, or why they needed to bring the tribal name back from a classification table to the village. [^3] [^4]

There are three different scales here. The first is the national scale: recognition in 2004 brought the name into systems. The second is the tribal scale: the Naming Movement re-presents classified history. The third is the village scale: youth follow elders up the mountain, reconnecting the name with topography, hunting grounds, and dwelling ruins. Without any of these, naming remains only on paper.

## After Naming, Who Can Walk into the Mountains?

The recognition of a name does not automatically dissolve the boundaries left by land and systems. The traditional territory of the Truku people includes mountains, hunting grounds, tribal migration, and ancestral memory, while modern governance divides these among different systems such as National Parks, Forestry Management, Wildlife Conservation, and local administration. This is why "naming" must be understood alongside traditional territory tracing, oral history, and autonomous hunting management. [^1] [^2] [^8]

Academic research reminds us that the dilemma of the Naming Movement does not end on the day of administrative approval. Disputes over name usage can still affect the community's political development. [^5] This is not a denial of the naming outcome, but asking a more precise question: after the state recognizes a tribal name, is it also willing to understand the mountain paths, language, norms, and land relationships that this name carries?

Huang Chang-hsing’s tracing provides an answer that does not rely on slogans. He starts from the village, guides youth across cliffs and steep slopes, marks elders' memories onto recognizable topography, and compiles the scattered results into a book. [^1] The Naming Movement allows "Truku" to appear in official classifications. Tracing reconnects this name to the place it once lived.

> **Editor's Note:** The name has returned; the mountain path must still be walked by oneself.

The administrative approval of 2004 is a clear date, but not the end of the story. Only when the community walks again toward Qilai Mountain, the Litwu River, and the three hundred-plus old villages that were recorded does the history of the Truku people transform from a classified name into a path still walked by people.
