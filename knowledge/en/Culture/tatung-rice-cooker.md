> **30-Second Overview**: The Taiwan rice cooker originated from Japanese technology but re-evolved within Taiwan’s specific rice varieties, power infrastructure, processing conditions, and steaming habits. Its classic status lies not just in the Tatung name, but in how a single outer pot allowed families to entrust heat, time, and a meal to one switch.

In 1960, Tatung delivered its first batch of TAC-6 rice cookers into Taiwanese kitchens. This was not a moment where an "invention" suddenly descended: two years prior, Taipei’s Yongda Electric Motor Industry Society had already introduced the Crown brand automatic rice cooker to the market. In that same era, over thirty manufacturers in Taiwan were producing rice cookers. [^1]

Looking back today, the white body, aluminum inner pot, and the black knob in the center of the lid seem as if they have always been there. But initially, it did not face an empty kitchen waiting for modernization; it faced coal briquette stoves, kerosene stoves, large brick stoves, and a city with unstable power. [^2]

Thus, the most striking contrast of the Taiwan rice cooker is: **it looks like a copy of Japanese technology, but what truly remained was Taiwan’s reorganization of rice, electricity, time, and a meal.**

## While the Kitchen Still Burned Coal, the Rice Cooker Learned to Wait for Power

In 1950s Taiwan households, cooking rice, boiling water, and preparing dishes were often completed within the same solid fuel system. For a rice cooker to enter a home, it first had to prove it wouldn’t cause other household lights to flicker or go out. Power supply was insufficient; peak load shedding and energy conservation were daily realities. The capacity of general household meters was typically between 5 and 10 amps. If an 800-watt rice cooker operated simultaneously with an iron and lighting, it could trip the circuit. [^1]

This is why energy-saving models appeared in Taiwan rice cookers early on. The Tatung 10-cup TAC-8 used 800 watts, while the TAC-8s dropped to 600 watts. The capacity was the same, but power consumption differed. This difference was hidden in the small print of product catalogs, revealing engineers asking: "When this family cooks rice, can they still keep the lights on?" [^1]

By the mid-1970s, rice cookers had become the primary cooking system in urban areas. Later, imported Japanese electric rice cookers and locally made electric rice cookers after 1983 appeared, yet the traditional rice cooker did not immediately exit the stage. Until the late 1980s, both types of appliances coexisted in households. [^1]

## Dozens of Brands, Only One Name Remains

Equating "Tatung" with "rice cooker" easily erases other manufacturers from history. Industrial cultural asset data from the National Museum of Science and Technology indicates that in the 1960s, there were over thirty rice cooker manufacturers in Taiwan. Most were concentrated in Taipei, Taichung, and Tainan; many originally produced kerosene stoves, electric fans, motors, transformers, or metal machinery. [^1] [^3]

Therefore, the story of the Taiwan rice cooker is not solely the corporate history of Tatung. It is also a history of the transition from processing industries to home appliance manufacturing: lathes, punch presses, aluminum pot forming, and heating elements were recombined into an item that appeared on dowry lists and in newlywed kitchens. [^1]

Tatung’s advantage lay in timing and production capacity. The company manufactured electric fans after the war, accumulating processing foundations. During the First Five-Year Plan, it received aid from the US to establish a meter factory and collaborated with Japan’s Toshiba on technology, acquiring equipment, hardware processing, and mass production methods. [^1]

This makes "the first unit" an inaccurate question. Museum records cite the 1960 Tatung rice cooker as Taiwan’s first, but food history research reminds us that the Crown brand was already on the market in 1958. A more meaningful question is: Which product most successfully integrated technology, price, distribution channels, and family habits? [^2] [^3]

## Toshiba’s Pot Had to Change Its Cooking Method for Taiwan

The ancestor of the Taiwan rice cooker can be traced to the ER-4 automatic electric rice cooker manufactured by Japan’s Toshiba in 1955. The Japanese model provided a divided inner pot, allowing rice to be cooked on one side while miso soup or curry was heated on the other. This was the answer for nuclear families and _danchi_ (housing complex) living. Hong Kong, meanwhile, introduced Japan’s Matsushita direct-heat automatic keep-warm rice cooker in 1959, which later became another Asian rice cooker lineage. [^1]

The engineers in Taiwan, however, were given a different problem: local families were already accustomed to separating rice and steamed foods. Thus, Tatung and other manufacturers developed an upper steaming basket or upper inner pot, allowing steamed dishes to be stacked above the rice, and usable alone when no steaming was needed. This small accessory changed the usage logic: the rice cooker was no longer just a machine for cooking a pot of rice; it also took over the work of steaming, stewing, and heating dishes on the stove. [^1] [^4]

The white Tatung rice cooker collected by the National Taiwan Museum of History consists of four components: the outer pot, lid, inner pot, and power cord. The collection description also notes it can cook rice, make congee, and handle steamed, boiled, stewed, and braised foods. This list seems ordinary, yet it explains why it resembled the Taiwanese kitchen more than a single-function rice cooker: one outer pot accommodated the different timing of a table full of dishes. [^2]

📝 **Curator’s Note**: The most honest evidence of technology transfer is often hidden in the steaming tray that originally didn’t exist.

## 230°C Is Not Showing Off; It’s for That Pot of Rice

Taiwan and Japan both eat rice, but the rice is not exactly the same. In 1960s Taiwan, new rice, old rice, _zailai_ rice, and _Penglai_ rice coexisted, with different water absorption rates for each variety. Families still cared about the rice aroma produced by coal briquette and large brick stoves. Engineers therefore set the rice cooker’s maximum temperature to approximately 230°C, higher than the Japanese model’s ~180°C. [^1]

The temperature setting also involved the manufacturing site. Early aluminum pots varied in thickness and processing precision, making standardization difficult. Thicker pots absorbed more heat and cooled slower. Raising the temperature was both to cook the rice and to use thermal energy to compensate for material and processing variations. This made "localization" more than a cultural slogan; it was an engineer’s adjustment of the knob and heating element in response to an unevenly thick aluminum pot. [^1] [^5]

The lid was not always quiet either. During technology transfer, the difference between Japan’s 100V and Taiwan’s 110V could cause steam to vibrate and lift the lid. Later, as lids became thinner, users still reported jumping and noise during cooking. Engineers added a gap next to the lid to provide an outlet for saturated steam. Grooves on the bottom of the inner pot also increased in response to usage. [^1]

These modifications do not appear in photos of the "Japanese prototype," but are hidden in the sounds Taiwanese people hear daily: the switch pops up, steam dissipates, and the rice remains in the pot.

## Dowries, Dormitories, and Overseas Suitcases

The Tatung rice cooker later came in red and green. The Taiwan Industrial Cultural Assets Network records that red was linked to the "Dowry Appliances" series, while green was launched alongside green refrigerators. Colors placed a cold appliance into life events: marriage, starting a family, moving, or taking a pot from parents’ home. [^3]

The National Taiwan Museum of History lists this white rice cooker under "Artifacts > Dining Utensils > Cooking Tools," with Taiwan as the place of manufacture and a historical period extending from 1965 to the present. It was collected not because every rice cooker is rare, but because common life needs proof from a concrete pot. [^2]

This artifact also left Taiwan with its people. An article from the NTU International Chinese Language Program (ICLP) compiled the significance of the Tatung rice cooker in the lives of Taiwanese-Americans and foreigners residing in Taiwan: it could continue to cook familiar rice after migration and became a small container for imagining Taiwan. [^6]

The company states it has produced rice cookers and steamers since 1960, selling over 15 million units in sixty years. This is a cumulative figure provided by the enterprise and cannot be directly equated with household penetration rates in Taiwan. But it at least shows how a product transformed from a market option into a multi-generational, cross-national portable item. [^7]

## The Tatung Baby: The Child Who Saved Coins Beside the Rice Cooker

If the rice cooker is Tatung’s body entering the home, the Tatung Baby is the face that grew in the living room. Official Tatung data records that in ROC Year 58 (1969), purchasing Tatung home appliances worth 10,000 NT dollars entitled the buyer to a corporate doll wearing a red helmet and holding an olive football. At the time, materials were not abundant; this doll was often placed on TVs or refrigerators, transforming from a promotional gift into a household ornament. [^12]

This era is crucial. Rice cookers, TVs, refrigerators, and electric fans were bringing families into appliance-based living. Companies no longer just sold a product; they wanted the brand to remain visible in the place seen every day upon returning home. The Tatung Baby thus shared the same living scene with the rice cooker: one responsible for cooking, the other standing by watching the family grow. [^14] [^15]

Tatung officially described the first batch of dolls as the "eyes-closed meditation" pose, using a square logo on the chest, later changing to an open-eyed, circular logo appearance. The coin slot on top, the founding year number on the chest, the oversized head, and large feet were not arbitrary decorations. Officially, they are interpreted as corporate meanings of frugality, thinking, practice, and being grounded. [^12]

The "Ceramic No. 71 Tatung Baby" recorded in the National Cultural Memory Bank preserves this visual language as a concrete artifact. It wears a red-and-white helmet, holds an olive football in its left hand, has its right hand on its hip, and has a gold "71" printed on the front of its tracksuit. The record notes that Tatung made the first Tatung Baby in 1969 for its 51st anniversary, subsequently using the chest number to mark the founding anniversary year. [^13]

The olive football is not the most intuitive choice for a Taiwanese corporate mascot today. Tatung’s official explanation is that in 1969, the olive football was popular in Taiwan; the image of players cooperating and sending the ball toward the goal corresponded to the company’s emphasis on integrity, diligence, frugality, and service spirit. This is a corporate self-interpretation of the shape, not a complete sports history, but it transforms the ball in the doll’s hand from a prop into a clue of the era. [^12]

The interesting thing about the Tatung Baby is that it is simultaneously a commodity, a piggy bank, a promotional gift, and a family memory. The English edition of _The Global View_ magazine described it as a ceramic figurine common in Taiwanese households, noting that the child’s action of inserting coins made frugality not just a slogan, but a daily repeatable ritual. This observation aligns with the keywords "savings, piggy bank, toy" in the National Culture Memory Bank collection. [^13] [^15]

But the Tatung Baby cannot only be written as warm nostalgia. Compilations by StoryStudio show that early versions like the first No. 51, due to differences in quantity, material, and preservation status, have gradually entered the collectibles market. When an object originally given away with appliances begins to be valued separately, it transforms from a marketing item into a historical artifact of Taiwan’s consumer society. [^14]

From this perspective, the Tatung Baby adds a layer to the rice cooker’s story: the rice cooker brings heat into the kitchen, the Baby places the brand image in the living room. When appearing together, Tatung sold not just home appliances, but an imagination of "domestic goods are reliable, days will get better" established in post-war families. This imagination would later be re-collected, re-designed, and even carried in the memories of overseas Taiwanese. [^6] [^15]

## When "Smart" Arrived, Why Did the Old Pot Remain?

Electric rice cookers can precisely control temperature; pressure cookers can shorten time; smart home appliances can connect to phones. In contrast, the traditional rice cooker has only one main switch: add water to the outer pot, and it automatically pops up when the water evaporates. The National Taiwan Museum of History’s description even lists "simple structure, low price, durability" as reasons it is still used. [^2]

But "only one switch" is not the whole answer. The rice cooker delegates complexity to the user: how many times to wash the rice, how much water to add to the outer pot, whether to raise the steaming tray, when to put sweet potatoes in. Its flexibility thus comes from family experience, not menus on a display. Everyone can use the same pot to cook a slightly different meal.

National Chengchi University’s post-war food research places the 1950–1970 family dining table within the imagination of modern life. The rice cooker sits precisely at that turning point: it made cooking easier to split into several parallel steps, and allowed family members not to constantly stand by the fire. [^8]

The Taiwan Rice Food Culture Association, when compiling the history of rice, also placed the Tatung rice cooker in a position of continued sales after the appearance of electric rice cookers. This reminds us that an artifact’s lifespan is not determined solely by new technology. As long as it can still hold a food memory, old methods have reasons to remain. [^9]

## A Pot’s Parts Correspond to a Whole System of Household Division of Labor

The museum’s component list breaks the Tatung rice cooker into an outer pot, lid, inner pot, and power cord. This breakdown is interesting because users often remember not a complete machine, but the feel of a specific component: the inner pot taken to the sink to wash rice, the lid becoming hot in the steam, the power cord stored behind the cabinet, and the outer pot always remaining on the countertop. [^2]

The shape of the outer pot is not neutral. Collection data describes it as a bucket-shaped vessel, wider at the top and narrower at the bottom, with three bakelite feet at the bottom, a lever switch near the bottom edge on the front, and a power cord socket on the side. These details make the rice cooker a household item that can be moved, wiped, disassembled, and recombined, rather than a closed device that can only be sent back to the factory. [^10]

The inner pot is a different scale. Early collected inner pots were made of aluminum metal, with vertical walls, rounded lips, and two side ears for easy handling when serving rice. Modern inner pots mostly use stainless steel, reflecting changes in material choice and health considerations. In other words, the rice cooker continues not every material, but the daily action of "the inner pot can be taken out." [^11]

This detachable, washable, and replaceable structure also explains why old rice cookers are often kept. As long as the outer pot can still heat, the inner pot, lid, or power cord may each find replacements. The artifact does not need to end its life with the scrapping of the whole unit; it can continue to work in the home in the form of parts.

## Family Time Was Re-arranged

The change brought by the rice cooker was not just swapping fire for electricity. It split cooking into several staggerable time slots: wash rice, add water, press the switch, then go prepare side dishes or do chores. When the water evaporates and the switch pops up, the act of cooking rice reminds the user of completion via sound. This ability to "leave the stove" meant the kitchen no longer required one person to stand continuously at the range. [^1] [^8]

But this convenience should not be written as a simple story of liberation. After the rice cooker entered the home, tasks like washing rice, cutting vegetables, plating, and cleaning the inner pot still needed to be done, often by women. A more accurate statement is that the rice cooker redistributed work rhythm but did not automatically eliminate internal family division of labor. This is a social layer that cannot be ignored when reading "kitchen electrification." [^8]

From the food perspective, the rice cooker placed rice at the core while expanding the scope of steaming, boiling, stewing, and braising. Steamed eggs, sweet potatoes, pork rib soup, braised pork, and leftover dishes could all be completed using the outer pot’s water and heat. These uses were not necessarily the most important functions in the original product manual, but accumulated in user experience into a trust that "anything can be put in." [^2] [^4]

Therefore, the cultural value of the rice cooker lies not only in its ability to cook a pot of rice. It concentrated Taiwan’s common rice foods, steaming/boiling habits, and family shared meals onto a single predictable heat source. The Taiwan Rice Food Culture Association placing the Tatung rice cooker in a position of continued sales after the appearance of electric rice cookers illustrates one thing: when an artifact is bound to a staple food, family rhythm, and memory, upgrading and replacement will not just be a comparison of performance. [^9]

## From "Tatung" Back to Taiwan

If we only say "the Tatung rice cooker accompanied Taiwanese people for half a century," the story is too smooth. It was once a reference for Japanese technology and competed with over thirty Taiwanese manufacturers. The reason it remained is not that Taiwanese people rejected more complex machines, but that this pot early on placed limited power, different rice varieties, steaming habits, and processing conditions into the same outer pot. [^1] [^5]

Therefore, reducing the Taiwan rice cooker to "one brand represents a nation" is still too simple. It is more like a collaborative work completed by engineers, housewives, user feedback, aluminum pot factories, power systems, and rice. The Tatung name became loud, but it cannot erase the hands that together made the rice cooker look like Taiwan.

Next time you press that black switch, notice that it does not tell you what to cook. It only heats, waits, and then pops up at the moment the water disappears. From 1960 to today, what was truly preserved may not be a specific model, but the Taiwanese family habit of entrusting a meal to time and believing that when they return, there will be steam in the pot.
