## Origins and Introduction to Taiwan

In April 1977, Professor Fang Jui-min returned to Taiwan from St Paul's College in Shrewsbury, UK, carrying a Tchoukball net purchased for £30 and an English rulebook. He had just witnessed this sport—invented in 1970 by Swiss biologist Hermann Brandt—for the first time at the annual meeting of the International Sports Federation in Belgium. The sport is named after the "tchouk" sound made when the ball hits the elastic net.

Designed originally to reduce sports injuries through an emphasis on "zero physical contact," this "gentleman's ball" grew from Taiwan's elementary school PE classes into a global powerhouse. Upon his return, Fang Jui-min immediately held workshops at Keelung High School and the NTNU (National Taiwan Normal University) gymnasium, distributing rules and equipment to students and teachers. The Ministry of Education later integrated Tchoukball into the primary and secondary school curricula; within just a few years, over 200 school teams had formed across Taiwan.

📝 **Curator's Note**: A sport invented by a Swiss professional to "reduce sports injuries" became a highly popular competitive item in Taiwan's schools. This contrast is a story that is quintessentially Taiwanese.

## Rules and Characteristics

The rules are simple enough to learn in five minutes: the attacking player throws the ball at the elastic net from outside the forbidden zone; the opposing team must catch the ball before it hits the ground, or they lose a point; after catching it, they immediately counter-attack. Players must release the ball within three seconds, can take a maximum of three steps, and are prohibited from dribbling or physical contact. While it sounds like a gentle version of dodgeball, masters of the game can impart spins that create eerie angles, making the ball impossible to catch—this is precisely the specialty of Taiwanese players.

Taiwanese athletes excel particularly in "spin shots" and "rapid reactions," bringing a high-intensity competitive edge to an otherwise gentle-looking sport. In international competitions, it is common to see the Taiwan team use precise angles and seamless coordination to overturn scores in the final seconds.

📝 **Curator's Note**: The core design of the rules is "zero contact," yet in Taiwan, it has developed a very high technical threshold. This is a microcosm of Taiwanese sporting culture—replacing brute force with intelligence and teamwork.

## Taiwan's Glorious Record on the International Stage

Since the 1980s, Taiwan has participated in the World Championships, winning the men's title almost every year and consistently placing in the top two for the women's division. According to statistics from the International Tchoukball Federation (FITB), Taiwan has lost only a few finals since 1980, maintaining its long-term position as world number one.

In August 2025, a junior girls' team composed of students from Taipei Bihu Elementary School and Guting Elementary School made their first foray into the Thailand World Junior Beach Tchoukball Championships. They secured the world championship after three consecutive victories against Thailand and Hong Kong. Coach Lin Cheng-chieh (an alumnus of Bihu Elementary who returned to coach for 15 years) was moved to tears after the match: "The children turned the impossible into possible through their sweat." Team captain Wu Yung-yao remarked, "Before we left, we thought we were just going to a competition; we never imagined we would actually bring the trophy back to Taiwan."

From its introduction in 1977 to the elementary school victory in 2025, Taiwan has transformed an obscure sport into a school culture passed down through generations over 43 years—this is a true grassroots miracle.

## International Contributions and Domestic Challenges

The President of the International Tchoukball Federation (FITB) is indeed Taiwanese, Huang Chin-cheng. For over 20 years, he has personally spent over 6 million TWD to establish the federation's headquarters in Kaohsiung and has traveled the world to promote the sport. The Taiwan team once nearly missed the World Championships due to a lack of funding; as a result, the Canadian and Swiss teams publicly stated, "If Taiwan doesn't go, we won't go either," leading to the cancellation of the World Cup—an extremely rare occurrence in the history of single-sport federations.

However, very few people domestically recognize this "pride of Taiwan." With limited resources, there are only a few full-time coaches across Taiwan (such as Liao Hsueh-ming of Suxian Elementary School in New Taipei City), and because it is not an Olympic or Asian Games sport, funding and attention remain insufficient. Athletes often balance studying and training, relying on passion and synergy to sustain their world-class record.

📝 **Curator's Note**: Taiwan's strongest sports are often not the ones discussed daily. Tchoukball reminds us that true strength is sometimes hidden in the quietest corners of our school campuses.

## Future Outlook

Next time you pass by an elementary school playground and hear a crisp "tchouk" sound, do not assume it is just children playing a game. It might be future world champions practicing—and this sport, in the most Taiwanese way possible, continues to quietly dominate the international stage.

With the rise of new formats like Beach Tchoukball and the continued efforts of more elementary and junior high school teams, Taiwan is poised to bring the glory of this "invisible champion" into more international competitions and the public eye over the next decade.
