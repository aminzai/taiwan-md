> **30-Second Overview:** Mayasvi is often translated as the "War Festival," but today's ritual does not reenact headhunting or warfare. Centered on the _kuba_—the Tsou men's lodge—it allows clans to reaffirm their responsibilities through spirit-welcoming, song and dance, the sacred house, and spirit-sending. The most striking contrast is this: the War Festival preserves not violence, but how the Tsou continue to organize their community after violence has left daily life.

## The Fire of Dapan, Not Primarily for Tourists

In Dapan on Alishan, the Mayasvi site is not a temporary stage erected for the occasion. It begins with the _kuba_: a raised lodge with a thatched roof, constructed with wooden pillars and bamboo, featuring a ritual plaza in front and sacred flowers (wood orchids) and sacred trees (red banyans) planted nearby, which the tribe regards as sacred. This building is not merely a display of "traditional style"; it is the political, religious, educational, and public deliberation center of the Tsou great clan. [^1] [^2]

The festival date is not a single, unified date for all Tsou people. Official English records indicate that Dapan and Tebuina have alternated hosting the festival, and dates have shifted between traditional practices and fixed dates. In recent years, the method has returned to being determined by the host tribe. Writing Mayasvi as a fixed annual festival held simultaneously by all Tsou in Taiwan erases the differences between tribes. [^3]

The Tsou are primarily distributed in the Alishan mountain area of Chiayi, with other members residing in Xinyi, Nantou, and Namasia, Kaohsiung. The Indigenous Peoples Commission's ethnic data describes Tsou seasonal rituals separately: the _homeyaya_ (Millet Harvest Festival) focuses on thanking the millet god and strengthening family bonds. _Mayasvi_, however, is linked to victory, the war god, ancestral spirits, unity, and tribal memory. [^1]

**Curator's Note:** The difficulty with Mayasvi lies not in translating it into a specific Chinese name, but in acknowledging that "War Festival" is merely an entry point, not the answer.

## What the Name "War Festival" Leaves Behind

In earlier Tsou society, _Mayasvi_ was connected to headhunting, warfare, and rituals marking the return of warriors. Indigenous literature cites 1915 Japanese survey records, noting that early rituals included segments centered on human head offerings. However, the same study clearly states that while inter-tribal headhunting customs have disappeared, the War Festival continues in Tebuina and Dapan. [^4]

There is a time gap here that cannot be omitted: the ritual has not survived intact to the present day. It has changed in content, participants, modes of observation, and the language tribes use to talk about themselves. Researcher Pu Zhong-yong views the contemporary War Festival as a collective religious activity and a field of ethnic identity. Another post-colonial study points out that the ritual is constantly decontextualized and recontextualized in modern life, producing different understandings such as nostalgia, decolonization, and cultural hybridity. [^4] [^5]

Therefore, "War Festival" cannot be simplified into two extremes: one viewing it as a distant, pure, unchanged ancient relic; the other dismissing it as an outdated remnant because it was once associated with violence. The contemporary significance of Mayasvi lies precisely in the continuous negotiation between historical memory and daily life.

The Chinese terms "War Festival," "Unity Festival," and "Victory Festival" each capture part of the meaning, but no single word can encompass the entire ritual. Researchers saw another narrative in the 1999 Tebuina War Festival poster: tribe members used the phrase "Fire of Kuba" to explain how to place ancestral wisdom, the current tribe, and the future world in the same sentence. This shift in naming is significant because it moves the focus from "what wars were fought" to "how the tribe keeps the fire burning today." [^4]

Therefore, this article retains the Tsou name _Mayasvi_, treating Chinese translations not as the only correct answer. The coexistence of names is not chaos, but different historical layers remaining simultaneously in the ritual: memories of war merit, ethnic unity, spiritual order, return, and cultural inheritance overlap to constitute today's Mayasvi.

## Kuba is Not Background, But Institution

The Tsou _kuba_ is often translated as "men's lodge." This translation points to entry norms but easily leads external readers to mistake it for merely a house where men gather. The registration data for traditional wisdom creations of the Dapan _kuba_ describes it as the center of political, religious, social operation, and cultural ethics. When major events occur, the chief, clan elders, and warriors still gather here to deliberate and handle issues. [^2]

Its architectural details are not a list of decorations. The lodge door faces east toward the sunrise, with wood orchids planted near the roof and entrance. The red banyan beside the plaza is pruned during rituals, leaving only three branches, symbolizing the path for deities to descend to the ritual site. The hearth, fire-starting tools, floor, and preserved old pillars inside the lodge connect respectively to rituals, clan authority, refuge functions, and memories of generational reconstruction. [^2] [^3]

"Preserving the oldest pillar" is one of the details in the _kuba_ that best explains the Tsou view of time. Dapan's registration data records that when the lodge is rebuilt, the oldest pillar is preserved and bound with rattan to the new pillar. The new lodge can be rebuilt, materials can be updated, but the old pillar remains bound beside the new one. This is not sealing the building into a museum, but allowing the update itself to carry the支点 (fulcrum) left by ancestors. [^2]

Official ethnic data for the Tsou also places the _kuba_ at the intersection of men's education, tribal meetings, war mobilization, ritual training, hunting socializing, and public affairs. The lodge's integration with annual rituals represents that it is not a venue "borrowed for use" by the festival. Rather, the ritual is a way the lodge, as a public institution, continues to operate. [^1]

## From Welcoming Spirits to Sending Them Off: A Ritual That Is Not a Single Dance

Seasonal ritual data breaks _Mayasvi_ into multiple segments: rebuilding the lodge, start of the festival, welcoming spirits, unity, first entry into the lodge, coming-of-age ceremony, reporting war merits, road rituals, song and dance festival, and closing ritual. Procedures and names may differ among tribes; data should not be treated as a uniform flowchart for all Tsou. [^6]

During spirit-welcoming, tribe members bring the fire from the _kuba_ into the ritual site, offer sacrificial gifts, and prune the red banyan, leaving three branches pointing to the lodge and sacred house. Official English cultural maps interpret these branches as the ladder for the war god to descend from the heavenly realm. This action simultaneously binds myth, plants, architecture, and clan space together. [^3]

Next comes song and dance. War festival songs consist of polyphonic choral singing, slow rhythms, and heavy harmonies, preserving myths, ancestral deeds, and tribal history. Research indicates that these songs and dances carry ethnic aesthetics but, due to their visibility, have become the intersection of contemporary artistic preservation and the tourist gaze. [^4]

After the main priest segment, not everyone is excluded. English reports and official data record that during the song and dance festival, tribe members, led by elders, hold hands and sing, praising the war god and the heroic deeds of ancestors. This arrangement extends Mayasvi from a sacred space centered on adult men to the shared memory of the entire tribe. [^3] [^7]

The closing ritual must be completed before midnight. Warriors sing the spirit-welcoming, spirit-sending, and war songs; the central hearth is extinguished, and the ritual is considered concluded. Fire is not an event effect, but the boundary of ritual time: it is welcomed, guarded, and formally sent away. [^3]

**Curator's Note:** A lodge does not preserve an "ancient appearance," but rather who can enter when, who is responsible for what, and whose memories are sung by whom.

## The Distance Between Sacred Space and External Observation

Mayasvi has been incorporated into the national context of important folklore preservation. A 2011 certification report records that the War Festival, centered on the _kuba_, assumes functions of remembering ancestors, transmitting culture, uniting tribe members, and maintaining tribal ethics. This status as a national cultural asset grants the ritual resources and public visibility, making it easier for outsiders to treat it as a "show worth visiting." [^8]

Two things must hold simultaneously. The preservation of cultural assets is important. Tribes also have the right to decide how the ritual is seen, when it is open, and what areas are off-limits. Official visitor information clearly states that morning rituals are not open to public participation; tourists may not enter the ritual site or interrupt the ceremony. Regardless of whether a festival is held, women may not enter the _kuba_. These norms are not travel tips, but the Tsou's sovereign boundaries for sacred space. [^3]

A 2014 Indigenous Peoples literature source more sharply describes problems brought by commercialization: external visitors and large TV screens turn the ritual into a spectacle for viewing, potentially causing the original collectivity to become fragmented, blurred, and discontinuous. Festival songs may mix with rock and Western religious music, forming new hybrid forms. [^4]

This does not mean all changes are "distortion," nor that change equals progress. Post-colonial research reminds us that Tsou people use, adjust, and reinterpret Mayasvi within modern society. What truly needs respect is that this negotiation is conducted by the tribe itself, not decided by external audiences on what counts as "pure." [^5]

External observation has another easily ignored layer: audiences do not just watch dances; they also extract "men's lodge," "warrior costumes," and "sacred trees" as cultural symbols to take away. But for tribe members, these objects belong to different responsibilities and taboos and cannot be arbitrarily pulled out of the ritual context. Official data reminds visitors not to enter the ritual site or interrupt the ceremony. This boundary tells audiences that understanding need not come at the cost of closeness or photography. [^3]

For Taiwan.md, this is also a boundary in writing: we can explain ritual processes and public data, but we do not write closed rituals as "I will reveal the secrets for you," nor do we package tribal taboos as travel guides. What the article can do is bring readers to the boundary, letting them know why they must stop there.

The gender norms of the lodge cannot be extracted as a separate curiosity segment. Official data simultaneously records that women participate in certain segments of the ritual, such as holding torches; the song and dance festival is joined by all tribe members led by elders. In other words, "cannot enter the kuba" and "do not participate in the festival" are not the same thing. The former is a norm of sacred space; the latter is a conclusion external readers easily jump to. [^1] [^3]

Equally unignorable is age. Dapan lodge data records that Tsou men have relationships with the _kuba_ from childhood: first entry into the lodge, learning hunting and combat skills, listening to tribal history—all are educational processes placing the individual within the clan and great clan. Even if warfare and hunting are no longer the center of life, the lodge still bears the memory of "how to become a tribe member." [^2]

This gives the collectivity of Mayasvi a concrete shape. It is not abstractly saying "everyone unites," but different clans bringing their own history, sacred houses, and responsibilities, walking into shared time according to a recognizable procedure. The ritual unites tribe members precisely because it does not eliminate differences, but allows differences to find their places within the lodge and ritual site.

## After War Leaves, the Ritual Leaves Communal Life

Today's Mayasvi no longer centers on warfare or hunting for Tsou daily life. It retains the war god, ancestors, warriors, fire, and songs, as well as the lodge's entry norms and ritual order. But these elements are placed into a new question: when tribe members disperse to cities, and when language and lifestyles face assimilation pressure, how does the tribe remember who it is?

The answer is not returning to a pre-modern past. The answer is more like returning annually to the _kuba_, relighting the fire, allowing different clans to meet in the same time frame, letting young people know which songs cannot be preserved by text alone, and letting elders, warriors, women, children, and returning tribe members each find their place of participation. English reports call this function social regeneration. Researchers call it a field where ethnic subjectivity is continuously constructed. [^5] [^7]

Thus, the most important thing about Mayasvi is not "the Tsou still preserve an ancient ritual." A more accurate statement is: the Tsou have transformed a ritual carrying war memories into an institution for repeatedly practicing communal life. The fire of the _kuba_ going out does not mean the story ends. It simply returns the responsibility of the next gathering to the tribe.

This transformation also explains why Mayasvi cannot be placed solely in the "festival" category. Festivals usually suggest a single annual point in time. Mayasvi, however, strings together annual clan relations, song learning, lodge maintenance, sacred space, and public deliberation. The ritual is the visible few days; the _kuba_ and tribal ethics are what continue to operate on the remaining days.

When the next fire is welcomed into the lodge on Alishan, Tsou people welcome not just deities, nor a replicable "traditional performance." They welcome a question: in new life, who is willing to return, who remembers how to sing, who is responsible for binding the pillars together. Mayasvi does not seal away the answer. It asks the question anew each year.

This gives "preservation" a meaning different from static display. Preservation is not freezing the ritual in a specific year, but preserving the tribe's right to decide dates, maintain the lodge, arrange roles, limit observation, and interpret changes. As long as these decisions return to the tribe, Mayasvi is not a shadow of the past, but present-tense public life.

For those hearing this name for the first time, the best entry point may not be remembering a long list of ritual names, but remembering three connected objects: a pile of fire welcomed into the ritual site, a red banyan leaving three branches, an old pillar bound to a new one. Fire points to shared time, the red banyan points to the sacred path, the old pillar points to unbroken generational relationships. These three objects are closer to the actual weight of Mayasvi than "ancient" or "mysterious."

They also remind readers that the core of the ritual lies not only in the liveliest moments of song and dance. The enduring work happens in every decision where fire is prepared, trees are pruned, pillars are preserved, and tribe members learn when to approach and when to step back.

These works are not always captured by lenses, but they are the conditions allowing the ritual to continue.

They allow the fire of Mayasvi to illuminate not just one night, but how the tribe hands memory to the next generation.
