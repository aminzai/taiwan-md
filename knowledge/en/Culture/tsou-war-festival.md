> **30-Second Overview:** Mayasvi is often translated as "war ritual," but today's ceremony is not about replaying headhunting or war. It centers on the _kuba_—the Tsou men's lodge—where various clans reaffirm their responsibilities through welcoming spirits, dancing, rituals at the house, and sending off spirits. The most notable contrast is this: the ritual preserves not violence, but how the Tsou continue to organize as a community after violence has left daily life.

## The Fire of Daban Was Not Meant for Tourists

At Daban in Alishan, the ritual site for Mayasvi is not a temporary stage. It begins with the _kuba_: an elevated lodge constructed of thatch and wooden posts, situated before a ceremonial plaza, flanked by _mokulan_ (a flower considered sacred by the tribe) and the _chiruong_ tree (a sacred tree). This building is not merely an exhibit in "traditional style"; it is the political, religious, educational, and public negotiation center of the Tsou community.[^1] [^2]

The annual festival does not have a single unified date for all Tsou. Official English records indicate that Daban and Tufuya have taken turns hosting the ceremonies, with dates sometimes adjusted between traditional practice and fixed calendars. In recent years, however, it has returned to being determined by the host clan.[^3]

The Tsou people are mainly distributed in the Alishan area of Chiayi, with members also residing in Xinyi, Nantou, and Namaxia, Kaohsiung. The Indigenous Peoples Council separates the seasonal rituals of the Tsou: _homeyaya_, the rice harvest festival, focuses on thanking the millet god and strengthening kinship. Mayasvi, conversely, is connected to victory, war gods, ancestors, unity, and clan memory.[^1]

## What Does the Name "War Ritual" Leave Behind?

In earlier Tsou society, Mayasvi was linked to rituals of headhunting, war, and the return of warriors. Indigenous literature cites Japanese survey records from 1915, which indicate segments in early rituals centered on human sacrifice. However, the same study clearly points out that inter-clan headhunting customs had disappeared, yet the war rituals continued in Tufuya and Daban.[^4]

There is a temporal gap here that cannot be omitted: the ritual did not survive unchanged to today. Its content, participants, modes of viewing, and even the language used by the clan have changed. Researcher Pu Zhong-yong views contemporary war rituals as a field of collective religious activity and ethnic identity. Another postcolonial study points out that the rituals are continuously decontextualized and recontextualized in modern life, leading to various understandings such as nostalgia, decolonization, and cultural hybridization.[^4] [^5]

Therefore, "war ritual" cannot be simplified into two extremes: one is treating it as a distant, pure, unchanging ancient relic; the other is dismissing it as an outdated remnant because of its past association with violence. The contemporary meaning of Mayasvi lies in the continuous negotiation between historical memory and daily life.

The Chinese terms "war ritual" (戰祭), "unity ritual" (團結祭), and "victory ritual" (凱旋祭) each capture a part of the meaning, but no single word encompasses the entire ceremony. A saying found on a Tufuya war festival poster in 1999 suggests another interpretation: the clan uses the "fire of _kuba_" to place ancestral wisdom, contemporary clan life, and the future world into one sentence. This naming shift is significant because it moves the focus from "what battles were fought" to "how does the clan keep its fire today."[^4]

For this reason, this article retains the Tsou word _mayasvi_, refusing to treat the Chinese translation as the sole correct answer. The coexistence of names is not chaos; rather, it is different historical layers simultaneously present in the ritual: memories of military achievements, ethnic unity, divine order, return home, and cultural inheritance overlap to constitute today's Mayasvi.

## _Kuba_ Is Not Background; It Is Institution

The Tsou _kuba_ is often translated as a "men's lodge." This translation points to the rules of entry but also easily leads external readers to assume it is merely a place for men to gather. The traditional wisdom creation records from Daban Clan describe it as the center of political, religious, social operation, and cultural ethics. During major events, chiefs, clan elders, and warriors still gather here to discuss and resolve issues.[^2]

Its architectural details are not mere decorative lists. The lodge faces east toward sunrise, and _mokulan_ is planted near the roof and entrance. The _chiruong_ tree beside the plaza is pruned during rituals, leaving only three branches that symbolize the path for deities to descend to the ritual site. The hearth, fire-starting apparatus cage, floor, and preserved old posts inside the lodge are connected to ritual, clan authority, refuge function, and memory of generational reconstruction.[^2] [^3]

"Preserving the oldest post" is one detail in the _kuba_ that best illustrates the Tsou concept of time. The records from Daban Clan state that when the lodge was rebuilt, the oldest standing pillar was preserved and bound next to a new pillar with vine bark. A new lodge can be rebuilt; materials can be updated, but the old post is still bound beside the new one. This is not turning architecture into a museum; it is allowing renewal itself to carry the anchor left by ancestors.[^2]

Official Tsou clan data also places the _kuba_ at the intersection of male education, clan meetings, military gatherings, festival training, hunting socialization, and public affairs. The lodge is linked to the annual rituals, meaning it is not a place "borrowed" for the ceremony. On the contrary, the ceremony is one way that the _kuba_, as a public institution, continues to operate.[^1]

## From Welcoming Spirits to Sending Them Off: A Ritual That Is Not a Single Dance

Seasonal ritual data breaks down Mayasvi into multiple sections: building the lodge, commencement of the festival, welcoming spirits, unity, first entry to the _kuba_, coming-of-age ceremony, reporting military achievements, funeral rites, singing/dancing festivals, and concluding ceremonies. The procedures and names may differ between clans; this data should not be treated as a process chart identical for all Tsou. [^6]

When welcoming spirits, the clan brings the fire from the _kuba_ to the ritual site, offers sacrifices, and prunes the _chiruong_, leaving three branches pointing toward the lodge and the ritual house. The official English cultural map interprets these branches as steps by which war gods descend from the celestial realm. This action binds mythology, plants, architecture, and clan space together.[^3]

Next is the singing and dancing. War songs are composed of multi-part chorus, slow rhythms, and rich harmonies, with lyrics preserving myths, ancestral deeds, and clan history. Research points out that these dances carry ethnic aesthetics but also become a boundary for contemporary art preservation and tourist gaze because they are easily observed.[^4]

After the main ritual segment, not everyone is excluded. English reports and official documents record that during the singing/dancing festival, clansmen, led by elders, hold hands and sing praises of the heroic deeds of war gods and ancestors. This arrangement extends Mayasvi from a sacred space centered on adult males to the collective memory of the entire clan.[^3] [^7]

The concluding ceremony must be completed before midnight; warriors sing welcoming, sending-off, and war songs, and only when the central hearth is extinguished does the festival conclude. The fire is not an effect for show; it is the boundary of ritual time: it is welcomed in, guarded, and formally sent away.[^3]

**Curator's Note:** A lodge preserves not an "ancient appearance," but who can enter at what time, who is responsible for what, and whose memories are sung out.

## The Distance Between Sacred Space and External Viewing

Mayasvi has been incorporated into the national framework of important folk customs. A 2011 accreditation report records that Mayasvi centers on the _kuba_, fulfilling functions such as remembering ancestors, inheriting culture, uniting clans, and maintaining clan ethics. This status as a national cultural asset provides the ritual with resources and public visibility, making it easier for outsiders to view it as an "event worth visiting."[^8]

Two things must hold true simultaneously. The preservation of cultural assets is important. Clans also have the right to decide how the rituals are viewed, when they are open, and where entry is prohibited. Official visitor information clearly states that morning ceremonies are not open to the public, and tourists may not enter the ritual site or interrupt the ceremony. Regardless of whether a festival is held, women are forbidden from entering the _kuba_. These regulations are not tourist tips; they are the Tsou people's sovereign boundaries for sacred space.[^3]

Indigenous literature from 2014 describes the problems brought by commercialization even more sharply: external visitors and large television screens turn the rituals into performances to be watched, potentially causing the original collectivity to become fractured, vague, or discontinuous. Festival songs may also incorporate rock music and Western religious music, forming new hybrid forms.[^4]

This does not mean all changes are "distortion," nor does it mean that change equals progress. Postcolonial studies remind us that the Tsou people use, adjust, and reinterpret Mayasvi in modern society. What truly deserves respect is this negotiation conducted by the clan itself, not what external audiences decide is authentic.[^5]

There is another layer of external viewing that is easily overlooked: viewers do not just watch the dance; they also dissect "men's lodge," "warrior attire," and "sacred tree" into detachable cultural symbols. But for the clansmen, these objects belong to different responsibilities and taboos and cannot be arbitrarily extracted from the ritual context. Official documents remind visitors not to enter the ritual site or interrupt the ceremony. This boundary tells the audience that understanding does not require proximity or filming.[^3]

For Taiwan.md, this is also a writing boundary: we can explain the ritual process and public data without framing the closed rituals as "us revealing secrets for you," nor package clan taboos into a travel guide. What the article can do is lead the reader to the boundary, making them understand why it must stop there.

The gender regulations of the lodge cannot be isolated into a sensationalist section either. Official documents also record that women participate in certain segments of the ritual by carrying torches, and the singing/dancing festival involves all clansmen led by elders. In other words, "not allowed to enter the _kuba_" is not the same as "not participating in the ceremony." The former is a regulation of sacred space; the latter is an easy conclusion for external readers.[^1] [^3]

Age cannot be overlooked either. Daban Clan records show that Tsou men have been involved with the _kuba_ from childhood: first entry to the lodge, learning hunting and martial skills, listening to clan history—all place the individual within the educational process of the clan and community. Even if warfare and hunting are no longer central to daily life, the lodge still holds the memory of "how to become a clan member."[^2]

This gives Mayasvi its concrete form of collectivity. It is not abstractly saying "everyone unites"; rather, it is different clans proceeding through a recognizable process, each bringing their own history, ritual house, and responsibilities into a shared time. The festival can unite the clan precisely because it does not erase differences but allows differences to find their place within the lodge and the ritual site.

## After War Left, the Festival Leaves Community Life

Today's Mayasvi is no longer centered on warfare or hunting in Tsou daily life. It retains the war god, ancestors, warriors, fire, and song, as well as the entry regulations and ceremonial order of the _kuba_. But these elements are placed into a new question: when clansmen disperse to cities, and language and lifestyles face assimilation pressure, how does the clan remember who it is?

The answer is not returning to a past devoid of modernity. The answer is more like returning to the _kuba_ every year, relighting the fire, allowing different clans to meet in the same time, letting young people know which songs cannot be preserved by text alone, and enabling elders, warriors, women, children, and returnees to find their place in participation. English reports call this function social regeneration. Researchers refer to it as a field where ethnic subjectivity is continuously constructed.[^5] [^7]

Therefore, the most important thing about Mayasvi is not that "the Tsou still have an ancient ritual." A more accurate description is: the Tsou transform a ritual carrying memories of war into a system for repeatedly practicing community life. The extinguishing of the _kuba_'s fire does not mean the end of the story. It simply returns the responsibility of gathering again to the clan.

This transformation also explains why Mayasvi cannot be placed solely in the category of "festival." A festival usually suggests a single point in time each year. Mayasvi weaves together kinship relations, song learning, lodge maintenance, sacred space, and public negotiation throughout the year. The ceremony is the visible few days; the _kuba_ and clan ethics are what operate in the rest of the days.

When the fire is welcomed into the lodge in Alishan next time, the Tsou people welcome not just a deity, nor a replicable "traditional performance." They welcome a question: in new life, who is willing to return, who remembers how to sing, and who is responsible for binding the posts? Mayasvi does not seal away the answer. It re-asks the question every year.

This also gives "preservation" a meaning different from static display. Preservation is not freezing the ritual in a certain year; it is preserving the right of the clan to decide the date, maintain the lodge, arrange roles, and limit viewing and interpret change. As long as these decisions return to the clan, Mayasvi is not a shadow of the past but a contemporary public life.

For those hearing this name for the first time, the best entry point may not be remembering a long list of ritual names, but remembering three interconnected objects: a pile of fire welcomed into the site, a _chiruong_ tree with three remaining branches, and an old post bound next to a new one. The fire points to shared time; the _chiruong_ points to the sacred path; the old post points to unbroken generational relationships. These three objects are closer to the actual weight of Mayasvi than "ancient" or "mysterious."

They also remind readers that the core of the ceremony is not just in the most lively singing and dancing moments. The truly enduring work happens in every decision—in preparing the fire, pruning the tree, preserving the post, and learning when to approach and when to retreat.

These works may not always be captured by cameras, but they are the conditions for the continuation of the ceremony.

They allow the fire of Mayasvi to illuminate not just one night, but how the clan entrusts its memory to the next generation.
