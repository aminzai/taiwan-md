In the spectrum of commoner culture in Taiwan, no art form has permeated as perfectly through the clamor of temple fairs, the black-and-white and color waves before television screens, and the visual marvels of contemporary digital effects as glove puppetry. When we look back at this journey, the names of the glove puppetry master Huang Hai-tai and his family from Hujia, Yunlin, are almost synonymous with the modern evolutionary history of Taiwanese glove puppetry [^1] [^2]. This is not merely a transmission of puppet artistry; it is a cultural struggle for breakthrough and rebirth amidst monumental changes in the era.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:**
> Many people think glove puppetry is just folk entertainment for children. However, in Taiwan during the 1970s, _The Great Scholar of Yunzhou_, produced by Huang Jun-xiong, achieved a legendary viewership rating of up to 92%—this was the collective memory of Taiwanese people gathered around black-and-white televisions, and it represented the loudest voice of traditional art bursting forth in the wave of modern media.

## From Temples to Indoor Stages: The Founding of Wuchou School and Years of Jianghu Life

The roots of Taiwanese glove puppetry can be traced back to hand puppetry imported from Fujian, China; however, through the amalgamation by generations of local artists, it developed a unique artistic life [^5]. Huang Hai-tai, born in Yunlin in the eleventh year of the Republic (pre-1912), grew up under the tutelage of his father Huang Ma's troupe and Northern music [^3]. In 1931, Huang Hai-tai established the famous "Wuchou School" in Hujia, officially commencing the glorious chapter of the Wuchou school.

Early glove puppetry was often performed as "continuous play scripts" at temple fairs or indoor theaters, with a single performance sometimes running for dozens of days without rest. Huang Hai-tai not only mastered Northern music, Southern music, _Luantan_, and Siping opera but also adapted traditional chapter novels (such as _Romance of the Three Kingdoms_, _Investiture of the Gods_, and _Wild Old Man's Talk_) into scripts with exquisite narration and dramatic plots through his deep literary knowledge [^4]. The characters he created, such as "Wen Su-chen," became immensely popular and laid a solid narrative foundation for later martial arts glove puppetry.

During the heyday of indoor theater, Huang Jun-qing, Huang Hai-tai's eldest son, further pioneered the Jin Guang glove puppetry tradition, pushing indoor glove puppetry to the peak of commercial entertainment with magnificent lighting, mechanical effects, martial arts special effects, and bold narration styles [^2].

## The Television Sensation That Shook Jianghu: From Shi Yanwen to Su Huan-zhen

As social structures changed and televisions gradually entered ordinary households, traditional indoor glove puppetry faced an unprecedented existential crisis. However, Huang Jun-xiong, the second son of Huang Hai-tai, boldly seized the opportunity of the new medium, launching _The Great Scholar of Yunzhou_ on TAI TV in 1970. The script was adapted by his father, Huang Hai-tai, and the narration and arrangement were personally handled by Huang Jun-xiong [^4] [^2].

The protagonist "Shi Yanwen" instantly became a household name across Taiwan, joining classic characters like Zang Jingren, Liu San, and Guai Laozi in streets and alleys [^2]. This production not only changed the daily routines of Taiwanese people but also created a viewership myth in Taiwanese television history. Although it was later banned due to political factors such as the "Mandarin language policy," the resilience of glove puppetry—which transitioned to the outdoor stage and videotape markets—proved that this art form had long permeated the blood and flesh of the Taiwanese people.

Entering the 1980s and 1990s, Huang Hai-tai's grandson, Huang Qianghua, and Huang Wenze (Pili International Multimedia), ignited another revolution. With _Pili Jin Guang_, they launched the "Pili" universe featuring Su Huan-zhen and Yi Ye Shu, combining traditional glove puppetry with modern cinematic camera work, computer effects, rock music, and high-difficulty puppet manipulation, successfully elevating glove puppetry from a niche local opera to an exquisite cultural industry [^6] [^7].

From the narration inherited from Huang Hai-tai to the television pioneering by Huang Jun-xiong, and finally to the digital innovation by the Pili brothers, each generation faced the most severe media challenges of their time, yet always managed to carve out new aesthetic boundaries in the cracks.

## Preservation of Technique and Contemporary Challenges: The Sustainable Path of Palm Universe

With the diversification of entertainment media, traditional glove puppetry faces a structural dilemma marked by the aging of traditional artists and a lack of successors. Although figures like Huang Hai-tai received numerous honors during his lifetime, such as the National Arts Award and Important Traditional Art Preserver, symbolizing the highest national recognition for this art form, how to fully preserve the essence of the "Palm Universe" and root it in the next generation remains an unresolved issue for contemporary cultural workers [^8].

In recent years, many young puppeteers and grassroots groups have begun attempting to bring glove puppetry into schools, combining it with contemporary theater, and even utilizing Virtual Reality (VR) and Augmented Reality (AR) technologies to help younger generations re-engage with this comprehensive aesthetic that integrates sculpture, embroidery, music, literature, and puppetry. From traditional wooden puppets to heroes on digital screens, the story of Taiwanese glove puppetry has never stopped.

## Conclusion: The Palm Epic of a Puppeteer's Life

In 2007, Huang Hai-tai, revered as the "Heavenly Master" of Taiwanese glove puppetry, passed away peacefully at the age of 107, concluding his legendary life [^2]. In his century of life, he witnessed Taiwan's dramatic transition from an agricultural society to an industrial powerhouse and finally to the digital information age.

Yet, the puppets in his hands always enacted loyalty, filial piety, grievances, affections, and the myriad aspects of human life on a miniature stage. Taiwanese glove puppetry is not just an intangible cultural heritage; it is a clear mirror reflecting the unyielding resilience of this island amidst the turbulent tides of history.
