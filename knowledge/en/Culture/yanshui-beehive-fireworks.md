> **30-second overview:** Yanshui Beehive Fireworks ties together faith in Guandi, cannon fort craftsmanship, and the Lantern Festival pilgrimage along the same street. The widely accepted origin of plague driving is only one layer; today's beehive fireworks must also face traditional cannon fort practices, tourism, crowd safety, and air pollution; so it is neither simply fireworks nor an ancient ritual to be preserved unchanged.

In 1885, the legendary crisis in Yanshui was not a fireworks show, but the fear of chaos and prolonged epidemics. On June 27, 2008, Yanshui Beehive Fireworks was officially registered as a folk custom by the Ministry of Culture's Cultural Heritage Administration. Today, during the Lantern Festival, the cannon fort is still pushed before the sedan, allowing thousands of rockets to fire simultaneously. [^1] [^2]

That scene is easily captured in a sea of orange-red: the crowd wearing safety helmets, sedan bearers pushing the sedan into the flames, and the beehive fireworks bursting from the wooden frame in all directions. But what makes Yanshui Beehive Fireworks truly special is precisely that it is not "everyone comes to watch the fireworks." To the people of Yanshui, the cannon fort is an offering to Guandi, the explosion is part of the ritual, and the street is where the local community reassembles.

> **The most counterintuitive aspect of Yanshui Beehive Fireworks: tourists see the firepower, while residents guard the rules.**

## The Cannon Fort Is Not a Fireworks Frame, but a Temporarily Completed Local Craft

The cannon fort is also called the cannon nest. It is first built with wooden strips or angle iron as the base frame, then fixed with cotton rope, iron wire mesh, or plastic mesh to secure the rockets, arranged with dual-directional, four-directional, six-directional, or even eight-directional firing angles, with a central fire heart, and finally decorated with external shapes. It looks like a building ready to burn, but is actually a synthesis of structure, angles, materials, and ignition sequence. [^2]

In the early days, the cannon forts were smaller in size, usually made by families. As occupational division of labor changed, customized or purchased ready-made cannon forts gradually increased. The cannon fort thus transformed from household handicraft into a technology requiring both professional construction teams and local apprenticeship programs to maintain. The Cultural Heritage Administration still lists cannon fort construction training and apprenticeship programs in recent years, because the disappearance of a cannon fort means not only the loss of a shaped fireworks display, but possibly the loss of a set of ways in which the locals know how to collaborate. [^1]

The word "fort" is very accurate. The cannon fort has a skeleton, a facade, a fire heart, and a time that must be waited for. It will not complete its meaning alone in an open space; only when the sedan arrives, the red silk cover with "Wenheng Sheng Emperor" is lifted, and the joss paper is ignited, does this temporary building enter the ritual.

📝 Curator's note: **The core craftsmanship of Yanshui Beehive Fireworks is not to make the cannons bigger, but to put a group of people in the same ignition moment.**

## Four Origin Stories, Officials Don't Pretend to Stamp on Legends

Regarding the origin of the beehive fireworks, the National Cultural Heritage Network has compiled four local sayings: anti-Qing restoration of Ming, inheriting the fire-fighting customs of Zhangquan homeland, using firecrackers to welcome Emperor Jiading, and the most commonly accepted plague-driving explanation. [^1]

The Ministry of the Interior's Taiwan Religious and Cultural Map also lists 1885, the 11th year of the Guangxu era, as a speculative origin time, describing how the Yanshui merchants invited Guandi to tour the area, using pilgrimage and firecrackers to pray for peace. The key word here is "speculative": it acknowledges the importance of local memory, and does not claim that a legend is the only answer proven by all historical materials. [^2]

The reason the plague-driving story is so powerful is not only because it sounds good, but also because it can connect local experiences. Immigrant settlements facing the uncertainty of the sea, soil, disease, and livelihood gradually turned the deities brought from their hometowns into local guardian gods. The pilgrimage of Guandi thus simultaneously carries faith, social order, and imagination of disaster and calamity. The Ministry of the Interior's data also places the development of Yanshui Port settlements, the faith in Guandi, and the origin of the beehive fireworks on the same local context. [^2]

This does not mean that sulfur smoke can really eliminate the plague. It means that a community once responded to uncontrollable dangers in a way they could understand and complete together. After the epidemic passed, the actions were preserved and reenacted every year during the Lantern Festival.

## From Linked Cannons to Beehive: Changes in Form Made the Ritual Like Today

The "releasing beehive" form familiar to people today was roughly formed after 1936 during the Japanese colonial period. In the early days of the pilgrimage, the main tools used were firecrackers and fireworks. After the war, beehive fireworks based on rockets developed, with cannon forts appearing in 1980 and further shaped in 1984. [^2]

This history shows that tradition is not a box from ancient times carried unchanged to today. The materials have changed, the cannon forts have grown larger, the shapes have become more complex, and the audience watching the beehive fireworks has expanded from local residents to tourists from other places. Folk customs are still called traditions, but they grow new shapes through repeated technological choices and social negotiations.

Around the year 2000, super-large cannon forts began to appear. In 2007, there were even themed cannon forts like the "ship cannon fort" and "teapot cannon fort." These objects pushed Yanshui's firecracker rituals to a more prominent public performance, making the boundaries between "folk custom" and "tourist activities" even thinner. [^2]

📝 Curator's note: **Tradition is not about refusing change. Tradition is that after change, the locals still recognize what they are doing.**

## When the Sedan Enters the Fire, It's Not the Performer Challenging the Audience

The climax of the beehive fireworks usually falls on the night of the Lantern Festival when Guandi makes his pilgrimage. When the sedan procession approaches a shop or residence, the cannon fort is quickly moved onto the road. The ritual first lifts the red silk cover with "Wenheng Sheng Emperor" written on it, and burns it together with joss paper, then ignites the fire heart. In an instant, the cannon fort fires rockets in all directions, with sound and smoke turning the street into a temporary fire field. [^2]

The sedan bearers wear safety helmets and heavy clothing, pushing and pulling the sedan into the gunfire, making the sedan jump back and forth in front of the cannon fort. This movement is called "plowing beehive fireworks" or "charging beehive fireworks." "Plowing" is both a farming tool for turning over the land and a connection to the local use of plow symbols to ward off evil. The smooth ignition of the beehive fireworks is understood as Guandi accepting the offerings from the believers. [^2]

Therefore, the viewing position is not the only issue. Who can touch the cannon nest, when the cannon fort is moved out, who can participate, and whether outsiders can release their own beehive fireworks all involve the integrity of the ritual. The Ministry of the Interior's data specifically records the local emphasis on these rules and taboos, as well as the residents' disapproval of tourists arbitrarily interfering with the ritual. [^2]

Writing the sedan bearers simply as "brave people" misses a more important part: they are not trying to prove to the audience that they are not afraid, but are playing a role between the sedan and the local community. That set of fully armed clothing is both protective gear and the appearance of ritual identity.

## When Local Faith Becomes a Large-Scale Event, Who Is Responsible for Keeping It Going

Yanshui Beehive Fireworks was promoted as a large-scale tourist event around the year 2000, thus gaining wider visibility. The reason for its cultural heritage registration emphasizes its locality, regular holding, and integration into residents' lives. These two descriptions do not necessarily conflict, but they push the event in different directions: tourism needs observable climaxes, while local rituals need respected order. [^1]

Public safety is therefore not an afterthought at the end of the article. The Tainan City Government's guidance points require Yanshui District Office to assist the temples in applying for permits 30 days before the event, in accordance with the safety management regulations for large-scale cluster activities. This means that today's beehive fireworks must face the deity, residents, tourists, fire department, traffic, and administrative managers at the same time. [^4]

The environmental cost cannot be obscured by the orange flames. A research summary from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University records that during specific beehive fireworks periods, the 5-hour average concentration of ground-level particulate matter is 443.9 μg/m³, and the particulate polycyclic aromatic hydrocarbons are 33.9 ng/m³. The total particulate exposure for participants over 3.5 hours is 2865.9 μg/m³. These are specific time periods and samples from that study, and should not be directly inferred to all years or all people's health outcomes, but they are enough to show that "stimulation" is also exposure. [^6]

Another study from National Cheng Kung University analyzes the composition of air pollutants released by the beehive fireworks, pointing out that potassium ions, magnesium ions, and nitrate can be used as tracking components, and observes that the concentration of suspended particles during the beehive fireworks period is about twice that of normal times. The Ministry of the Environment's previous monitoring reports also pointed out that the peak hourly instantaneous value of suspended particles during the beehive fireworks release is far higher than the daily average background. [^7] [^8]

📝 Curator's note: **Preserving folk customs is not about defending risks, but about giving people the ability to decide how to do it after knowing the cost.**

## What the People of Yanshui Want to Preserve May Not Be Just That Night

The Cultural Heritage Administration lists Yanshui Beehive Fireworks as a ritual, festival, and celebration category of folk customs, with the preserver being the Yanshui Temple Management Committee, and the cycle being annual. This registration method reminds us that what is being preserved is not a photo of fireworks, but a whole set of relationships: how the cannon fort is built, who inherits it, how the sedan tours, how the residents offer cannons, and how the locals maintain order under modern safety standards. [^1]

The book published by the Tainan City Cultural Heritage Management Division, "The Great Tainan Cultural Heritage Series 02—Yanshui Beehive Fireworks," written by Huang Wenbo, published in 2015, with a total of 183 pages. Its existence itself shows that Yanshui Beehive Fireworks not only needs to be filmed, but also needs to be studied, recorded, and reinterpreted by the locals. [^3]

When tourists stand behind the safety helmet and camera, what is truly worth taking away is not the line "I was hit by cannons," but another question: why is a place willing to rebuild a fort every year that will disappear in a few minutes? The answer is not only in the gunpowder, but also in the residents handing over memory, faith, and responsibility to the people of the next year.

The story of the 1885 epidemic may not be restored to a single historical truth. But the 2008 cultural heritage registration, the continuous cannon fort apprenticeship, the safety specifications of the activities, and the environmental monitoring have given this local memory its weight today. Yanshui Beehive Fireworks is not preserving the past as a static object, but letting a cannon fort briefly burn on the street every year, and then asking the living to take over the rules.
