> **30-Second Overview:** The AAMA Taipei Cradle Program represents a rare phenomenon in Taiwan: a startup mentorship initiative that neither invests capital, takes equity, nor charges tuition. Launched in 2012 by Hung-Tze Jang, publisher of _Digital Times_, and retired accounting consultant Charles Yen, it pairs seasoned executives with one growing-stage entrepreneur each, providing two years of one-on-one guidance. Accepting only around twenty participants annually, it has reached its fifteenth cohort over thirteen years, yet has nurtured names like Appier, Social Enterprise Lab, and Portaly. Its most counterintuitive feature lies in a deliberate choice: a group of people who could have retired to enjoy life opted for the slowest, least scalable path to hand down the hard-to-articulate craft of "how to scale a company" to the next generation, asking for nothing in return.

The public's impression of the AAMA Taipei Cradle Program in Taiwan likely stops at the annual group photo published by _Digital Times_ every June—a crowd of people squeezed together, making a hand gesture representing the current cohort number. This "reveal moment" has continued uninterrupted for thirteen years, but it has never been a widely recognized brand. Unlike AppWorks or Meet Taipei, it rarely makes headlines, living instead in the memories of those within the startup ecosystem: a place defined by one-on-one, two-year commitments without equity stakes[^1].

To understand why it stands out, we must first look at how it began.

## A Group of People Who Could Have Retired, Choosing the Slowest Path

When the program officially launched in May 2012, it was not yet a formally registered organization. It was an experiment orchestrated by just two people, Hung-Tze Jang and Charles Yen, leveraging their respective networks[^2]. They recruited Zhu Ping, Fang Zheng, Fan Youwei, Chang Jung-kuei, Chen I-chiang, Pan Chien-cheng, Chien Li-feng, and Su Li-mei, along with the two founders themselves, to form a cohort of ten mentors paired with twenty entrepreneurs recruited for the first cohort[^3].

The mentorship model operates very differently from the common assumption of "inviting successful figures to give a lecture." Each participant is paired with a "primary mentor" for one-on-one in-depth discussions, while also interacting with other non-primary mentors, forming a one-to-many, many-to-many network[^4]. The official terminology is "co-learning," not "guidance": mentors and participants set learning goals together, review progress together, and meet continuously over two years. The relationship forged over those two years is far more valuable than a single lecture.

The selection process is also far from a blind resume lottery. It follows four steps—application, written preliminary review, final interview, and announcement. The preliminary review is vetted by external venture capitalists, professional advisors, and an executive committee, narrowing applicants to around forty. For the final stage, the twelve mentors of that cohort conduct personal interviews to decide the final twenty to twenty-four accepted participants[^5]. In other words, mentors are involved from the moment of acceptance: because they personally selected you, they commit to accompanying you for the next two years. This imbues the "mentor" role with a sense of responsibility from start to finish.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> When people talk about a "startup accelerator," they typically picture Silicon Valley's Y Combinator model: rapid iteration, taking the stage on Demo Day to raise funds from investors, and running in cohorts lasting just a few months. AAMA inverts every single aspect: the cycle extends to two years, the cohort shrinks to around twenty, and it fundamentally lacks a Demo Day. The official vocabulary contains no "graduation ceremony," only an "acceptance announcement"[^6]. Because it neither invests nor takes equity, participants have no structural need to pitch to investors for funding. The underlying logic of its entire ceremonial design is entirely different from that of an accelerator.

This slowness is deliberate. A relationship capable of truly passing on judgment simply cannot be rushed.

## Beijing Did It Once; Taipei Is the Second Time

To trace the origins of this model, we must first dispel a common misconception born from reading the name literally. The name AAMA comes from an Asian technology association founded in Silicon Valley in 1979 (Asia America MultiTechnology Association), which maintains global chapters in Silicon Valley, Beijing, Shanghai, Seoul, and Hong Kong[^7]. However, the Silicon Valley parent organization never had a "Cradle Program." Its core activities are traditional association affairs: an annual conference and monthly regular meetings[^8].

The two-year, one-on-one mentorship model was not an original design from 1979. It was "invented" when Charles Yen was stationed in Beijing in 2004, transplanting Deloitte's internal corporate culture of "pairing new employees with a senior mentor" into the field of startup coaching[^9].

Charles Yen's own story is compelling. He spent thirty years in an accounting firm, serving as President of Deloitte Taiwan from 1995 to 2003. In 2004, he was stationed in Beijing, where he expanded the team from 400 to 2,000 people[^10]. This is a man who has led large teams and scrutinized countless corporate financials. After retirement, he could have easily played golf and enjoyed time with his grandchildren, but he chose the most time-intensive path of startup coaching. Moreover, he had already run a Cradle Program in Beijing, earning the title of China's "Entrepreneurship Philanthropist of the Year" in 2009 for his efforts[^11]. Taipei represents the second time he brought a model he experimented with elsewhere back to his hometown.

When the exact same system was brought back to Taiwan, the resulting culture proved quite different. According to reports, Yen compared the two regional versions: the Beijing iteration was highly competitive and goal-oriented, carrying a scent of hunting for opportunities; the Taiwan version, however, incorporated more elements of companionship and sharing[^12]. This is hardly surprising: the temperature of an institution is ultimately determined by the local people and culture. The same concept of "seniors guiding juniors," when transplanted to different soil, shifts from a competitive race into a form of support. This ensures that Taipei's Cradle Program is not merely a copy-paste of Beijing's.

As for Hung-Tze Jang's role, it is often misread as "co-inventor," but that is not the case. Jang himself clarified during the tenth-anniversary celebrations: "When Principal Yen (Charles Yen) brought up this program, I thought it was a very good idea."[^13] It was Yen who approached him with a concept already tested in Beijing. Jang's contribution was validation and leveraging the exposure of _Digital Times_ as a media platform. One provided the methodology, the other provided the stage. Their roles complemented each other, leaving no record of ideological conflict[^14].

## No Equity, So What's the Point?

This is the question everyone asks upon first hearing of AAMA. If it neither invests, takes equity, nor charges tuition for profit, how does the program operate? What do the mentors hope to gain from investing their time?

Let's start with funding. For its first decade, it operated as an informal community sustained by personal connections. It was not until a board resolution at the end of 2019 that it formally established the Taiwan Foundation for Entrepreneurs' Co-Creation Platform in April 2020, with a cash endowment of 10 million New Taiwan dollars[^15]. The list of donors is particularly interesting: Jusi Culture (Hung-Tze Jang's media group and parent company of _Digital Times_), the Advantech Cultural Foundation, Wistron, 999 Realty founder Chou Chun-chi, Charles Yen himself, and twenty alumni from the program[^16].

Notice the final item on that list. Among those who funded the program to become a formal legal entity, twenty were individuals who had been guided by it. This connects directly to the answer from the mentor side. Yen stated this plainly: "AAMA is not a social platform, but a learning platform... these mentors are willing to share life experience and management wisdom."[^17] An even more complete loop is that alumni themselves return. The "AAMA NEXT Alumni Alliance," established in 2019, features Yeh Kuan-yi from the first cohort as its inaugural president and Yu Chih-wei from the second cohort as vice president, with alumni themselves forming the governance structure[^18]. Starting from the fourteenth cohort, alumni returning as mentors shifted from a special case to a standard practice[^19].

Thus, the entire thread grows into a closed loop: a mentor guides you, you graduate and become an alumnus, alumni form an alliance, some alumni return to serve as mentors, and some alumni donate money to keep the program alive. Both capital and human resources circulate from within the community itself; a community sustains itself. Regarding corporate partnerships, the only one with a verifiable specific amount to date is the "AIoT Corporate Co-Creation Program," co-hosted by Advantech and Chunghwa Telecom, which provides cloud resources worth millions of dollars[^20].

So many people return to give back, partly because the relationships here are unvarnished. The alumni interview series on the AAMA official website is candid enough to read less like a press release. For example, Chien Shih-chieh, founder of UNT Nail Polish, stated: "Cross-border ventures inevitably burn cash initially!... Like our nail polish, which has high market penetration, but half of it is just luck."[^21] An entrepreneur who openly admits "half of it is luck," paired with a mentor willing to sit down and accompany them for two years, indicates that neither side is performing a success narrative. This space that allows for admitting vulnerability and acknowledging luck is itself one of the reasons the community holds people together.

> **✦** "Whether you are an entrepreneur or not, as long as you come to AAMA, you will feel love, patience, and empathy."

One necessary caveat should be clarified: philanthropy does not equal entirely free. The official website clearly states that while the program itself "does not charge participants fees for profit," certain learning activities and collaborations with external professional advisors require self-funding[^22]. It is a philanthropic program that honestly marks its boundaries, not a paradise where everything is free.

## The Man Who Later Helped Taiwan's First AI Company List in Tokyo

Let's return to the first cohort list from 2012. Among those twenty individuals was one named Yu Chih-han. The Appier he later co-founded became Taiwan's first AI company to list overseas (on the Tokyo Stock Exchange)[^23]. His current identity is a mentor for the fourteenth cohort: transitioning from the person who was guided to the person who guides others[^24].

This contrast of "first-cohort alumni returning as mentors" is real, and quite elegant. But here we must carefully draw a line: **this is a symbolic connection, and nothing more**. It does not constitute a causal chain of "AAMA created Appier." In public interviews, Yu Chih-han himself thanked two key figures: Andrew Ng from the Stanford AI Lab and Masayoshi Son from SoftBank, neither of whom were mentors paired with him by AAMA. When discussing how he approached Ng's lab, he said: "I printed a thick stack of my background materials and research achievements, went to every professor's office related to AI research, knocked on the door, and used my broken English..."[^25] This narrative has nothing to do with AAMA.

Therefore, what can be said is: Yu Chih-han is the most notable alumnus from the first cohort, and his return as a mentor demonstrates the program's stickiness. What cannot be said, and lacks evidentiary support, is: "The advice he received at AAMA helped him build Appier." Treating symbolism as causality is deification; honestly acknowledging that we cannot trace that causal chain is journalistic integrity.

Other alumni also sustain a cross-industry roster: Lin Yi-han from Social Enterprise Lab (third cohort), Chang Wei-hsuan from Womanism (second cohort), TapPay Qiaorui Technology (sixth cohort, acquired by a PChome Group subsidiary in 2022), Kneron (ninth cohort), Chai-zi-tang (Tea Seed Hall) which revitalized traditional industries, and Lin Chih-wei from the thirteenth cohort—who founded Portaly, which later became Taiwan's largest creator commerce platform[^26].

## The Two Mentors Who Returned Three Times

If mentorship were merely an honorary title, the program would lack a soul. But its stickiness can be proven by two individuals.

Shen Fang-cheng, CEO of the Regent Hotel Group, has returned as a mentor three times (first, eighth, and thirteenth cohorts), spanning thirteen years[^27]. Huang Li-yan, founder of WAVE Brand Marketing, has also returned three times (fifth, eighth, and twelfth cohorts)[^28]. The fact that an individual is willing to repeatedly return to the same program over more than a decade, repeatedly spending two years accompanying a complete stranger, speaks for itself: what they gain in return is something that cannot be exchanged elsewhere. A resume title is far from enough to explain this stickiness.

Charles Yen's commentary on the traits of entrepreneurs they seek actually reflects, in reverse, why mentors are willing to give. He stated that an entrepreneur who "knows their own shortcomings and learns with discipline... possesses a very uncommon and highly valuable trait"[^29]. A person who has led a team of two thousand and witnessed countless rises and falls does not want to pass on "how I succeeded back then," because that is unreplicable. What he wants to pass on is judgment—the craft that is too difficult to write into textbooks and can only be discussed one-on-one, repeatedly.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The phrase Charles Yen repeatedly emphasizes is "success cannot be replicated, but wisdom can be passed on"[^30]. Deconstructed, this phrase contains the foundational premise of the entire program. The Silicon Valley model believes in scalable methodologies, standardizing processes to bring hundreds of companies through a single cohort. What AAMA believes in is precisely the opposite: the truly valuable portion of judgment is exactly what cannot be standardized or scaled. Therefore, it can only be transmitted in the oldest way possible: master-apprentice, face-to-face, and slowly. Its cohort size of around twenty may seem restricted, but it is deliberate: what it believes in was never meant to be mass-produced.

## 80% Attendance Rate, and "Completing the Program Without Graduating"

Everything up to this point feels warm. But an honest collective portrait must accommodate its own tensions.

AAMA loves to talk about "completing the program without graduating." Yen's exact words were: "We often say AAMA is completing the program without graduating."[^31] The meaning is that you will never "graduate" and leave; you will stay in this community for life. This is its most moving narrative: a relationship that never ends.

Yet, on the application page of the exact same program, it explicitly requires in black and white that participants maintain an "over 80% participation rate in learning activities" over the two-year period[^32]. On one side lies the warm promise of a "lifelong community"; on the other, the concrete governance threshold of an "80% attendance rate." These two are not so contradictory that they require conflict, but they undeniably coexist. A community bound by personal connections and emotion, once it formalizes, becomes accountable to donors, and seeks sustainability, must inevitably grow quantitative rules. This is the growing pain every organization faces when transitioning from "a group of friends" to "an institution."

There is one more honest blank that must be addressed: this article cannot find any public record of an alumnus's company going bankrupt or exiting. Multiple searches from different angles yield only stories of growth and success. But this does not mean AAMA alumni have "zero failures." Media willingness to report on failure cases is inherently low, and AAMA has never publicly released exit-rate statistics. The only "exit" case found to date (TapPay's acquisition) was a successful acquisition rather than liquidation[^33]. Therefore, a more accurate statement is: the sample we see is biased toward survivors. This report card truly presents a landscape selectively illuminated by the media, not a guarantee of a 100% success rate.

## Two Parallel Worlds

Within Taiwan's startup support ecosystem, AAMA is often compared to AppWorks. The differences between the two can be summarized in a clear comparison: AppWorks runs cohorts every six months, with thirty to forty teams per cohort, manages four venture capital funds, and invests from seed to Series C rounds, operating on an "accelerate plus invest" model. AAMA operates on a two-year, one-on-one basis, accepting around twenty people annually, neither investing nor taking equity, and targeting "growth-stage" rather than earliest-stage entrepreneurs[^34].

Interestingly, the founders of these two distinctly positioned institutions, often mentioned together, actually participate in each other's ecosystems. Lin Chih-chen, founder of AppWorks, is listed on the AAMA official website's mentor roster[^35]. Rather than a confrontation, this is more like the natural overlap of familiar faces within a small ecosystem. Taiwan's official government census (FINDIT Ecosystem Survey) also categorizes AAMA as a "comprehensive startup base," explicitly excluding it from the "accelerator" classification system. It has never been the same thing from the start[^36].

What truly deserves a pause is another comparison. AAMA has a sister program with highly overlapping target audiences that also does not focus on investment and equity (SLP, Taipei Startup Leadership Program). Logically, these two programs should often be discussed together. But after scouring both official websites and media reports, one discovers a fact: **they are two parallel worlds, never publicly mentioning each other**.

This "zero intersection" itself explains the landscape of Taiwan's startup support system better than any competition narrative: it resembles more a cluster of small galaxies operating independently without interacting, rather than an integrated market. Each program operates on its own trajectory, catching a small group of entrepreneurs in the way it believes.

## The Journey Is Only Halfway Done

In 2022, AAMA celebrated its tenth anniversary. This is a moment suited for self-praise: it had accumulated 106 mentors, accompanied 299 entrepreneurial ventures, and reached 10,000 hours of interaction[^37]. By 2024, the official website reported that the combined annual revenue of alumnus companies exceeded 57 billion New Taiwan dollars. For the 2026 fifteenth cohort, 158 applicants set a five-year high, the female applicant ratio reached 34%, and the number of applicant companies with annual revenue exceeding 100 million doubled from nine to nineteen[^38]. These numbers are all impressive.

Yet at the tenth-anniversary forum, Charles Yen chose not to celebrate. Citing the argument from the book _Startup Communities_, he made a statement: "Building a startup community requires at least 20 years of accumulation to exert true influence, so we have only gone halfway."[^39]

This statement outlines a "work-in-progress" framework drawn by the founder himself, stemming from internal reflection rather than external criticism. For someone who has accumulated a decade of achievements, standing at a celebration and choosing to say "we have only gone halfway" reveals a rare clarity. It acknowledges one thing: the transmission of judgment is inherently a slow craft whose results cannot be clearly seen in ten years, but must be measured over twenty or thirty years.

Looking back, the most counterintuitive aspect of the AAMA Taipei Cradle Program has never been how many unicorns it has produced. It is that thirteen years ago, a group of people who could have retired to enjoy life, in an era when everyone was pursuing "faster, bigger, more," chose the slowest, least scalable, and least cost-effective path: two years, one-on-one, no equity, to do something whose results would only be visible after twenty years. Among those first twenty individuals, some have now returned to sit in the mentor's chair, waiting to accompany the next young person who is still stumbling and temporarily cannot see the finish line. Craft is passed down exactly like this: one pair of hands to another.
