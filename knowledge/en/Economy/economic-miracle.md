# Economic Miracle: From Sweet Potato Flour to TSMC, the Turnaround Story Built by Twenty Million People

![Premier Yen Chia-kan presiding over the inauguration ceremony of the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone, officials and guests posing at the ceremony site, backdrop shows unveiling banner and early factory buildings](/article-images/economy/kaohsiung-epz-inauguration-1966.webp)
_December 3, 1966: Premier Yen Chia-kan presides over the inauguration of the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone, the world's first export processing zone. Photo: Executive Yuan Precious Historical Materials Exhibition. [Source](https://history.ey.gov.tw/en/Items/premier-yen-chia-kan-hosts-the-inauguration-of-the-kaohsiung-export-processing-zone/)._

> **30-Second Overview:** In the early 1950s, Taiwan's average per capita income was only about $150[^1], placing it among the poorest countries in the world. A Taiwanese worker's monthly salary couldn't buy a pair of American-made sneakers. The turnaround's starting point wasn't factories — it was land reform, where the government exchanged landlords' fields for corporate stock, letting tenant farmers own their land for the first time[^2]. Then came women working 12-hour days in export processing zones, SME owners staking family savings on small factories, and merchants traveling the world with a single suitcase. Forty years later, per capita income surpassed $10,000, and Taiwan stood alongside South Korea, Hong Kong, and Singapore as the "Four Asian Tigers." Taiwan's economic miracle wasn't the government's achievement — it's a blood-and-tears history written by twenty million people together.

On December 3, 1966, the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone officially opened[^3]. This was the world's first "export processing zone," an economic experiment that existed only in the imagination of Taiwanese officials.

That morning, only one factory in the zone began production: General Instrument Corporation. Thirty female workers sat on the production line assembling radio components. Their monthly salary was NT$600, about US$20 — barely enough to buy a 10kg bag of rice.

No one knew if this experiment would succeed. American advisors believed "Taiwanese worker quality is too poor," Japanese businessmen thought "Taiwan's infrastructure is too terrible," and even government officials lacked confidence, merely adopting a "desperate measures for desperate times" attitude.

Twenty years later, the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone became a symbol of Taiwan's economic takeoff. One afternoon in 1985, on that same production line, female workers were assembling the latest electronic calculators. Their monthly salary had risen to NT$15,000 — enough to buy a motorcycle.

This is a microcosm of Taiwan's economic miracle: **not an overnight riches fairy tale, but a turnaround story bought with one generation's blood and sweat.** And the true starting point of this story came more than a decade before the export processing zone.

## From Poverty to Modest Prosperity: The Real Stories Behind the Numbers

### 1950s: The Starting Point of Destitution

How poor was Taiwan in the early 1950s? A few numbers tell the story:

- **Average per capita income: ~US$150** (not far from the poorest countries of the same period)[^1]
- **Infant mortality rate: 44.7‰** (45 out of every 1,000 newborns died before age 1)
- **Life expectancy: 53 years** (30 years shorter than today)
- **Literacy rate: 42%** (less than half the population could read and write)

What were Taiwanese people eating then? **Sweet potato flour with watery congee.** Sweet potato flour is sweet potato shredded into strips and sun-dried for preservation, because rice was too expensive to afford. A typical family's "good day" was sweet potato flour mixed with white rice, roughly a 7:3 ratio.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> There was a saying in that era: "Raising chickens and ducks is not as good as raising a daughter who can read." Meaning it's better to raise a literate daughter than livestock. Why? Because a literate daughter could marry a civil servant, giving the family stable income. This saying reflects Taiwan's poverty level at the time: even raising chickens and ducks was a luxury.

### Land Reform: Swapping Deeds for a Chance to Turn Around

![Premier Chen Cheng receiving a gratitude banner from the public at a ceremony commemorating the implementation of the Land-to-the-Tiller policy, personnel wearing period civil servant uniforms](/article-images/economy/land-reform-chen-cheng-1953.webp)
_Premier Chen Cheng accepts a gratitude banner from the public, thanking the Land-to-the-Tiller policy for enabling tenant farmers to own their land. Photo: Executive Yuan Precious Historical Materials Exhibition. [Source](https://history.ey.gov.tw/en/Items/premier-chen-cheng-accepts-a-banner-of-appreciation-for-the-land-to-the-tiller-program/)._

Before the export processing zones, before the Ten Major Construction Projects, before TSMC, the true first step of Taiwan's economic miracle was a quietly executed land reform.

From 1949 to 1953, the government transferred land from landlords to the farmers who actually tilled it in three phases[^2]:

1. **37.5% Rent Reduction** (1949): Rent forcibly lowered from the original 50-60% to no more than 37.5%
2. **Public Land Release** (1951): Public farmland sold to current tenant farmers on installment plans, letting them buy the land they'd farmed all their lives
3. **Land-to-the-Tiller** (1953): Compulsory purchase of landlords' land exceeding retention limits, resold to landless tenant farmers

These three moves had immediate effect: farmers owned their land for the first time, farming enthusiasm rose, and agricultural productivity increased; landlords received compensation in the form of stock in four state-owned enterprises (Taiwan Cement, Taiwan Paper, Taiwan Machinery, Taiwan Forestry), forcibly transforming from "rent-collecting landlords" into "industrial-investing shareholders" — this capital squeezed from the countryside became the earliest seed of Taiwan's industrialization.

> **💡 Did You Know**
> In 1968, the ROC government jointly established the "Land Reform Training Institute" with the American Lincoln Institute of Land Policy to teach Taiwan's land reform experience to other developing countries; this institution was renamed the "International Land Policy Research and Training Center" in 2000 and remains operational today[^4].

Of course, this land reform had its costs. The expropriation rate for jointly owned leased land reached over 80%, while individually held land was only expropriated at about 30% — in other words, the most powerless small joint landowners were cut the deepest; there were also cases like the Lukang Koo family, who cooperated with land reform by taking stock and later developed into a major conglomerate. The turnaround story, from the very beginning, wasn't one where everyone benefited equally.

### 1960s: The Export Processing Zone Miracle

In 1966, Taiwan made a decision that changed its destiny: establishing the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone, dedicated to contract manufacturing for foreign orders[^3].

The background to this decision was starkly realistic: **Taiwan had nothing but cheap labor.** No oil, no iron ore, no technology, no brands — the only advantage was workers willing to work long hours for very low wages.

The first companies to move in included:

- **General Instrument Corporation** (Japanese, assembling radios)
- **Philips Electronics** (Dutch, producing TV components)
- **General Instrument** (American, manufacturing semiconductors)

These factories shared a common trait: **employing large numbers of young female workers.** Why? Because electronics assembly required delicate manual work, and contemporary social norms held that "women have nimble fingers, patience, and don't cause trouble."

By 1970, women accounted for 85% of total employees in the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone. Most were unmarried women aged 18-25 from rural areas, living in factory dormitories, working 10-12 hours a day.

**These female workers rewrote Taiwan's economic history.**

### 1970s: The Ten Major Construction Projects Gamble

In 1973, the oil crisis erupted, dealing a heavy blow to Taiwan's economy. Exports shrank, inflation soared, unemployment rose. Many felt "Taiwan is finished."

But Chiang Ching-kuo made a bold decision: launch the "Ten Major Construction Projects," using government power to invest in infrastructure, preparing conditions for the next wave of economic growth.

![Official portrait of Chiang Ching-kuo in a suit, solemn expression, standard official photo from the government archives](/article-images/economy/chiang-ching-kuo-portrait.webp)
_Premier Chiang Ching-kuo, who spearheaded the Ten Major Construction Projects gamble when the 1973 oil crisis struck Taiwan's economy. Photo: Executive Yuan Precious Historical Materials Exhibition. [Source](https://history.ey.gov.tw/en/Items/%E8%94%A3%E7%B6%93%E5%9C%8B/)._

**Total investment in the Ten Major Construction Projects: ~NT$200 billion** (several times the foreign exchange reserves at the time)

Including the Sun Yat-sen Freeway, Taoyuan International Airport, Taichung Port, Nuclear Plants 1 and 2, China Steel, China Shipbuilding, petrochemical complexes — transportation and heavy industry projects. The complete planning context and controversies are detailed in "[Ten Major Construction Projects: The NT$200 Billion Gamble on US$1 Billion in Reserves](/en/history/ten-major-construction-projects)."

These projects were criticized at the time as "grandiose vanity projects" and "leaving debt to future generations." But hindsight proved that without this infrastructure, the 1980s economic takeoff would have been impossible.

> **⚠️ Controversial Perspective**
> Though the Ten Major Construction Projects succeeded, the cost was high. To raise funds, the government issued massive public debt, creating a heavy debt burden. Heavy industry development also caused environmental pollution, aftereffects still being addressed today. The economic miracle wasn't without cost.

### 1980s: The Golden Age of SMEs

The 1980s were Taiwan's most economically glorious decade. But the true protagonists weren't government mega-projects — they were the SMEs spread across Taiwan.

Look at the numbers:

- **1980**: Taiwan had 550,000 SMEs
- **1990**: Taiwan had 900,000 SMEs
- **SME employment**: Over 70% of Taiwan's total workforce

What were these SMEs doing? **OEM contract manufacturing.**

OEM stands for Original Equipment Manufacturer. Simply put: foreign companies provide designs and specifications, Taiwanese factories handle production, products sold under foreign brands.

**Nike sneakers, Gap clothing, Dell computers** — in the 1980s, most of these American brands' products were "Made in Taiwan."

### The Legend of Merchants Traveling the World with a Single Suitcase

Taiwanese SMEs' success relied on the owners' "desperate drive."

The classic image is "one suitcase traveling the world": owners carrying samples, flying to trade shows worldwide, closing deals on the spot. **Language barriers didn't matter — use a calculator to punch numbers; cultural differences didn't matter — low prices win.**

This business model had several characteristics:

1. **Family enterprises**: Owner, spouse, siblings all managing together
2. **Flexible production**: Could accept small-batch, diverse orders
3. **Cost control**: Minimizing all unnecessary expenses
4. **Speed above all**: Rapid response to customer needs

**Pou Chen Group**'s story is typical. Founder Tsai Chi-jui started with NT$200,000 in 1969, specializing in contract manufacturing athletic shoes. He personally traveled the US, visiting shoe stores one by one, eventually securing Nike's contract manufacturing order.

Forty years later, Pou Chen became the world's largest athletic shoe contract manufacturer, annual output 300 million pairs — nearly 1 in 3 Nike shoes made by Pou Chen.

## The Human Cost Behind the Miracle

Taiwan's economic miracle created astonishing growth figures, but also exacted enormous human costs.

### Female Workers' Lives in the Export Processing Zone

In the 1970s, the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone was called "Oriental Sweatshops" by foreign journalists.

**Working Conditions**:

- 10-12 hours daily, 1 day off per week
- Monthly salary NT$2,000-3,000 (~US$60-90)
- 8 people per dormitory room, 11 PM curfew
- Dating and marriage prohibited

**Health Issues**:

- Long hours hunched over work caused severe cervical spine and eye problems
- Exposure to chemical solvents led to common skin allergies and respiratory diseases
- High mental stress, elevated depression and suicide rates

But these women had no choice. For a rural girl, entering the export processing zone was already the best path. Back home, they could only farm or do piecework at home — even less income, even bleaker prospects.

> **💡 Did You Know**
> In the 1970s there was a hit song "Hometown at Dusk" with lyrics "People living for livelihood, temporary sojourners in a foreign land," describing these young people leaving home for factory work. The song swept the nation because it voiced an entire generation's heart.

### Leaving the Factory: Education, Urbanization, and the Flip in Women's Status

The female workers' blood and sweat is the heaviest face of this miracle, but the same generation also unwittingly drove a flip in Taiwan's social structure.

In 1968, the government extended compulsory education from six to nine years[^5], and universities expanded massively. For a factory girl, this meant her next generation wouldn't have to enter factories after elementary school — vocational education gradually improved, and studying abroad became a common choice for middle-class families. The traditional landlord class declined with land reform, while industrialists and professional technicians rose in social status, forming a new middle class in this process.

Rural populations simultaneously flooded into metropolitan areas like Taipei and Kaohsiung, new towns sprang up around industrial zones, and nuclear families gradually replaced extended families as the mainstream. Meanwhile, stable employment from export processing zones, electronics, and textiles gave women their own income on a large scale for the first time — economic independence brought not just wages but accumulated workplace skills, and the traditional "women only belong at home" role观念 slowly loosened, with women's political participation markedly increasing after the 1980s.

The turnaround story often hides in the gap of "factories were sweatshops, but the next generation didn't have to enter factories."

### The Cost of Environmental Pollution

Another cost of economic development was environmental pollution.

**1970s-1980s**, for rapid industrialization, Taiwan kept environmental standards loose. Results:

- **River pollution**: Kaohsiung's Love River turned black, Taichung's Green River turned red (from dye wastewater)
- **Air pollution**: Kaohsiung refineries and petrochemical plants emitted massive exhaust, local residents had higher lung cancer rates
- **Soil pollution**: Organic solvents from electronics factories seeped into groundwater, causing long-term contamination

**Consequences still borne today.** Love River took 30 years to run clear again; some industrial zone soil pollution remains uncleaned to this day.

### Sacrifice of Labor Rights

During the economic miracle era, labor rights barely existed.

**Under martial law**, workers couldn't form independent unions, couldn't strike, couldn't bargain collectively. Management could arbitrarily impose overtime, transfer work, fire employees.

The most extreme example was the **1973 Asia Chemical Factory explosion**. To rush production, the factory made workers labor continuously for 72 hours in high-temperature, high-pressure conditions, finally triggering an explosion — 5 dead, 20+ injured. But this incident received zero media coverage, because martial law prohibited reporting "negative news affecting the investment environment."

## From Imitation to Innovation: The Rise of the Tech Industry

By the late 1980s, Taiwan's economy faced transformation pressure. Wages rose, the New Taiwan Dollar appreciated, and the traditional contract manufacturing model's competitive advantage vanished. Taiwan had to find a new development path.

### Hsinchu Science Park: The Government's Vision

In 1980, the Hsinchu Science-based Industrial Park was established. This was the government's attempt to emulate America's Silicon Valley, hoping to develop high-tech industries[^6].

**Initial tenants numbered only 20**, mostly foreign R&D centers or small Taiwanese firms. Many considered it a "government money pit" due to huge investment and slow returns.

But officials Li Kuo-ting and Sun Yun-suan saw differently. They glimpsed the future: **the information age was coming, and Taiwan had to position early.**

The key breakthrough came from the **"Return of Overseas Scholars" program**. The government offered generous terms to attract Taiwanese engineers working in the US to return and start businesses.

### TSMC's Birth: A Decision That Changed the World

In 1987, ITRI President Morris Chang made a history-altering decision: establish TSMC, dedicated to contract manufacturing chips for other companies[^7].

At the time, no "pure-play foundry" existed worldwide. Intel designed and manufactured its own; IBM designed and manufactured its own. Chang's idea was considered crazy: "Who would need a company that only manufactures, doesn't design?"

But Chang saw the trend: more and more companies would want to focus solely on chip design, not spend billions building fabs. **TSMC's role would be to become these companies' "chip factory."**

37 years later, TSMC became the world's most valuable semiconductor company, market cap exceeding US$800 billion. It proved Taiwan could move from "contract manufacturing" to "technology leadership."

![Exterior of TSMC's Hsinchu campus, gray-white building clusters with elevated piping typical of fabs, neatly landscaped roadsides](/article-images/economy/tsmc-hsinchu-factory-2009.webp)
_TSMC's Hsinchu campus, from where the pure-play foundry model radiated to the global chip supply chain. Photo: Arusanov, 2009-07-20, Public Domain. [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tsmc_factory_hsinchu.JPG)._

### Tech Industry Clustering Effect

TSMC's success drove the entire supply chain's development:

- **IC Design**: MediaTek, VIA, Realtek designing chips
- **Packaging & Testing**: ASE, SPIL handling backend processing
- **Equipment & Materials**: Hermes-Epitek, Scientech providing manufacturing equipment

**Hsinchu Science Park became the world's most complete semiconductor industry cluster.** From chip design to manufacturing to packaging, all links within a 50km radius, forming an irreplicable industrial ecosystem.

## The Truth Behind the Miracle Numbers

Reviewing Taiwan's economic miracle figures, they are indeed astonishing:

### Economic Growth Rate

- **Early 1950s to early 1990s: Average annual growth rate long maintained at 8-10%**[^8]
- **Global average for same period: 3.8%**

### Per Capita Income Growth

- **Early 1950s: ~US$150**[^1]
- **Early 1990s: Over US$10,000**[^8]
- **Sixty-fold growth in forty years**

### Export Expansion

- **1952 exports: US$116 million**
- **1990 exports: US$67.4 billion**
- **Growth multiple: 580x**

### Industrial Structure Transformation

- **1952 agriculture share of GDP: 32%**
- **1990 agriculture share of GDP: 4%**
- **Industry grew from 18% to 46%, services from 50% to 50%**

The story behind these numbers: **20 million Taiwanese used 40 years to successfully transform from an agricultural society into an industrial society.**

### Four Asian Tigers: Taiwan's Unique Path

Taiwan wasn't the only place creating miracles in this period. South Korea, Hong Kong, and Singapore followed the same export-oriented industrialization path, plus government-led industrial policy, heavy education investment, and two shared international opportunities: US market opening during the Cold War and Japanese technology spillover[^9].

But Taiwan's path had several distinct differences:

- **SMEs vs. Chaebols**: South Korea concentrated resources on chaebols like Samsung and Hyundai; Taiwan had 550,000 SMEs each "traveling the world with a suitcase" — both achieved high growth, but wealth distribution shapes were completely different
- **Thoroughness of land reform**: Compared to the other three tigers, Taiwan's land reform was most thorough, rural capital and labor smoothly transferred to industry — the starting point of that "turnaround" mentioned earlier
- **Political path difference**: Taiwan's transition from authoritarianism to democracy differed sharply from Singapore and Hong Kong — the middle class created by the economic miracle later became one of the drivers of 1990s democratization

The same formula of "export-oriented, government-led, education-first" produced four different social outcomes in four places. This is why Taiwan's story deserves separate remembrance: not just "how the miracle happened," but "on whom the miracle happened, and what kind of society it left behind."

## The Cost and Reflection of the Miracle

Taiwan's economic miracle was successful, but it came with a price.

### Success Factors

1. **Government's forward-looking policies**: Land reform, export processing zones, Ten Major Construction Projects, science parks
2. **People's diligent spirit**: Long working hours, high savings rate, education investment
3. **International environmental opportunities**: US aid during Cold War, contract manufacturing demand in early globalization
4. **Social stability**: Authoritarian system restricted freedom but maintained political stability

### Costs Paid

1. **Environmental pollution**: River, air, soil damage still being repaired today
2. **Labor rights**: Long hours, low wages, lack of protections
3. **Social inequality**: Wealth concentrated among entrepreneurs, workers shared less; land reform itself made small joint landowners bear disproportionate losses
4. **Cultural rootlessness**: For international market integration, local culture was neglected

### Lessons for Today

Taiwan's economic miracle experience remains instructive today:

1. **Education investment is key**: Human resources are Taiwan's most important asset
2. **Manufacturing cannot be neglected**: Services cannot fully replace manufacturing's employment opportunities
3. **Innovation matters more than imitation**: Shift from contract manufacturing to brands and technological innovation
4. **Sustainable development must balance**: Economic growth cannot sacrifice environment and social justice

> **✦** "The most precious thing about Taiwan's economic miracle isn't the income figures, but that it proved one thing: with enough effort, a small place can create great achievements. But the next miracle must be more sustainable, more fair, more humane."

## From Miracle to Transformation: Future Challenges

In 2024, Taiwan no longer needs an "economic miracle." Per capita GDP exceeds US$30,000 — an undeniable developed country. But new challenges are emerging:

### Industrial Transformation Pressure

Manufacturing relocation, low service sector productivity, wage stagnation — Taiwan needs new growth engines. The semiconductor industry is powerful but cannot support the entire economy alone. Taiwan must cultivate more "hidden champion" enterprises.

### Aging Population Challenge

Low birth rates and aging create labor shortages. The past "human wave tactics" development model must change — automation, AI, international talent recruitment are all unavoidable issues.

### Sustainable Development Requirements

Climate change forces Taiwan to rethink its development model. How to transform high-energy industries? How to develop green energy? How to build a circular economy? These are the new era's economic issues.

Taiwan's economic miracle story isn't over. From land reform to TSMC was chapter one, about poverty to modest prosperity. The next chapters must tell of modest prosperity to happiness, from quantitative growth to qualitative upgrade, from economic miracle to social progress.

The authors of this story remain every single Taiwanese person.
