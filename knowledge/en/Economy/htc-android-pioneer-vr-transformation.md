> **30-Second Overview**
>
> HTC was founded in 1997 by Cher Wang, Peter Chou, and H.T. Cho. It manufactured the world's first Android phone, and in 2011 its market capitalization surpassed Nokia to reach NT$1 trillion. However, intensified competition in the smartphone market led to decline. In 2015, HTC pivoted to VR, launching the HTC VIVE which became one of the world's three major VR platforms. In 2024, EPS reached NT$7.21, finding new positioning amid the metaverse wave.

## A Phone That Changed the World

On October 22, 2008, U.S. carrier T-Mobile unveiled a somewhat clunky-looking phone: the T-Mobile G1. It had a thick body, a protruding chin, and its appearance was far less elegant than the iPhone of the same period. But no one expected that this phone, manufactured by Taiwan's HTC, would completely reshape the global smartphone industry landscape.

The T-Mobile G1, officially named the HTC Dream, was the world's first smartphone running Google's Android operating system[^1]. While the iPhone was redefining the smartphone market, HTC chose to partner with Google, becoming the vanguard of the Android camp and opening a whole new era.

Behind this decision lay the most precious traits of Taiwan's tech industry: keen technical judgment and the courage to take risks and innovate. In an era when Nokia still reigned as the mobile phone overlord and BlackBerry dominated the business market, HTC chose to believe in a fledgling operating system and tied the company's fate to it.

## The Golden Trio of Three Founders

HTC's success began with the perfect combination of its three founders.

Cher Wang, daughter of Taiwan's "Business God" Wang Yung-ching, brought substantial financial strength and a macro business vision. She understood technology, had foresight, and was an investor with entrepreneurial qualities.

Peter Chou, a technical genius, was the soul of HTC. He possessed a deep understanding of mobile device technology, having researched mobile computing since the PDA era, and was the technical driving force behind all of HTC's key products.

H.T. Cho, a manufacturing management expert, was responsible for turning innovative ideas into mass-producible products. In an era when Taiwan's OEM manufacturing dominated globally, Cho's manufacturing experience was a critical advantage for HTC.

This combination was near-perfect: capital, technology, and execution capability. The three also shared a common vision—to build a Taiwanese-owned brand that would shine on the global technology stage.

## From PDA OEM to Brand Giant

HTC's story began in 1997, when the company was named "High Tech Computer Corporation" (founded: 1997, listed: 2002), primarily producing Windows CE devices as an OEM for international brands like Compaq and HP.

This starting point seemed ordinary, but it accumulated invaluable technical depth for HTC. From hardware design to software integration, from industrial design to manufacturing management, HTC built comprehensive capabilities in the mobile device field.

In 2006, HTC made a pivotal decision: to launch its own brand. This decision required enormous courage, because the OEM business was already quite stable, and shifting to brand management meant taking on much greater risk.

But Cher Wang's judgment proved correct. She saw the smartphone era approaching, and she saw the historic opportunity for Taiwanese enterprises to transition from OEM to own-brand. HTC had to choose at this critical moment: continue comfortably as an OEM, or bravely forge its own brand.

The result is well known. The 2008 HTC Dream (model: HTC Dream, T-Mobile G1, 2008-10) ushered in the Android era and also opened HTC's golden age.

## 2011: Standing Atop the World

2011 was HTC's peak year. That year, HTC launched multiple well-received products: the HTC Sensation with a dual-core processor, the HTC Desire which became a global bestseller, and the HTC Incredible which achieved great success in the U.S.

Numbers tell the story best. In 2011, HTC's global shipments reached 43 million units, market share hit 8.8%, ranking fourth globally behind only Nokia, Samsung, and Apple[^2]. Even more stunning, HTC's market capitalization once surpassed the then-mobile giant Nokia, its stock price broke the NT$1,000 barrier, and company value exceeded NT$1 trillion.

A Taiwanese company, in just a few years, standing shoulder-to-shoulder with international giants like Apple and Samsung—this was an almost unimaginable achievement at the time. HTC became the pride of Taiwan's tech industry and the best example of a Taiwanese brand going global.

"Quietly brilliant"—HTC's brand slogan perfectly interpreted the traits of Taiwanese enterprises: not showy but outstanding, letting strength do the talking.

## A Precipitous Decline

But the good times didn't last. Starting in 2012, HTC's market position plummeted dramatically. Several key factors led to this dramatic reversal.

First was the changing competitive environment. Samsung rose rapidly with its Galaxy series, gaining dominance in the Android camp; Apple continued launching stunning iPhones, consolidating the high-end market; Chinese brands like Xiaomi and Huawei attacked the mid-to-low-end market with price advantages. In this multi-sided melee, HTC's market share slid continuously.

Second was product strategy missteps. While HTC still held advantages in design and craftsmanship, it lacked breakthrough innovations like Samsung's Galaxy Note series. In a smartphone market with severe feature homogenization, differentiation became crucial.

Most fatal was insufficient marketing resources. Compared to the massive marketing investments of Samsung and Apple, HTC's marketing budget appeared stretched thin. In the era of brand competition, lack of sufficient exposure meant marginalization.

In November 2012, Apple and HTC reached a patent settlement[^3], signing a 10-year licensing agreement (not a pure HTC defeat). This litigation saga burned for years, dealing a considerable blow to HTC's U.S. market business.

## 2015: The Critical Decision to Pivot to VR

Facing the smartphone business predicament, HTC made a critical decision in 2015: enter the virtual reality field.

This decision demonstrated HTC's forward-looking vision. At the time, VR was still an emerging technology with limited market size, and most people were still watching from the sidelines. But Cher Wang keenly sensed VR's enormous potential and decided to go all-in on this entirely new field.

HTC partnered with game company Valve to develop the HTC VIVE head-mounted display. Cher Wang's logic was simple: 80% of VR components are the same as phones, and HTC could replicate its past collaboration model with Google Android—Valve handling software and systems, HTC handling hardware.

In April 2016, the HTC VIVE officially launched (2016-04), immediately causing a sensation in the VR market. With precise positional tracking technology and a premium user experience, VIVE quickly joined Oculus and PlayStation VR as one of the three major VR platforms[^5].

## VIVE Redefines the VR Industry

HTC VIVE's technical innovations cannot be underestimated. Its Lighthouse positional tracking system achieved millimeter-precision spatial positioning, allowing users to move freely in virtual worlds. This technology later became an industry standard, adopted by many third-party VR devices.

HTC's layout went beyond hardware, building a VR ecosystem: the VIVEPORT content platform, SteamVR (the mainstream PC VR platform), and VIVE Studios (VR content creation) forming a closed loop. This ecosystem thinking gave HTC a competitive advantage in the VR field.

In the enterprise market, HTC VIVE found even broader application space: medical surgical simulation, industrial design review, educational training experiences, remote meeting collaboration... The market value of these B2B applications may far exceed the consumer market.

## 2024: Hope for a Rebound from the Bottom

After years of cultivation, HTC's efforts in the VR field began to bear fruit. In 2024, HTC delivered a surprising result: earnings per share of NT$7.21 (2024-FY), far exceeding market expectations[^4].

Behind this performance, HTC's VR layout began to pay off. With the popularization of the metaverse concept, VR technology garnered more attention, and HTC, as a VR industry pioneer, began enjoying first-mover advantages.

Particularly noteworthy is the growth of the enterprise VR market. In the post-pandemic era, enterprise demand for remote collaboration and virtual training has increased significantly, bringing new opportunities for HTC's enterprise VR solutions.

HTC also launched the VIVERSE metaverse platform, laying out Web3.0 and metaverse ecosystems. This platform integrates VR hardware, software, content, and services, demonstrating HTC's ambition for the future digital world.

## New Opportunities in the AI Era

Entering the 2020s, the rapid development of artificial intelligence technology has brought new momentum to the VR industry. HTC has integrated AI technology into its VR products: intelligent scene recognition, personalized recommendations, natural language control—these AI functions significantly enhance the VR user experience.

The maturation of generative AI will completely transform VR content creation models. Previously, creating VR content required extensive time and professional skills; now, through AI tools, ordinary users can quickly create virtual worlds, dramatically lowering the barrier to VR content creation.

HTC has already begun laying out AI+VR combined applications, including AI-driven virtual characters, intelligent VR interaction interfaces, and personalized immersive experiences. These innovations may become HTC's key advantages in the next round of competition.

## The Innovative Spirit of Taiwan's Tech Industry

HTC's story is the best portrayal of the innovative spirit of Taiwan's tech industry.

From Android phone trailblazer to VR technology pioneer, HTC has always stood at the forefront of technological evolution. This spirit of daring to explore the unknown and disrupt tradition is precisely the most precious trait of Taiwan's tech industry.

Although HTC suffered setbacks in the smartphone market, its persistence and innovation in the VR field still demonstrate the competitive strength of Taiwanese enterprises. In the tech industry, failure and success are often separated by a thin line; the key is having the courage to transform and the ability to innovate.

HTC's experience provides a case study for Taiwan's tech industry: in a rapidly changing tech environment, enterprises must possess keen market insight, strong technical capabilities, and the determination to transform courageously.

## A Fresh Start in the Metaverse Era

Today's HTC, though no longer the smartphone market overlord of yesteryear, maintains a solid position in the VR field. With the popularization of the metaverse concept, the maturation of 5G networks, and advances in AI technology, the VR industry is ushering in new development opportunities.

HTC has the opportunity to rise again in this new era. It possesses deep VR technology accumulation, an industry chain layout spanning hardware-platform-content, and enterprise application experience—advantages difficult for other competitors to replicate in the short term.

From a 1997 PDA OEM factory, to a 2008 Android pioneer, to a 2024 VR leader, HTC has used 27 years to demonstrate the resilience and vitality of Taiwan's tech industry. Regardless of future development, HTC's status as a representative of Taiwan's technological innovation has already been deeply etched into industry history.

In this era full of uncertainty, HTC's story tells us: as long as we maintain our original innovative spirit and the courage to transform, Taiwanese enterprises can continue to shine on the global technology stage.
