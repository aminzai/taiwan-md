> **30-Second Overview:** In January 2020, Taiwan could produce only about 1.88 million medical and surgical masks per day. The Central Epidemic Command Center (CECC) requisitioned relevant masks starting January 31, and the machine tool and mask industries completed 92 new production lines within 40 days, pushing daily capacity from approximately 2.71 million to nearly 20 million by the end of May. [^1] [^2] [^3]
>
> This story is often simplified as "everyone worked together, so we succeeded." But what the numbers reveal is more significant: how a group of manufacturers, who normally compete with each other, connected machinery, raw materials, logistics, and distribution systems into a temporary yet effective network without much time to wait for standardization.

## Mask Shortages First Appeared on an Unconnected Production Line

On January 20, 2020, Taiwan established the Central Epidemic Command Center (CECC) for Severe Pneumonia with New Pathogens. Three days later, the Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA) announced that starting January 24, it would control the export of Class 6307 masks, keeping limited medical masks within the country. [^4] [^5]

The problem at the time was concrete: domestic existing capacity was insufficient. The CECC later reviewed that, starting January 31, it continuously implemented a mask requisition policy, with an initial daily capacity of about 2.71 million pieces. _The Reporter_, calculating based on pre-pandemic capacity, recorded a daily output of approximately 1.88 million pieces at that time. These two figures correspond to different time points and cannot be merged into a single baseline. [^1] [^2]

The gap lay in individual machines. Taiwan had mask manufacturers and machine tool operators familiar with automation equipment, control systems, and components. However, mask machines cannot simply be assembled by moving any machine tool over. What the government truly needed to address was enabling different industries to move forward simultaneously regarding raw materials, machinery, installation, trial runs, and manpower. The National Development Council’s case study describes this process as cross-sector collaboration, noting that the Machine Tool Industry Association took the initiative in February 2020 to help connect operators. [^6]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** The first challenge for the Mask National Team was not "whether there were factories," but "whether factories could wait for each other within the same deadline." The crisis turned work that was normally priced separately in the supply chain into a single countdown timer.

## The Forty Days When Machine Tool Factories Stepped Up

The National Team was officially mobilized in early February. _Focus Taiwan_ reported that approximately 100 technicians spent six weeks assembling 92 surgical mask production lines, aiming to increase daily production capacity from 4 million to 13 million pieces. This was a阶段性 (phased) estimate at the time, which explains why the later "92 production lines" did not equate to an immediate output of 20 million finished products on a single day. [^7]

The goal for the first 60 production lines was to complete shipment and installation within one month, adding approximately 6 million pieces of daily capacity. If existing mask factories shifted from 11-hour shifts to 24-hour operations, output would also increase from about 1.88 million to about 4 million pieces. However, actual progress initially lagged: on March 2, daily production was about 8.2 million pieces, falling short of the 10 million piece target set by the Executive Yuan. [^2]

This gap is significant. It reminds us that "assembly completion" of production lines, "stable operation" of machinery, and "allocability" of products are three distinct things. Mask machines require shipment, positioning, calibration, and trial runs; raw materials must also arrive synchronously. If any link slows down, the final impact will be reflected on pharmacy shelves. Data from the National Development Council also noted that operators faced issues such as aging machinery, difficulty in calling for repairs, and delays in ordering new machines. [^6]

At the end of February, the Executive Yuan added a budget of 90 million NTD to purchase an additional 30 production lines, bringing the total number of National Team production lines to 92. Research findings from the Taiwan Institute of Economic Research (TIER) indicate that among these, 2 lines were for surgical band-type masks, the 92 lines were distributed among 15 operators, and mobilization exceeded 3,200 person-times. [^2] [^3]

## Masks Are More Than Just a Machine: Five Processes and a Layer of Meltblown Fabric

Mask machines are only the visible part of connecting the supply chain. Data from the National Development Council summarizes the formation of medical masks into five steps: feeding, forming, welding, cutting, and outputting. The three-layer non-woven fabric must be accurately aligned in different processes, and ear loops or ties must be completed in the later stages. This means "having a machine" does not equal "having qualified products"; raw material specifications, calibration, trial runs, and quality inspections must all be in place simultaneously. [^6]

What is most easily overlooked is the meltblown non-woven fabric in the middle. Only after the meltblown fabric undergoes corona electrostatic charging does it form the important filtration layer of medical masks. Test data compiled by _The Reporter_ shows that filtration efficiency can increase from 37% to 96% after electrostatic charging. Therefore, the Mask National Team did not just add machine quantities; it also had to simultaneously handle PP polypropylene raw materials, meltblown equipment, and material supply. [^2]

| Process Stage | Main Work                                                            | Why We Cannot Just Look at Production Line Quantity                                 |
| ------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Feeding       | Introducing outer, filtration, and inner non-woven fabrics           | Raw material specifications and tension affect subsequent forming. [^6]             |
| Forming       | Folding and positioning multi-layer materials                        | Unstable inter-layer positions affect mask structure. [^6]                          |
| Welding       | Fixing the mask body and connection points                           | Welding conditions need adjustment based on materials and equipment. [^6]           |
| Cutting       | Cutting to specified dimensions                                      | Dimensions and edge quality affect usage and inspection. [^6]                       |
| Outputting    | Completing later-stage operations like ear loops/ties and outputting | Completing output still does not mean packaging and distribution are finished. [^6] |

## Only After Capacity Increased Could Masks Be Fairly Distributed

On February 6, Taiwan launched the first generation of the mask real-name system, allowing citizens to purchase requisitioned masks at health insurance-contracted pharmacies using their National Health Insurance cards. The Ministry of Health and Welfare later explained that the direct background of this system was the sudden increase in demand during the early pandemic, while domestic capacity remained limited; the government needed to distribute requisitioned masks to the public. [^8]

The real-name system did not exist in isolation as a digital innovation. It relied on daily output, requisition quantities, pharmacy inventory, and logistics data to function. Executive Yuan data from April 2020 recorded that starting April 9, each person could purchase 9 adult masks every two weeks. The CECC recorded that purchase quantities gradually increased from 2 pieces every 7 days to 9 pieces every 14 days, following the increase in daily capacity. [^4] [^5]

This is why the "Mask National Team" cannot refer only to machine tool factories. Military reserve personnel assisted with packaging, the Textile Industry Research Institute coordinated requisition practices, Chunghwa Post handled logistics, mask factories managed production, and the government handled requisition, procurement, distribution, and policy pacing. Each unit did different things, but ultimately, they had to converge at the moment a family received its masks. [^4]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** The real-name system looks like a sales system, but is actually more like a distribution network connecting capacity, health insurance data, pharmacies, convenience stores, and logistics. Only when production lines increase does the system have something to distribute; only when the system stabilizes does the increased capacity not just stay in the factory.

## From 16 Million to 20 Million: Numbers Also Have Timestamps

On April 26, 2020, _The Reporter_ recorded that with the 92 National Team production lines added to the existing capacity of mask factories, daily output had reached 16 million pieces, an increase of approximately 8.5 times within three months. By April 27, reports still used 16 million pieces as the "stabilized" daily capacity. [^2]

The CECC’s review published on June 2 used a baseline of approximately 2.71 million pieces on January 31, stating that daily capacity increased to nearly 20 million pieces by the end of May. The Taiwan Institute of Economic Research (TIER) article also summarized the capacity change as "from 1.88 million to 20 million pieces." These numbers seem different, but are actually capacity records from different dates, different statistical methodologies, and different stages. When writing, one should preserve the time points of the sources and not arrange every number into a single unannotated straight line. [^1] [^3] [^4]

Only after capacity and inventory were sufficient to meet domestic demand did the policy begin to loosen export restrictions. The CECC explained that starting June 1, it would change to a daily requisition of 8 million pieces, opening the remaining quantity for domestic or foreign sales, while retaining a mechanism to increase requisition quantities if inventory dropped below 100 million pieces. [^4]

This turning point shifts the story from "how to survive the shortage" to "what to do with the excess capacity." In April, _The Reporter_ had already recorded operators' questions about the sudden end of the pandemic and excess capacity, compiling the MOEA’s then-proposed concepts for strategic reserves, domestic medical needs, and export layouts. [^2]

## Production Lines Abroad: Do Not Confuse Lithuania and the Czech Republic

The overseas story of the Mask National Team must be divided into at least two types. In April 2020, the Ministry of Foreign Affairs announced the first wave of international humanitarian aid, providing the United States with 100,000 pieces per week plus an additional donation of 2 million pieces, donating 7 million pieces to the European Union and other European countries with severe outbreaks, and planning to donate 1 million pieces of Taiwan-produced masks to diplomatic allies. [^14] In 2020, Taiwan aided Lithuania with 100,000 pieces of medical-grade masks [^18], assisting local frontline medical personnel. In 2021, Lithuania donated 20,000 doses of AstraZeneca vaccines to Taiwan.

The official statement from the Presidential Office is clear: the Lithuania case was an aid of finished mask products, not the export of mask production lines confirmed by that source. [^10]

The case with clear records of "production lines arriving abroad" is the Czech Republic. [^15] [^16]

_Focus Taiwan_ reported that the automated medical mask production lines promised for donation by the Taiwan government arrived in the Czech Republic in February 2021. The local _Brno Daily_ reported that this equipment was jointly donated by machine tool operators from southern Taiwan, including 5 fully automatic 3D mask production lines, with equipment costs of approximately 1.52 million USD; the report estimated daily production of 43,000 pieces. [^11] [^13]

Subsequent CNA reports described the estimated volume after the production line went into operation as approximately 5 million pieces per month, noting that some capacity would be donated for public welfare. 43,000 pieces/day and 5 million pieces/month are not the same number; the two may involve different equipment scopes, working days, or estimation stages. Without more complete technical specifications, this article does not convert them into the same curve, but only retains the verifiable conclusion that "Taiwanese equipment has arrived and entered Czech防疫 (epidemic prevention) manufacturing." [^12]

| Overseas Case  | Content Provided by Taiwan                 | Verifiable Result                                                                                                               | Confusion to Avoid in Writing                                                                      |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------ | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lithuania      | 100,000 pieces of medical-grade masks      | Assisted frontline medical personnel. In 2021, Lithuania donated 20,000 doses of vaccines to Taiwan. [^10]                      | Do not write finished mask aid as production line export.                                          |
| Czech Republic | 5 fully automatic 3D mask production lines | Arrived in February 2021, subsequently entered mass production with some capacity planned for public welfare. [^11] [^12] [^13] | Do not directly apply the Czech case's capacity estimate back to Taiwan's domestic daily capacity. |

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** "Taiwan Can Help" operates on two scales: one is delivering masks to other people's hospitals; the other is delivering the method of manufacturing masks to other people's factories. The former solves immediate gaps; the latter leaves behind a capacity that can continue to operate.

## Beyond the Hero Narrative: There Is Also an Industrial Problem

The most common narrative of the Mask National Team is "40 days, 92 lines, 20 million pieces." These numbers are indeed important, but they easily hide industrial difficulties: the government can concentrate resources during a crisis, but cannot guarantee long-term markets relying solely on a single mobilization.

_The Reporter_ raised the question in April 2020 of whether this would become a "mosquito machine" (useless/obsolete equipment) after the pandemic, discussing mask capacity oversupply and industrial upgrading in the same paragraph. This is not a denial of the National Team's achievements, but a follow-up question for the next round of policy: Can government procurement, strategic reserves, international certification, and export markets transform the equipment and experience left by short-term emergency measures into sustainable industrial capacity? [^2]

The MOEA later responded to controversies related to the Mask National Team in official press releases, reiterating that the early pandemic period in 2020 involved cross-ministerial and private sector cooperation, and listing again the claim that daily output increased from 1.88 million to 20 million pieces by the end of May. This data can support the basic facts of capacity and cooperation, but one cannot infer the operational results of every participating manufacturer from it. [^9]

What is certain is that this mobilization allowed Taiwan to see a condition usually not visible: machine tools, textiles, raw materials, mask factories, the military, and logistics, which originally had their own work rhythms, were required by the crisis to share the same node. The speed of mask machines thus became a mirror, reflecting the collaborative capacity accumulated in the supply chain during peacetime, and also reflecting the boundary where temporary mobilization cannot replace long-term systems.

## Screws, Meltblown Fabric, and a Shared Deadline

Looking back at the spring of 2020, the value of the Mask National Team lies not only in pushing output from millions of pieces to nearly 20 million. The memory it leaves is of technicians assembling 92 production lines within approximately six weeks, of mask factories, raw material suppliers, and the government readjusting their rhythm when output lagged, and of pharmacies and logistics turning factory counts into masks truly obtained by families. [^2] [^7]

The resilience of Taiwan’s manufacturing often does not lie on the signboard of a single factory, but in whether different factories are willing to hand over their processes to the same deadline. Forty days later, the 92 production lines began to operate. A few months later, the real-name system moved from limited purchases to broader distribution. [^4] [^8] The question this story truly leaves behind may not be whether we can replicate 92 lines next time, but whether we can maintain that temporarily connected line as a trustworthy mode of cooperation during peacetime.
