> **30-second overview:** On June 15, 1949, Taiwan exchanged 40,000 old Taiwan dollars for one New Taiwan Dollar, attempting to hit pause on an already runaway inflation. Strangely, this currency in actual daily use did not formally become the Republic of China's currency in regulation until 2000. The New Taiwan Dollar's story is therefore not just about swapping banknotes, but a history of how trust, legal name, and daily life gradually caught up with each other.

## 40,000 Banknotes, Unable to Buy Back the Old Life

On June 15, 1949, the Taiwan Provincial Government announced the New Taiwan Dollar issuance regulations. The exchange rule was direct: 40,000 old Taiwan dollars for 1 New Taiwan Dollar. This was not to simplify accounting, but because the old Taiwan dollar could no longer be simplified.[^1]

In the early postwar takeover period, the Bank of Taiwan first issued the Taiwan dollar in 1946, replacing the Bank of Taiwan notes still circulating in the market. Initial denominations were 1, 5, and 10 yuan, but market figures soon inflated, even producing 100,000-yuan time certificates of deposit.[^3]

By late May 1949, prices recorded in the National Archives no longer resembled daily transactions: 1.6 to 1.7 million yuan per 100 jin of rice, 5,000 yuan for a single duck egg. People faced not "things getting a bit more expensive," but the money in their hands thinning daily.[^1]

The old Taiwan dollar's collapse was not an isolated event in Taiwan alone. Fiscal pressure from the Chinese Civil War, cross-regional exchange and material flows transmitted the mainland's financial chaos to Taiwan. Academic research treats 1945–1952 as a key period for studying Taiwan's inflation and stabilization.[^6] [^9]

📝 Curator's note: The New Taiwan Dollar's birth certificate was not a pretty banknote, but a "40,000-to-1" tourniquet.

But "stopping the bleeding" did not mean healed. Between 1948 and 1949, the mainland's currency crisis transmitted to Taiwan through exchange, capital flows, and price linkages. This history later became the subject of economists studying Taiwan's 1945–1952 inflation and stabilization.[^1] [^6]

This angle matters because it prevents the currency reform from looking like a button the government unilaterally pressed. Taiwan at the time bore both postwar reconstruction's fiscal pressure and the exchange consequences linked to the mainland. The New Taiwan Dollar carved a breathable path between two forces.

## The New Taiwan Dollar Did Not Start from Zero

Before the New Taiwan Dollar, Taiwan had already seen several different monies. During the Japanese colonial period, Bank of Taiwan notes circulated; postwar they were first swapped for the Taiwan dollar, then in 1949 for the New Taiwan Dollar. Each renaming involved who could issue, who guaranteed it, and whether Taiwan would be dragged along by another runaway financial order.[^1] [^3]

The 1949 New Taiwan Dollar used the US dollar as its calculation benchmark: 1 New Taiwan Dollar equaled US$0.20, or US$1 to 5 New Taiwan Dollars. The old Taiwan dollar converted at 40,000 to 1. The government simultaneously capped the initial total issuance at 200 million yuan, backed by gold, silver, and foreign exchange reserves, attempting to make the public believe the new notes would not again increase without limit.[^1]

This design reveals a very practical fear: currency reform cannot rely on announcement alone. People who yesterday used million-yuan notes to buy things would not, just because the banknote changed color, instantly trust the government today. To restore trust, issuance caps, reserve assets, and exchange deadlines had to be written into the system.[^1] [^3]

The day after the New Taiwan Dollar's issuance, the Taiwan Provincial Government also halted exchange between the New Taiwan Dollar and the Gold Yuan note or various mainland currencies. This decision cut off the channel through which crisis kept pouring in, and redrew Taiwan's own financial boundary. The National Archives Administration places this step at the core of the currency reform narrative, not merely as an administrative notice.[^1]

## One Banknote, Two Names

![Bank of Taiwan Head Office building, which once handled New Taiwan Dollar issuance operations](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/89/Bank_of_Taiwan_Head_Office_Building_20070727.jpg/1280px-Bank_of_Taiwan_Head_Office_Building_20070727.jpg)

_Image: Carpkazu, CC BY-SA 2.5. See [Wikimedia Commons image detail page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bank_of_Taiwan_Head_Office_Building_20070727.jpg) and [license terms](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.5/deed.en)._

This building was not the New Taiwan Dollar's printing press, but it is a good entry point in institutional history. From 1946, the Bank of Taiwan handled postwar Taiwan dollar and later New Taiwan Dollar issuance operations. The institution name on the banknote entered people's daily lives earlier than the currency's legal identity.[^3]

The New Taiwan Dollar circulated early, but its legal identity remained ambiguous for a long time. After the government relocated to Taiwan in late 1949, the Central Bank downsized its organization, delegating many operations to the Bank of Taiwan, including banknote issuance. The notes bore the Bank of Taiwan name, while monetary authority gradually returned to the Central Bank.[^2]

The Central Bank resumed operations on July 1, 1961, yet continued to commission the Bank of Taiwan as agent for note issuance. This arrangement let a single banknote carry two identities simultaneously: the money Taiwan people used every day, and an institutional legacy whose legal name and central authority had not been fully resolved.[^2] [^3]

![Central Bank First and Second Buildings, where the Central Bank resumed operations in Taiwan in 1961](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/a/a0/Central_Bank_of_ROC_Main_Building_20230510.jpg/1280px-Central_Bank_of_ROC_Main_Building_20230510.jpg)

_Image: Yu tptw, CC BY-SA 4.0. See [Wikimedia Commons image detail page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Central_Bank_of_ROC_Main_Building_20230510.jpg) and [license terms](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/deed.en)._

This awkwardness was not confined to bureaucratic documents. The English-language _Taipei Times_ historical reporting notes that the New Taiwan Dollar was long viewed as a "local currency," even though it had long been Taiwan's de facto legal tender. Even after the Central Bank's 1961 resumption, the issuance-rights arrangement did not immediately eliminate this name contradiction.[^5]

In 1991, the silver yuan — originally the legal currency — was abolished, leaving Taiwan in a state of "actually using the New Taiwan Dollar, but legal text not yet fully caught up." Only in January 2000 did the Executive Yuan announce relevant issuance regulations, stipulating that the Republic of China's currency is the New Taiwan Dollar. That July, the Central Bank began directly issuing the New Taiwan Dollar.[^3] [^5]

📝 Curator's note: A society may first use a money into being the nation's money; the law acknowledges what everyone already knew only years later.

## Kinmen's Banknotes, Reminding You That Currency Has Geography

In the 1950s, Kinmen and Matsu remained in a battlefield environment. The Bank of Taiwan therefore overprinted "Kinmen" or "Matsu" on New Taiwan Dollar notes, restricting their circulation area. These notes looked like the same currency series, but their backs stated where they could be used.[^1]

They are not curiosities only collectors notice, but the financial system's response to geographic reality. Unified issuance, zoned circulation gave frontline areas their own transaction tools, and let the government draw a line between military and exchange controls. Not until 1979 was this overprinting regulation lifted, and Kinmen-Matsu switched to unified application with Taiwan proper.[^1] [^2]

Currency is usually imagined as a nationally universal object, but Kinmen regional notes print another fact on paper: the usage scope is not naturally formed; it is jointly determined by system, war, and market. The place names on the banknotes thus became geographic footnotes of the Cold War era.

## Banknotes Begin to Depict an Island

Early New Taiwan Dollar note imagery was largely tied to political figures and government agencies. In the 1960s, horizontal-format notes gradually appeared, and scenes like the Xiluo Bridge, Suhua Highway, and Eluanbi Lighthouse entered circulating paper money. A 10-yuan note then represented not just purchasing power, but brought public works and transportation infrastructure into every wallet.[^2]

In 1980, the Central Bank issued 500- and 1,000-yuan large denominations for the first time. In 1988, the 100-yuan note changed to red and added Braille, making denominations easier for the visually impaired to distinguish. The red later linked with the folk custom of arranging 100-yuan notes into the "double happiness" character at weddings.[^2]

The 2000 new series changed the imagery direction. Central Bank data shows current denominations include 100, 200, 500, 1,000, and 2,000 yuan, employing new anti-counterfeiting technologies. Among them, Yushan on the 1,000-yuan note, Nanhudashan on the 2,000-yuan note, and Dabajianshan and the Mikado pheasant on the 500-yuan note make natural landscapes and species part of the nation's daily life.[^4] [^7]

The Central Bank Currency Museum's explanation of banknote imagery makes this shift clear: a banknote can resemble a miniature nation, presenting through historic architecture, native flora and fauna, and mountains the image a place hopes to be remembered by.[^7]

This does not mean banknotes have detached from politics. The Presidential Office on the 200-yuan note remains a political building; Yushan on the 1,000-yuan note is not merely a landscape photo. Choosing what enters the wallet and what stays off the paper is itself a cultural choice. Only post-2000 did this selection cease to revolve solely around political figures, beginning to place ecology, land, and local landscapes on the same banknote.[^2] [^7]

📝 Curator's note: The back of a banknote is not empty space. It quietly tells you which mountains, which buildings, and which people an era wants to place in everyone's hands.

## Trust Is Not a Fixed Denomination

The New Taiwan Dollar began with denominations of 1, 5, and 10 yuan, later expanding to 2,000 yuan, reflecting changes in transaction scale and economic life. Ministry of Finance monetary historical materials note that the highest denomination rose stepwise in different periods, from early 5 yuan all the way to 2,000 yuan.[^3]

Larger denominations do not equal currency out of control. Central Bank data shows that at end-2014, the 1,000-yuan note accounted for 80.22% of total circulating currency value, the 500-yuan and 100-yuan notes 3.99% and 5.30% respectively. These figures reflect the public's preference for carrying larger-denomination cash, not standalone evidence for judging price stability.[^4]

This also explains why every note redesign simultaneously addresses two things: usability and trustworthiness. Denominations must keep pace with transactions, paper must be recognizable, watermarks and security threads must make counterfeiting harder. Central Bank currency materials list intaglio printing, color-shifting ink, metallic security threads, and holograms as part of anti-counterfeiting design.[^3] [^7]

Currency credibility thus has a very material side. It is not abstractly written in regulations, but in tangible paper, visible watermarks, touchable Braille, and the few seconds at the checkout counter recognizing a denomination at a glance. When these details operate stably, the system turns from document into habit.

The New Taiwan Dollar's stability therefore cannot be credited to a single currency swap alone. Postwar currency reform first handled issuance caps and foreign exchange reserves; 1950s US aid and production recovery changed fiscal conditions; the Central Bank's 1961 resumption reorganized the system; the 2000 issuance regulations then supplied the legal identity. Policies at these different times together let a piece of paper gradually become money trusted in daily life.[^1] [^3] [^5]

## After the Law Caught Up with Daily Life

The 2000 regulatory change did not make people suddenly switch to a new money. It changed who was written on the banknote as issuer, and how the system described a daily reality that had long existed. When law catches up with life, there are usually no street celebrations. The breakfast shop's change happens as before; the numbers in bank accounts move as before.[^3] [^5]

This "belated formal name" also explains why the New Taiwan Dollar deserves viewing beyond economic history. It has not only prices and exchange rates, but administrative regions, battlefield management, the Central Bank system, banknote imagery, and people's tactile sense of paper authenticity. Each layer answers the same question: what is worth everyone accepting as a common measure of exchange?

📝 Curator's note: A currency's nationality is not necessarily decided on its birthday. Sometimes, a whole generation's repeated use raises it into a nation.

## A History Folded Daily

Today, people getting change at breakfast stalls or buying vegetables at markets rarely think that the New Taiwan Dollar once relied on gold reserves to gain trust, or that the 100-yuan note long awaited formal issuance. Currency is most successful precisely when it need not remind you of its existence.

But the New Taiwan Dollar's history remains in the wallet. 40,000-to-1 remembers the life rupture caused by inflation. Kinmen regional notes remember the battlefield border. Xiluo Bridge and Eluanbi Lighthouse remember infrastructure. Yushan, the Mikado pheasant, and the Presidential Office place nature, politics, and memory in the same denomination set.

The New Taiwan Dollar gained its legal name only in 2000, yet was forced to shoulder an island's life from 1949. It was first a crisis-driven institutional patch; later it became something people pull out without a second thought. This may be currency's hardest job: letting a contested history finally lie quietly on the breakfast table.
