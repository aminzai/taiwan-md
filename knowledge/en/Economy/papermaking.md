> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's papermaking did not begin with a forest, but with the bagasse left behind after sugar factories crushed sugarcane. In 1917, researchers began searching for methods to make paper from bagasse. In 1935, the Zhongxing Paper Mill in Yilan began pulping with bagasse and rat-tail bamboo, becoming a large-scale factory. After the war, the paper industry was taken over by the state, received U.S. aid, and supplied newsprint. Later, under pressure from imported pulp, recycled paper, and digitization, it changed. The stone paper that has recently emerged mixes calcium carbonate mineral powder with resin to form a new material between paper and plastic. It does not need to be written as an environmental miracle that replaces everything, but it allows people to see how Taiwan's paper industry continues to deal with the constraints of raw materials, water, recycling, and pollution. Today, reading a sheet of paper, what we actually read is how an island handles its own resource constraints.

In 1917, Taiwan did not yet have the elegant phrase "paper industry modernization." The more concrete issue at the time was: sugar factories left behind large amounts of bagasse every day. Besides burning these fibers, could they be turned into useful paper? The Japanese chemical industry and papermaking industry invested in research, and the Taiwan Governor's Office selectively provided subsidies. About fifteen years later, in the early 1930s, viable technology was found.[^1] The starting point of this path was not logging, but renaming the byproducts of another industry as raw materials.

This transformation looks environmentally friendly, but in fact it was first a tug-of-war of industrial interests. Bagasse can be used as boiler fuel, compost material, and pig feed. After the war, Taiwan Sugar needed it to maintain its own production, while paper mills needed cheap and stable bagasse to reduce costs. In the 1950s, the supply conflict between Taiwan Sugar and the paper industry made it difficult for Taiwan's paper mills to compete with bagasse and foreign pulp paper mills.[^2]

📝 **Curator's Note:** Taiwan's paper industry's first problem was not "how to make paper," but that the same stalk of sugarcane was needed by three industries at once.

## The Paper Mill in Yilan, Turning Byproducts into the Scale of a City

In 1935, the precursor to the Zhongxing Paper Mill in Yilan's Wujie, "Taiwan Xingye Co., Ltd.," was established. The factory used bagasse and rat-tail bamboo to begin pulping, and later produced newspapers, sanitary paper, Douglas paper, and industrial paper. The historical data of the Yilan County Government records that it was once the largest papermaking factory in Taiwan, and its production volume once reached the forefront of Southeast Asia.[^3] Today, when people enter the Zhongxing Cultural Creative Park, they see warehouses, chimneys, and factory building remnants. In the years when the factory was still operating, these buildings were the pace-makers for workers commuting to work, paper roll shipments, and local life.

The fate of the paper mill was quickly rewritten by war. In 1937, the U.S. military bombed Yilan, and the boiler room, cooking room, pulping factory, laboratory, and warehouse were all damaged. After the war, the government merged five Japanese colonial period papermaking companies into the Taiwan Paper Industry Company, which included the Luodong, Dadu, Xinying, Shilin, and Fengshan five paper mills, and took over the forest farm in Lintian Mountain.[^4] A paper mill here represents not only a technology, but an entire set of methods for the state to receive assets, allocate raw materials, and arrange industrial geography.

Starting in 1953, papermaking was included in the four-year economic construction plan to assist U.S. aid. The Luodong Plant obtained more than 800,000 U.S. dollars and more than 150,000 U.S. dollars in equipment subsidies, purchased new papermaking machines, and built finished product warehouses. In 1958, the Taiwan Paper Industry Company decided to split into branches. The following year, Zhongxing Paper Industry was officially established, becoming the only state-owned enterprise in Taiwan's papermaking industry.[^4]

This history is usually shortened to "U.S. aid brought industrialization." But inside the paper mill, U.S. aid was not an abstract international assistance, but a new paper machine, a warehouse, and a group of people who had to learn how to operate and repair. Paper rolls shipped from the factory, the state was also learning how to manage an industry that needed forests, machines, energy, and markets.

The paper mill is also a site of local politics. The materials collected by the Lanyang Museum record that in 1952, Zhongxing Paper Mill employees who participated in a study group were implicated in the Taiwan Democratic Self-Government League case. The following year, many people were sentenced by military courts, and local interviews left behind the memory of "some people just joined a study group."[^4] This material does not romanticize the paper mill as a simple labor community, but reminds us: industrial sites also bear the weight of state power, ideological control, and local society.

From 1947's annual output of 14,000 tons to the 1960s' export volume increasing from 44,000 tons to 70,000 tons, the historical data of the paper industry association writes this period as industrial expansion. After 1971, export-oriented industries needed corrugated cardboard boxes, kraft paper bags, and white paper boards. Paper mills and paper products also began to jointly handle waste paper quality, import distribution, and product standards.[^8] The growth of the paper industry was therefore not accomplished by a single paper machine, but grew together with bananas, clothing, shoes, fertilizers, and export packaging.

## Self-Sufficiency in Newsprint, Why It Can Also Become a Loss

After the war, the government regarded self-sufficiency in newsprint as part of nation-building. In 1975, Zhongxing Paper Industry installed a waste paper deinking machine, trying to reduce costs and expand the source of raw materials. In 1979, the new paper machine completed its test run, with an annual output of 50,000 tons. Together with the existing production, it was sufficient to supply the domestic press.[^4] This story has a subtle turning point that is not easy to see: increased output does not mean the factory can make money.

The price of Zhongxing Paper Industry's newsprint was restricted by policy, and at one time was lower than the cost. The dumping of imported newsprint and the stagnation of domestic newsprint also made the company start to suffer losses in the 1970s.[^4] The harder the paper mill tried to complete the task of "supplying the domestic press," the more likely it was to leave the contradiction between market prices, state policies, and corporate accounts on itself.

📝 **Curator's Note:** A factory can complete the task assigned by the state, but it does not necessarily complete the arithmetic of the enterprise. The price of paper has never been determined by the paper machine alone.

Zhongxing Paper Industry later experienced privatization. In 2001, employees raised funds to establish Zhongzhong Paper Industry, purchased some equipment, rented some land, and continued production. This transition of employees taking over the factory makes "industrial heritage" not only the government preserving a piece of old factory buildings, but also how the people who used to work inside try to extend their workplace to the next system.[^4]

These changes also changed the meaning of "paper mill workers." In the early days, paper mills needed to handle wood, bagasse, and chemical pulp. Workers had to coordinate between cooking, bleaching, pulping, papermaking, and drying. Later, waste paper deinking and high-speed paper machines joined, and the focus of work shifted to quality control, equipment maintenance, and raw material specifications.

The surface of the paper looks increasingly uniform, but behind it requires more measurement, standards, and experience. The Taiwan Paper Industry Association's historical data records that after 1986, the production of paper and paperboard increased significantly in ten years. The industry also worked with the government to develop paper product national standards, which is precisely the process of industry moving from "can be done" to "stably done."[^8] This standardization also binds the paper industry and downstream users more tightly: printing factories need stable paper size and ink absorption, paper product factories need corrugated paper that can withstand transportation stacking, and household paper needs to find a balance between softness, water absorption, and cost. Paper does not enter society only after leaving the factory. At the moment when the specification is set, it has already predetermined how the next industry will work.[^7]

Local industries are not only transformed by the paper mill. The paper mill in Yilan needs transportation, lumber, storage, repair, and worker housing. The handmade paper in Puli is connected to water sources, agricultural fibers, painting markets, and later tourists. When the factory shuts down or shrinks, the affected are not only the company's revenue. A place name that people are used to, the work rhythm, and family income will also lose their attachments. This is why the preservation of Zhongxing Paper Mill cannot only look at the building's appearance, and the preservation of Puli handmade paper also cannot only display the finished products: both must tell the production relationship back.

In 2014, the Yilan County Government took over part of the old factory area, and after repairs and activation, it was transformed into the Zhongxing Cultural Creative Park. The paper mill is no longer mainly for large-scale production of paper, but begins to produce exhibitions, handicrafts, images, and local memories.[^3] The second life of the industrial site does not whitewash the first history. The reason why the park can be understood is precisely because the chimney and warehouse still point to the factory that once consumed energy, processed raw materials, and paid wages.

## The Water in Puli, Making Paper into Another Speed

If the paper mill in Yilan represents large-scale industry, the handmade paper in Puli is another path. The National Cultural Heritage Network lists Puli handmade paper as a traditional craft, recording its context beginning in the late Qing Dynasty, with existing technology influenced by Japanese papermaking technology, and developing its own processes and characteristics in the local area.[^5]

The process of making a sheet of handmade paper is slow: raw materials need to be processed, pulped, the paper is placed in the pulp tank and shaken left and right, and after the paper is formed, it still needs to be pressed, separated, and brushed with a hot press. The pressed paper is soft like tofu, and is called "paper tofu." The master then uses a paper-taking rod to separate each wet paper, sticking it on an iron plate to dry.[^5] These actions have not been eliminated by large-scale paper machines, but have become the most important knowledge of Puli handmade paper.

Puli's advantage is not just "good water quality." The official registration reason points out that Puli had about fifty papermaking businesses in the 1960s, and was an important supply place for Japanese and Southeast Asian handmade paper.[^5] Taiwan Panorama's report records that in the 1970s, Puli handmade paper once had more than fifty factories, mainly exporting to Japan and South Korea. By the 1990s, low-priced competition and the reduction of calligraphy students in Taiwan led to the gradual withdrawal of factories.[^6]

Guangxing Paper Yard is one of the few remaining examples. In 1965, Huang Yaodong set up Guangxing Paper Yard on the Ailan Terrace. His son Huang Huanzhang took over during the industry's decline, and did not treat paper as a product for mass shipment, but directly sent sample paper to painters, asking users to tell him the pros and cons of the paper. Later, the paper yard made bamboo fiber paper, different textures of painting paper, and paper with product history, and turned the factory into a tourist factory.[^6]

This is a different survival formula for handmade paper and large-scale paper mills. Large-scale paper mills need to handle raw materials, machines, energy, and output per ton. Handmade paper mills put the master, the paper formula, user feedback, and the origin experience into the product. It does not return to the pre-industrial era, but redefines the value of "good paper" in the market gap left by industrialization.

This also explains why Puli handmade paper is worth listing as cultural heritage, not just a local specialty. The official registration data places its value on two things: the technology has a traceable historical context, and it significantly reflects the characteristics of local life. When the paper returns from the factory's output to the master's hand, water, fibers, brushing, and judgment are no longer small steps in the process, but local knowledge itself.[^5]

## Forests Are Abundant, Why Is Pulp Still Dependent on Imports

Another counterintuitive point of Taiwan's paper industry is that a high forest coverage rate does not mean self-sufficiency in papermaking wood. Modern industry data shows that Taiwan's papermaking wood self-sufficiency rate is only 0.5%. About 70% of the pulp used by domestic paper industry comes from waste paper, and pure wood pulp accounts for about 30%. The domestic pulp supply shortage still needs to rely on imports. This makes Taiwan's paper industry connected to two directions: one end is the pulp sources such as Chile, Brazil, and Canada, and the other end is the waste paper that Taiwan classifies, recycles, and delivers to paper mills every day.

Paper is not a single product. Pulp can enter cultural paper, industrial paper, household paper, and packaging paper. Cardboard boxes, sanitary paper, writing paper, and special paper each face different needs and prices. The papermaking process requires pulping, grinding, sizing, screening, and then pressing, drying, coating, and rolling. It uses a large amount of water, energy, and chemical substances, and is not automatically environmentally friendly just by putting a "paper" label on it.[^7]

The Taiwan Paper Industry Association regards the 1973 oil crisis as an important turning point: pulp prices rose, and industrial paper mills began to increase the use of waste paper, which became an early green transformation of Taiwan's paper industry.[^8] This turning point was later standardized by the industry. The upstream of Taiwan's paper industry is pulp, waste paper, wood fiber, bagasse, and wood chips. The midstream is divided into cultural paper, industrial paper, household paper, and special paper. The downstream is connected to cardboard boxes, printing, retail, and export.[^7] Therefore, "recycled paper" is not the name of a kind of paper, but a position in the supply chain that will be reprocessed, re-priced.

But "recycling" cannot be written as a magic without cost. Waste paper needs to be classified, transported, pulped, and re-manufactured. Paper fibers become shorter after multiple cycles, and also face ink, glue, and other admixtures. The circular economy is not to make garbage disappear, but to put raw materials, costs, and pollution back on the same balance sheet.

📝 **Curator's Note:** The most Taiwan-like place of Taiwan's paper industry is not that it turns resources into products, but that it always finds a way to bring in other things when resources are not enough.

## Stone Is Not Paper, But May Become the Next Material of Paper

The name of stone paper is straightforward, but the process is not to cut the stone thin or dip it into the water tank. It usually mixes mineral powders such as calcium carbonate, and high-molecular polymers such as PE and PP, and then forms sheets through extrusion, calendering, blowing film, or sheet pulling. The feel of the finished product is close to paper, but more like "a highly mineral-filled synthetic material." In other words, it is a material between paper and plastic film, and should not be directly equated with traditional wood pulp paper.[^11] [^13]

Taiwan's stone paper story is often linked to Longzun Composite Materials in Tainan. The report of the National Federation of Industries records that Liang Shihui, in his early years, in order to increase the calcium carbonate content in plastic bag raw materials and reduce costs, further thought about whether stone powder could replace pulp, and then invested in many years of research and development. This is the R&D process in the corporate report, which can explain how Taiwan brought plastic processing experience into paper material innovation, but cannot be used alone to prove the environmental effects of all products.[^12]

Taiwan Patent TWI862349B proposes another path: using biodegradable resins such as PBAT, active calcium carbonate, degradation aids, biodegradable carriers, and reinforcing fillers to form materials, and then using metering hoppers, parallel twin-screw extruders, and flat-mouth dies to form sheets. The patent text lists an embodiment containing 60 wt% active calcium carbonate, and also lists the extruder temperature zone of 110 to 170°C. These are the formulas and processes proposed by the applicant, not the common standards of all stone paper products by a third party.[^11]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The most worth writing about stone paper is not the slogan "no need to cut trees," but it moves the papermaking problem to another test paper: with less wood, how to calculate the resin, mineral powder, recycling system, and product life cycle?

The environmental narrative of stone paper must retain conditions. The biodegradable formula proposed in the patent cannot be extended to all stone paper products with PE or PP as the base on the market. The survey report also points out that traditional high-fill composite paper may face recycling difficulties, and some biodegradable claims lack sufficient third-party data.[^11] [^13] A more practical issue is, if stone paper enters the existing waste paper recycling line, whether the mineral powder and resin composition will interfere with the original paper fiber recycling, still needs to be tested based on the material formula, classification method, and processing equipment, and cannot be concluded with just "recyclable."

This makes stone paper an extension of Taiwan's papermaking history, not the end. Bagasse once brought the byproducts of the sugar industry into the paper mill, recycled paper brought households and logistics into the industry, and stone paper connected minerals, plastic processing, biodegradable materials, and paper usage. It may be suitable for waterproof, tear-resistant, or special packaging purposes, but does not mean it can replace writing paper, newsprint, and all cardboard boxes. The real question that new technology needs to answer is still: where does the raw material come from, where does it go after use, and who bears the environmental cost.

## A Sheet of Paper, Still Rewriting Its Raw Materials

From bagasse to pulp, from pulp to recycled paper, Taiwan's papermaking history seems to be a material replacement, but in fact every replacement involves a way of life. This also makes the paper industry different from the general "Taiwan's outstanding" story. It does have technological breakthroughs, and has once had export growth and large factories, but every growth is accompanied by the cost of raw material prices, pollution, import competition, and local labor. If it is only written as an increase in output, it will not see the true characteristics of Taiwan's paper industry: it has never started after having unlimited resources, but has been maintained through cross-industry cooperation and replacement solutions under the condition of incomplete resources.

From bagasse to pulp, from pulp to recycled paper, Taiwan's papermaking history seems to be a material replacement, but in fact every replacement involves a way of life. Bagasse makes the sugar industry and the paper industry interdependent. The paper mill makes the settlements and labor in Yilan form their own rhythm. The water and handmade technology in Puli make paper a local craft. Recycled paper connects household classification, logistics boxes, factory equipment, and energy issues.

Today, Taiwan's paper and paperboard production is still mainly driven by industrial paper and packaging needs. In 2024, the total paper production was about 3.896 million tons, with about 2.52 million tons for domestic sales and about 1.368 million tons for export. The paper product output was about 745,000 tons. These figures show that the paper industry has not disappeared with digitization, but the demand has shifted from newspapers and writing to logistics, food, household, and industrial packaging.[^7]

The future of the paper industry is therefore not only about "using less paper." Using less paper will change the demand for cultural paper, but will not automatically eliminate the demand for cardboard boxes, sanitary paper, medical paper, and food packaging.

The real question is: which paper is worth recycling, which packaging can be reduced, which energy can replace high-carbon processes, and whether the paper mill should position itself as a raw material processor, packaging designer, or local cultural preserver. The TPEX industry information also lists high energy consumption, process water pollution, odor, and volatile organic compounds as environmental issues that the paper industry must face.[^7]

This problem does not have a beautiful answer. Paper is easier to recycle than some disposable plastics, but the pulp may still come from distant forests. Recycled paper can reduce the pressure on raw materials, but it needs stable classification and re-manufacturing equipment. Handmade paper preserves local technology, but cannot replace the millions of tons of industrial demand. The most honest picture of Taiwan's paper industry is that these answers exist at the same time.

Therefore, when we enter the site of a paper mill, the most worth asking is not "how much paper was made here before," but "how much of other things was needed here for the paper to be made": the bagasse of the sugar factory, the timber of the mountain, the pulp from abroad, the recycled materials classified by households, the hands of workers, and the heat of machines. The paper is thin, but the supply chain behind it is thick enough to write a history of Taiwan's industry. This history also reminds us not to misread industrial transformation as a single line of progress: the withdrawal of large-scale paper mills may be the loss of local jobs, the opening of cultural parks may be the re-packaging of memory, the revival of handmade paper may depend on the tourism market, and the increase in recycled paper may shift the responsibility of classification to households and cleaning teams. Every "new use" will reallocate costs and value.

If we want to leave the shortest annotation for Taiwan's papermaking, perhaps it is not "from traditional to modern," but "after every change of raw materials, institutions, and markets, re-determine what is worth keeping." Bagasse did not stay in paper forever, and the paper mill did not stay on the production line forever. But technology, labor, and local memory can still be used in another form.
