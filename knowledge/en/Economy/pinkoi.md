> **30-Second Overview:** In August 2011, Yan Jun-ting, Li Rang, and Lin Yi-chun founded Pinkoi in Taipei. It uses a curation system for originality, local teams and logistics for cross-border trade, and accumulated consumption data to provide recommendation and brand services. The counterintuitive aspect of Pinkoi is: this e-commerce company did not first pursue having the most products, but instead turned "designs that are difficult to describe, ship, or find" into daily items that can be exchanged internationally.[^1] [^2]

In August 2011, Yan Jun-ting, Li Rang, and Lin Yi-chun founded Pinkoi in Taipei, aiming not for another online store piled high with goods, but a platform where design brands and consumers could meet.[^1] [^2] By 2018, the platform had accumulated 4 million international members, expanding beyond Taiwan to Japan, Thailand, and Hong Kong. Six years later, public reports showed 6.25 million members and products sold to 150 countries.[^8] [^2]

This growth looks like a typical e-commerce story, but the real contrast lies here: **Pinkoi did not treat "more is cheaper" as the answer; it first acknowledged that designed goods are not suitable for management as standardized items.** It layered curation, designer communities, cross-border services, and data tools one by one. What was ultimately sold was not just a product, but a path to be found for small brands.

## The Problem Starting from a Two-Ping Office

Yan Jun-ting originally worked in Silicon Valley, USA. After graduating from the Department of Civil Engineering at National Taiwan University (NTU), he pursued studies in electrical and computer engineering in the US. At age 32, he quit his job and returned to Taiwan with his family to start a business. The early office was just a two-ping space partitioned off in their home; while coding, he was also raising children, and bottles had even been knocked over onto the keyboard. These details are not decorations for a startup myth, but explanations of the problems Pinkoi initially faced: there was no ready industry waiting for them to take over.[^2]

The founders' inspiration came from visiting markets and small designer shops. Those creators were good at making things, but they were not necessarily familiar with promotion, listing, logistics, or cross-border sales. If they were just put into the standard process of a large e-commerce platform, the story of the work, its dimensions, customization needs, and production time would become trouble within the system.[^2] [^9]

Therefore, the early Pinkoi team traveled across markets, handing out business cards to creators and inviting them to list their items. DFUN interview records noted that many creators at the time viewed online consumption as unrealistic, even suspecting the team that proactively contacted them was a scam. The platform had to make the process smooth enough for people to move from suspicion to willingness to try.[^9]

## "Not Enough Special" Can Also Be a Business Strategy

In 2018, _Business Weekly_ called Pinkoi's approach "anti-conventional business." At the time, the platform had over 8,000 design studios and 900,000 items, sold to 88 countries globally. However, the seller acceptance rate was only about 10%, with applicants undergoing review for multiple criteria including originality, photo quality, and sales experience.[^3]

The significance of the curation system was not just filtering products for buyers, but also drawing boundaries for the platform: if a product was "familiar," potentially infringing on intellectual property, or reported by customers after listing, it could be eliminated. DFUN's personal interviews also mentioned that Pinkoi maintained the line of "originality" through its mechanisms of curation, invitation, patrolling, and reporting.[^3] [^9]

This is contrary to the intuition of general platforms seeking supply volume. Fewer sellers meant fewer items available for immediate listing. But for customers who did not want an item identical to someone else's, choice itself was value. _Business Weekly_ recorded that Pinkoi’s members were mostly women aged 25 to 44, and the platform responded to the need "not to be like others" through product selection and customization.[^3]

Curation also brought costs. The platform could not just wait for brands to grow on their own; the early team once drove south every week to visit designers, taking a year to develop the first 100 designers. The same report also mentioned that Pinkoi held workshops teaching photography, packaging, and shipping cost calculation, and organized physical markets without commission. These practices showed that a platform is not just connecting two sides; it must fill in the missing capabilities for both sides.[^3]

These intermediary tasks also changed the role of designers. Designers no longer just needed to create an item; they also had to learn photography, calculate shipping costs, explain production methods, respond to customization questions, and understand how different markets read their brand. The platform provided tools but also returned part of the work of "becoming a brand" to the creators. Convenience and burden often appear together. The workshops recorded by _Business Weekly_ revealed this often-overlooked labor.[^3]

For consumers, curation turned trust into an invisible sorting mechanism. Buyers might not know how a certain brand was selected, but they treat the platform's name as a preliminary guarantee. If non-original products were mixed in, it was not just the individual store that suffered, but also the aesthetic promise the platform made to all stores. The patrolling and reporting mechanisms mentioned in DFUN interviews show that this commitment must be continuously maintained, not set once at launch.[^9]

## From Selling Products to Operating a Cross-Border Community

In 2018, Pinkoi launched Pinkoi Experience, extending design from "buying an item" to a lifestyle experience led by the brand owner. CAREhER's interview placed this shift in a larger context: consumers not only wanted to know if the product was good but also wanted to know about the culture, history, and makers behind the brand.[^8]

This platform relationship addressed three groups simultaneously: consumers, designers, and the local service teams. Yan Jun-ting mentioned in an interview that offline activities allowed consumers to interact with designers, and it allowed designers to directly understand consumer preferences. Local teams in Japan, Hong Kong, and Thailand were responsible for making the cross-border community more than just a translated page.[^8]

> Photo Caption: Visual from the 2023 Design Fest on the Pinkoi official product page; this is not a cross-border logistics flowchart, but the original image of the platform extending from online transactions to offline design activities.[^12]

Cross-border trade is not just changing the product page language. CAREhER recorded that Pinkoi made mistakes in the Japanese market early on: seemingly minor details like logos, fonts, and translations were insufficient in respecting local culture, leading to consumer distrust. This experience reminds us that what is hardest to translate in cross-border e-commerce is often not text, but "what looks like a trustworthy brand."[^8]

> 📝 Curator's Note: The moment a platform truly goes abroad is not when it can ship goods, but when it is willing to admit that it does not know how locals view a word, a logo, or a promise.

## Logistics: The Invisible Threshold for Designed Goods

A 2024 report in _Yuanjian_ stated that Pinkoi's overseas traffic first exceeded 30% in 2023. Japanese order turnover grew by over 20% annually for three consecutive years, and Taiwanese design brands accounted for over 50% of cross-border orders to Japan. The report also noted that the platform had 52,000 brands from 77 countries and 2 million designed items sold in 150 countries at that time. These figures belong to the point in time mentioned in the report in 2024 and cannot be taken as fixed numbers today.[^2]

The same report placed Pinkoi's next step on Taiwan-Japan logistics. The platform invested over 1 billion Japanese yen to build services and infrastructure between Taiwan and Japan, inviting the first batch of 50 Taiwanese brands to participate in direct shipping, attempting to handle customs duties, shipping losses, exchange rates, and delivery transparency. For small design brands, being able to make a product does not mean it can be reliably delivered to another country. Cross-border logistics is the segment where creativity becomes business.[^2]

A 2024 strategy reported in _Digital Age_ also pointed out that designers could check "agree to cross-border" on the backend, sending products to markets such as Taiwan, China, Hong Kong, Japan, and Thailand, with the platform assisting in complex processes involving financial accounts and logistics partners. The core of this service is not turning designers into logistics experts, but integrating the work they are least skilled at but that most easily stalls business into the platform.[^4]

Pinkoi's differentiation was therefore not just what was visible on the homepage. It was also in handling returns, packaging, translation, payment, and local market expectations. _Yuanjian_ cited platform data stating that Pinkoi's return rate was 3%, but this is company and platform data from the report, and should be read along with its statistical scope.[^2]

## When Data Starts Choosing Gifts for People

Pinkoi began building an AI and data foundation in 2015 because designed goods do not have a fixed set of specifications like phones, headphones, or washing machines. Cherubic's interview records show that the team spent years organizing data from mobile phones, desktops, apps, and websites, handling multilingual contexts including Chinese, Japanese, Thai, and Cantonese.[^7]

In 2023, Pinkoi publicly launched the "Lifestyle Intelligence Model." _Digital Age_ reported that this model was built on over 7.2 billion pieces of consumption behavior data, linking product attributes with consumer behavior for personalized shopping, ad placement, and cross-border assistance. Pinkoi stated that after the model went online, click-through rates increased by 1.5 times, browsing time increased by 125%, and it generated over 8 times more orders for design brands. These are performance figures presented by the company to the media; the article does not rewrite them as conclusions verified by independent research.[^5]

Data also brought new responsibilities to the platform. The recommendation model can describe products in greater detail, but it cannot automatically tell us which designers are difficult to recommend due to insufficient data. It can see clicks and dwell time, but that does not equal understanding why a person lingered on a certain page. The "Lifestyle Intelligence Model" mentioned in the article is Pinkoi's naming and explanation of its own service; the safer reading is to view it as a set of platform tools rather than a psychological test defining consumers.[^5]

> Photo Caption: Main image from the Meet Global interview with Pinkoi CEO; the article discusses Pinkoi’s cross-border transaction tools, AI translation, designer community, and Japanese market experience.[^11]

The same model also tried to solve details of language and classification. The report cited an example where "mountain backpack" in Taiwan might use a different name in Hong Kong. The model can add regional names as tags, giving the product a better chance of being found in another market. Pinkoi stated that the proportion of effectively tagged products increased from 60% to 98%.[^5]

Data services are not just for consumers. In 2019, Pinkoi began providing retail media networks, allowing design brands to place product, brand, and segmented advertisements on the site. After setting a budget and selecting a product, the system assists in placement. Both Cherubic and _Digital Age_ reported that related advertising and SaaS services have become part of the platform's revenue, but since the statistics and terminology differ between the two articles, this text only retains common verifiable directions without adding percentages from different time points.[^7] [^5]

> 📝 Curator's Note: The more precise Pinkoi makes "recommendation," the more like a familiar salesperson the platform becomes; but even if the salesperson knows you well, they should not decide what is worth liking for you.

## Crowdfunding: Moving Before Product Birth

In 2022, Pinkoi entered crowdfunding from design e-commerce, planning its first batch of 6 projects. _Digital Age_ reported that this was not a transformation abandoning the original e-commerce model, but an extension of existing logistics, advertising, and data services before product launch. Design brands could first raise funds through crowdfunding and then connect their products to Pinkoi's existing cross-border market.[^6]

This approach continued Pinkoi's requirement for originality: the crowdfunding platform only accepted 100% original design brands, and the brand had to pass Pinkoi e-commerce certification first. The platform also provided added value services such as logistics, invoicing, and advertising placement.[^6] It did not guarantee that every idea would succeed, but it tried to guide designers from "having an idea" to "being able to produce, deliver, and sell."

For the platform, this was also a business model test. The report noted that Pinkoi's in-site advertising launched in 2020 and contributed over 20% of revenue two years later. Crowdfunding was thus seen as an application of existing AI, logistics, and SaaS infrastructure, not just adding another product category. This is the time point of the 2022 report and cannot be used to infer today's revenue structure.[^6]

## The Other Side of the Platform: Turning Choice into Rules

> Photo Caption: Photo from BRIDGE’s 2016 coverage of Japan’s Iichi collaborating with Taiwan’s Pinkoi; it corresponds to the historical section "Platform extending cross-border from Taiwan to Asia."[^13]

Pinkoi's curation and recommendation made it easier for users to find products that were "suitable for them," but any filtering also meant exclusion. Originality needed judgment, style needed naming, and markets needed segmentation. When these judgments are written into the platform process, designers face not just consumers, but an invisible classification system. This is not a unique problem for Pinkoi, but a power dynamic that all curated platforms must clarify: who decides what is worth seeing?[^3] [^5] [^7]

Another tension lies in the platform's dual identity. On one hand, it must maintain product quality for consumers; on the other, it must provide traffic, advertising, logistics, and cross-border services for design brands. When a brand buys RMN ads, the platform is both the market manager and the matchmaker within the market. Cherubic’s interview records noted that Yan Jun-ting emphasized that user experience cannot be determined solely by bidding price. This was a principle stated by an interviewee; whether it can be maintained across different markets and under different business pressures requires continuous observation.[^7]

These contradictions do not disappear with the launch of AI. The model reduces search costs but cannot answer value questions like "what is originality" or "who should be included." What Pinkoi's technological investment truly brought was not eliminating the curator, but breaking down the curatorial work into tags, processes, ad slots, and recommendation results, making judgments that were originally human-made faster, broader, and more needing explanation.

From an e-commerce platform in 2011, to a service experience in 2018, and then to crowdfunding in 2022, Pinkoi did not discard its previous identity each time it moved forward; instead, it pushed the problems it had already dealt with further: how designers are found, how brands are understood, how products are delivered to another market, and how initial support is secured before mass production. This line makes it look like continuous transformation, but it is more like expanding a single unfinished intermediary task.[^6] [^8] [^9]

## Not Competing with Giants on Size

The challenges Pinkoi faces are not small. A 2024 interview listed post-pandemic real consumer return, the increase in low-priced goods from China, and the e-commerce market's "the strong survive" as environmental factors the platform must face. Cherubic cited Yan Jun-ting’s statement that Pinkoi chose to position itself on the track of non-standardized goods and Asian cross-border capabilities, rather than directly competing in capital with platforms selling standardized products.[^7]

This choice is not romanticizing niche markets. Curation slows down supply; customization increases communication costs; cross-border brings logistical and cultural risks; and data models require long-term organization. Pinkoi's approach simply changed the unit of competition: from "who can deliver fastest at the lowest price" to "who can get a difficult-to-describe, hard-to-ship, and not necessarily mass-produced item into the hands of someone who truly wants it." This is an observation compiled from the above reports, not a single metric self-proclaimed by Pinkoi.[^2] [^3] [^7]

Pinkoi's early figures—"10% acceptance rate," 1.75 million members in 2018, 4 million members in 2020, and 6.25 million members in the 2024 report—cannot be compressed into a growth curve without time markers. Each belongs to different articles, different years, and potentially different statistical scopes. What is certain is that at every stage, the platform prioritized "how to make design brands visible" over scale numbers.[^3] [^8] [^2]

## Beyond the Product: What Else Remains?

What is important about Pinkoi is not how beautifully it speaks of exporting Taiwanese design, but how it breaks down export into many unglamorous yet necessary details: establishing trust when inviting designers to list for the first time, judging originality during curation, facing fonts and logistics during cross-border trade, using data during recommendation, and securing support for a product that is not yet complete during crowdfunding.[^3] [^8] [^9] [^6]

The platform also left unfinished questions. When member behavior is converted into tags, and when advertising and recommendations become more precise, do consumers get easier choices or harder to leave the room of their existing preferences? When curation sets a threshold for originality, who has the capacity to invest time, photos, samples, and brand stories first, and who might be judged as not mature enough outside the gate? These questions cannot be glossed over with "the design is beautiful," nor are there ready numbers to answer them.

But looking back at the three-person team from Taipei in 2011, Pinkoi's counterintuitive nature remains clear: it did not treat niche consumption as a market waiting to be educated, but treated the niche itself as a world that needs proper service. Whether the platform can maintain this commitment depends on whether it is still willing to spend time dealing with things that cannot be completed with one click.

## Further Reading

To continue understanding Taiwanese design and the platform economy through Pinkoi, one can read it alongside Taiwan's design media, crowdfunding, and other online platforms. Pinkoi's uniqueness lies not in representing the entire design industry, but in placing "originality, cross-border trade, and daily consumption" on a single platform, making designers' problems also logistics, data, and community issues.[^4] [^6] [^9]
