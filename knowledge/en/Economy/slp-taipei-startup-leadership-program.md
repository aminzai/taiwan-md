# SLP Taipei Startup Leadership Program: A Course That Started at NT$6,000 and Rose to NT$58,000 Yet Remains Hard to Get

In July 2013, a reporter for _Business Today_ did the math. At the time, a group in Taipei was running a training camp called the “Startup Leadership Program” and charging NT$6,000 for a package that included seventeen sessions, three of which were full‑day workshops. Dividing the total fee by the total instructional hours produced a headline: less than NT$100 per hour[^1].

The same program, fourteen years later, now costs NT$58,000 for a one‑time fee[^2]. Around thirty spots are available, and the applicant pool exceeds one hundred each year[^3].

The fee has risen almost tenfold, yet the number of applicants has only grown. The core logic of the organization has not changed at all since the first cohort: a group of founders who are also entrepreneurs run a “founders help founders” class without taking any equity.

> **30‑second overview:** The Startup Leadership Program (SLP) Taipei chapter is the Taipei branch of a global nonprofit startup initiative founded in Boston in 2006; it launched in Taipei in 2012 as the 22nd chapter worldwide[^4]. It offers a six‑month intensive curriculum, a two‑day‑one‑night consensus retreat, and a Pitch Day, charging a single fee and taking no equity. The fee has risen from NT$6,000 in 2012 to NT$58,000 in 2026, but the website consistently states that “all funds are used for participants”[^2]. Over fourteen years it has gathered entrepreneurs from technology, food & beverage, law, design and other fields, most of whom organize the program without taking any money. This illustrates how Taiwan’s civil society can sustain a long‑running initiative without government subsidies or venture‑capital equity.

## The Account from NT$6,000 to NT$58,000

First, let’s look at the numbers, because they are the most concrete entry point to understanding SLP Taipei.

The first cohort in 2012 paid a one‑time fee of NT$6,000. Media at the time called it a “material fee,” but that phrasing was the journalist’s own paraphrase; the SLP website has never used the terms “material fee” or “tuition” at any point, always referring to a “one‑time fee”[^5]. This consistent terminology is a clue: they do not see themselves as a “course‑selling cram school.”

Data on the intermediate price jumps are spotty, but the available points trace a steep upward curve in the later years. First cohort = NT$6,000; around the 11th cohort the fee rose to NT$25,000; the 14th cohort (2024) was NT$48,000; the 15th cohort (Sept 2026 – Jan 2027) reached NT$58,000[^6]. The first decade saw the fee climb from six thousand to twenty‑five thousand; the next two‑three years it jumped from twenty‑five thousand to nearly sixty thousand. Taiwan’s startup ecosystem itself moved from a nascent stage to a more institutionalized phase during that period, and SLP’s fee curve can be read as a physical gauge of that institutionalization.

What does NT$58,000 buy? The website lists it clearly: instructor fees for more than twenty sessions, venue costs, the two‑day‑one‑night consensus retreat, Pitch Day, a year‑end banquet, and the graduation ceremony—all “used for participants”[^2]. There is no office, no seed capital.

It is worth pausing here for honesty. An organization that positions itself as a nonprofit, volunteer‑run education project has raised its fee from six thousand to nearly sixty thousand without publicly explaining each increase. Even the website’s aggregate statistics do not line up perfectly across pages (the homepage says “more than 360 graduates,” the association page says “nearly 300,” the 13th‑cohort admission page mentions “over 150”)​[^7]. The numbers are small and inconsistent, but they are real and verifiable; they do not constitute external criticism. The Taiwanese startup community has hardly discussed this publicly, and this article does not intend to label the practice “contradictory” or “deceptive.” It simply places the figures alongside the organization’s self‑description so readers can draw their own conclusions.

## No Equity, but You Must Also Contribute Time

That question naturally arises: without taking equity and without operating like a typical incubator, how can a model that relies solely on a one‑time fee cover venue and instructor costs?

The answer is hidden in a single sentence that appears on the global SLP website. The logic is common to the entire SLP network; Taipei did not invent it. The Indian chapter’s English page states it most plainly: “We take zero equity but we charge them…to take care of operational expenses.” The site goes on to explain that the program follows a lean, community‑driven model, and each participant must volunteer roughly twenty hours of time to help run the program. This mechanism cultivates a sense of “skin in the game” for fellows—not in the form of shares, but in the form of time and effort[^8].

In other words, participants are both paying customers and volunteers. Your money covers the hard costs; your time is the true fuel of the community. “Skin in the game” here refers to personal commitment, not equity. This explains why a program that raises its fee each year can still have core organizers who take no money. _INSIDE_ reported in 2013 that the SLP Taipei execution team participated “entirely on a voluntary, unpaid basis”[^9]. Money flows to participants; people flow into the community.

Compared with commercial startup bootcamps that charge NT$70‑80 k for two full‑day workshops and market hype louder than substance, SLP’s fee is not cheap, but every dollar’s destination and every organizer’s unpaid status are laid out transparently. The “no equity, but you must give time” line is how SLP distinguishes itself from venture‑backed accelerators and from tuition‑charging cram schools.

## One Organizer Later Became a Legislator

If there is a name that surprises most readers, it is Ko‑ju‑jun (葛如鈞).

Online he is known as “Doctor Bao.” He executed the SLP Taipei program in 2013‑2014. He later said that the experience of running SLP Taipei was “a key factor in his admission to Singularity University”​[^10]—the Silicon‑Valley‑based institute famous for exponential‑technology focus. Afterwards, he became a non‑district legislator for the Kuomintang.

From a young volunteer running a startup bootcamp, to Singularity University, to the Legislative Yuan, that trajectory never appears in any SLP prospectus. It reminds us that SLP Taipei’s output is far more than startup companies. It is a place that gathers people, lets them hone their skills, and then sends them off in directions the founders could never have imagined. Sometimes the biggest change is experienced by the organizers themselves.

This also raises a lesser‑known question about SLP Taipei’s origins: who were the people that launched it in 2012? The global SLP story is clear—founded in Boston in 2006 by Anupendra Sharma and his father‑in‑law Puran Dang, the first cohort had seven fellows, lasted twelve months, and focused on life sciences​[^12]. When the Taipei chapter launched, the earliest blog post simply stated, “SLP was founded in 2006 in Boston and had expanded to twenty cities by 2012,” without naming the Taipei founders​[^11]. One confirmed co‑founder is Hsin‑Fu Kuo (郭信甫), now CEO of the sports‑tech company bOMDIC; in the 2012‑2013 execution‑team bio he was listed as “CFO of M2 Communication,” a role unrelated to his current position​[^12]. When a project runs for a decade, the names of the earliest participants can become blurry—common for grassroots, unincorporated groups, and another facet of its “people‑first, rule‑free” nature.

## Personal Traits Count for Half: They Recruit People, Not Companies

SLP Taipei’s selection rubric makes its philosophy explicit.

The weighting is: personal traits 50%, goal planning 10%, learning & failure experience 10%, leadership & professional achievements 10%, company size 10%, other 10%​[^13]. Personal traits alone account for half the score; company size only a tenth. This means they care about “who you are” far more than “how big your company is.” There are no age or industry restrictions; you do not even need to already own a company.

How many people compete? The second cohort attracted over 140 applicants; the organization called it “the highest‑ranking SLP worldwide,” and ultimately admitted 33 participants plus an additional eight​[^14]. That yields an acceptance rate of roughly 20 %. The picture that emerges is counter‑intuitive: fees rise, but the gate remains “are you the right person?” while over a hundred applicants vie for thirty spots each year.

Admitted participants come from a surprisingly diverse set of industries. The first cohort in 2012 was composed of 40 % internet startups, 40 % biotech, and 20 % cultural‑creative​[^1]; by the 13th cohort (2024) the website listed sectors as “software technology, e‑commerce & retail, interior design, health care, law & marketing”​[^15]. There were pizza‑oven restaurants, law firms, and e‑commerce logistics teams. The early cultural‑creative share (20 %) faded over time, a drift that has received little commentary. In the 12th cohort (2023) the roster included brand‑research founder KT Chu, JENJAN founder Zhang Heshang, Wu Zheyu and about thirty other entrepreneurs across many fields​[^16]. The heavy weighting on personal traits yields exactly this broad, horizontally diverse community.

> **📝 Curator’s note**  
> A scoring sheet that gives “personal traits” 50 % is, in effect, an admission that the course does not sell pure knowledge. The twenty‑session curriculum (business‑model canvas, fundraising simulations, pitch training) can be found online or in bookstores; it does not require NT$58,000. What is truly scarce—and what participants actually pay for—is the six‑month period in which thirty equally driven peers and a whole line of alumni watch you work. They are not selecting “people who can be taught,” but “people worth being known by this group.”

## Alumni Return to Teach

Staying at SLP Taipei long enough reveals a cycle: today’s instructor was often yesterday’s participant.

Chen Chin‑chang (Casper) was a fourth‑cohort fellow who later became a recurring instructor, teaching organizational growth and team leadership​[^17]. Lu Yong‑wei (Boz) is an SLP alumnus who returned to run a brand workshop. This “alumni‑to‑new‑cohort teaching” pattern is institutionalized as the Founder Buddy system: each 15th‑cohort participant is paired with a 14th‑cohort Buddy for a monthly or bi‑monthly exchange​[^18]. Peer‑to‑peer mentorship passes horizontally from cohort to cohort.

On the mentor side, continuity is striking. Amber Chang (張毓純) of Xizi Technology has been listed as an instructor since the very first cohort in 2012​[^19]. The mentor pool comes from Boston consulting firms, PwC, KPMG, and eleven venture‑capital or corporate investors such as China Development, Puxun, Yiding, and Huayi​[^20]. A graduate may become a future instructor; a mentor may stay for a decade or more. This human‑resource structure costs almost nothing, relying on the same people returning repeatedly—exactly the “skin in the game” principle.

The curriculum revolves around three high‑emotional‑intensity settings. The consensus retreat is unique to SLP: a two‑day‑one‑night ice‑breaker where participants lower their guards in a hotel conference room​[^21]; then twenty‑plus intensive sessions covering the Business Model Canvas, fundraising simulations, and pitch training; finally Pitch Day, where each participant delivers a three‑minute founder pitch in the morning and showcases a booth in the afternoon. Earlier it was called “Demo Day,” but in recent years it has been standardized as Pitch Day. Pitch Day has no prize money, no judges, no rankings; it is simply a showcase of six months’ work. The combination of the intensive half‑year, the tearful consensus retreat, and the three‑minute pitch is what the program truly sells: a shared period that is far harder to replicate than any slide deck.

## “How lucky we are” and “Regret Not Setting a Goal”

To see what the half‑year leaves on participants, we must listen to their own words.

KT Chu, a 12th‑cohort participant, wrote after the consensus retreat: “How lucky we are to meet each other at the most important moment of our lives!”​[^22] Ken Ho, founder of Tracle Lako and a 11th‑cohort fellow, titled his reflection “Don’t fight solo; fighting as a group gives you a better chance,” and thanked seniors Zhou Daixiang, Chen Chin‑chang, and Ye De‑wei for their guidance during his application​[^23]. These two sentiments dominate alumni testimonials: a sense of belonging among peers and gratitude for senior support.

If only praise were recorded, the narrative would become promotional. The most candid, non‑praise excerpt comes from first‑cohort alumnus Zhou Qinhua, later founder of Tech‑Island‑Read and You‑Wu‑Report. He reflected, “Looking back, I regret not setting a clear goal before applying… If I had a clearer objective, I would have gained more.”​[^24] This is not a negative review; it is an honest self‑reminder that the resources you invest (time, money) yield results proportional to how clearly you define your purpose. Even the best course cannot help someone who does not know what they want.

Among the named alumni who have gone far: Ye De‑wei’s PackAge+ partnered with TSMC on an eco‑circulation box, opened a subsidiary in Singapore, and targets an IPO in 2027; Tsai Ming‑ru’s Yuan‑Chu Bean Shop entered all 7‑Eleven convenience stores nationwide in June 2026; Zheng Bo‑yuan’s Home Chat Room (10th cohort) secured a seed round exceeding NT$10 million and generated NT$5 million monthly revenue​[^25]. It is worth noting that no publicly documented alumni have been confirmed to have failed or shut down, but that may reflect limited media coverage of failures rather than a zero‑failure rate. Successful names are remembered; quiet closures fade away.

## A Name to Clarify: Wu Chia‑jun Is Not an SLP Alumnus

When a community relies on word‑of‑mouth transmission, the easiest mistake is to attribute a name that does not belong. Here is a clarification.

Wu Chia‑jun, co‑founder of iCHEF, is sometimes listed online as an SSLP alumnus, but that is incorrect. He participated in the fourth cohort of the AAMA Taipei Cradle program, a different organization; a thorough check of SLP’s official alumni lists for cohorts 1‑13 finds no mention of “Wu Chia‑jun”​[^26]. The only verified connection is that he was invited to speak at the SLP Taipei 8th‑cohort admission briefing, where he discussed the value of network resources​[^27]. He never claimed SLP as part of his own growth story.

This small correction highlights the landscape of non‑equity startup support in Taiwan. SLP charges a fee, runs a six‑month intensive, and uses a peer‑to‑peer “buddy” system; AAMA Taipei Cradle is a nonprofit, free‑of‑charge, two‑year, one‑on‑one mentorship program that pairs senior entrepreneurs with founders. Both launched around 2012, both take no equity. They exist in parallel worlds: no public comparison exists, and no founder has publicly claimed participation in both. The contrast illustrates that, besides equity‑taking accelerators like AppWorks, Taiwan has cultivated alternative, non‑equity support models.

> **📝 Curator’s note**  
> Looking globally, the SLP Bangalore (India) chapter was founded in 2010—two years before Taipei—offering a four‑month course for roughly INR 35,000 (≈ NT$12,600). Its alumni include Skyroot Aerospace, a leading Indian private‑space company​[^29]. A lower‑cost, shorter‑duration chapter produced the network’s most prominent single case, showing that the true output of these programs is not the companies they “teach” but the people they bring together and ignite.

## Epilogue: No Government, No Equity, Yet Enduring

In 2012 a group of founders self‑funded a class in Taipei. The first‑cohort one‑time fee was NT$6,000, translating to less than NT$100 per hour.

Fourteen years later the same initiative has become an annual, institutionalized community where over a hundred applicants compete for thirty spots and the fee approaches NT$60,000; in 2023 it formally incorporated as an association. The price has risen nearly tenfold, yet the core logic has not moved a single word—founders help founders, they take your time, not your equity, and every dollar is labeled “all used for participants.”

It has never made headlines in international media, and only a handful of major Taiwanese publications have covered it​[^30]. It quietly persists in _INSIDE_, in startup meet‑ups, and on its own YouTube channel with thirteen subscribers, passing from cohort to cohort. Ultimately, it is a slice of Taiwan’s civil‑society mutual aid: without government subsidies or capital‑market financing, a group of people leveraged personal connections and the mantra “fight as a group for a better chance” to turn a modest class into a lasting, hard‑to‑enter institution.

More alumni interviews and course snapshots are available on SLP Taipei’s [official YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@SLPTAIPEI).
