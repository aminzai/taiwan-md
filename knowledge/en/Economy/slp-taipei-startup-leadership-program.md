# SLP Taipei Startup Leadership Program: A Course Starting at NT$6,000, Rising to NT$58,000 While Still Being Hard to Find

In July 2013, a reporter from This Week Magazine did some calculations. At the time, a group in Taipei ran a training camp called the "Startup Leadership Program," charging NT$6,000 for seventeen classes, three of which were full-day workshops. The reporter divided the total hours to arrive at a headline: the cost per hour was less than one hundred dollars[^1].

Today, fourteen years later, the one-time fee is NT$58,000[^2]. With about thirty spots available, the number of applicants has exceeded one hundred every year[^3].

The fee has nearly tenfold increase, yet the demand has not decreased. And the core logic of the organizers has not changed from the first cohort to the present: a group of entrepreneurs running a "entrepreneur-to-entrepreneur" class pro bono, without taking equity.

> **30-Second Overview:** The SLP (Startup Leadership Program) Taipei chapter is the Taipei branch of a global non-profit startup program founded in Boston in 2006, establishing its 22nd chapter globally in 2012[^4]. It offers six months of intensive courses, a two-day/one-night consensus camp, and a one-day Pitch Day, charging a one-time fee without taking equity. This fee rose from NT$6,000 in 2012 to NT$58,000 in 2026, yet the official website has consistently stated that "all funds are used for students"[^2]. Over fourteen years, it has accumulated a group of entrepreneurs across technology, food service, law, and design. The organizers have mostly done this without charge. This is a concrete answer to how civil society in Taiwan can sustain something without relying on government funding or venture capital equity.

## The Fee That Grew from Six Thousand to Fifty-Eight Thousand

First, look clearly at the fee structure because this is the most concrete entry point into SLP Taipei.

In its first cohort in 2012, the one-time fee was NT$6,000. At the time, media outlets described it as a "material fee," but that was the reporter's own phrasing. The official website of SLP has never used the terms "material fee" or separate "tuition fee" from 2012 to the present; it always states "one-time fee" [^5]. This insistence on terminology is a clue: they do not position themselves as a "tutoring center selling courses."

There are large gaps in the data regarding the increases, but the few points that can be gathered draw a curve that steepens later. The first cohort was NT$6,000. Around the eleventh cohort, it reached NT$25,000. The fourteenth cohort (2024) was NT$48,000. The fifteenth cohort (September 2026 to January 2027) is NT$58,000 [^6]. In the ten years from six thousand to twenty-five thousand, and in the subsequent two or three years from twenty-five thousand to nearly sixty thousand. The Taiwanese startup ecosystem itself was transitioning from its nascent stage to a more institutionalized phase during this period; SLP's fee curve is, in a way, a physical scale of that institutionalization process.

What these NT$58,000 buy is clearly stated on the official website: lecturer fees for over twenty courses, venue costs, the two-day/one-night consensus camp, Pitch Day, year-end party, and graduation ceremony are all used for the participants [^2]. There are no offices, and there is no seed funding.

It is worth pausing honestly here. An organization that positions itself as a non-profit entity run by volunteers raised its fee from six thousand to nearly sixty thousand without publicly explaining the reasons for each increase; the statistics presented on the official website are inconsistent across different pages (the homepage states "over 360 graduating entrepreneurs," the association introduction page says "nearly 300," and the 13th cohort acceptance page mentions "over 150") [^7]. These numbers are not large, nor are they consistent, but they are real and verifiable; one can see them by looking at the official website. This is not some external criticism. Very few people in Taiwan's startup community have publicly discussed this matter, and this article does not intend to make a judgment of "contradiction" or "falsity," but merely places the numbers next to its self-positioning, allowing the reader to sit with it for a while.

## No Equity, But We Expect Your Time

That question naturally arose: how can a model survive if it doesn't take equity and isn't a typical incubator, relying only on one-time fees to cover venue and speaker costs?

The answer is contained in a single sentence from the global SLP. This logic is the operating philosophy common to the entire SLP network; Taipei continued this without self-founding. The official website of its India chapter translated this sentence most directly into English: "We take zero equity but we charge them⋯to take care of operational expenses." The site further explains that the program follows a lean, community-driven model where every participant must volunteer twenty hours to help operate it. This mechanism cultivates the Fellows' passion and sense of ownership in the program itself[^8].

In other words, students are both payers and volunteers. The money you pay covers fixed costs; the time you contribute is the real fuel for this community. "Skin in the game" does not refer to shares, but to time and commitment. This explains why core organizers can take nothing, even as the fees increase year after year. INSIDE wrote in 2013 that the executive team of the SLP Taipei chapter participated and organized on a "completely unpaid, voluntary basis"[^9]. Money flows to the students; people flow to the community.

Compared to commercial startup classes, this is a very different path. Some business courses charge seventy or eighty thousand for two full-day sessions with more hype than content, but those are unrelated to SLP and should not be confused with it. The one-time fee for SLP is also not cheap, but it lays bare the destination of every penny and the unpaid status of the organizers. This line—"no equity, but we expect your time"—is how it draws a clear boundary with venture capital accelerators and tutoring schools simultaneously.

## An Organizer Who Later Became a Legislator

If one were to pick the most unexpected name from this group, it would be Ge Ru-jun.

He is known online as "Dr. Bao." He was in charge of the SLP Taipei program during 2013 and 2014. He later stated that his experience running the SLP Taipei program "significantly influenced his admission to Singularity University" [^10]. Singularity University is an institution famous for exponential technology in Silicon Valley. Subsequently, he became a non-partisan legislator for the Kuomintang.

The trajectory—from a young person who voluntarily organized an entrepreneurial training camp, to Singularity University, and then to the Legislative Yuan—is not detailed in any SLP recruitment brochure. It reminds us of something easily overlooked: the output of SLP Taipei is far more than just startups. It is a place that gathers people together, allowing them to refine each other, and these individuals later branch out into all sorts of unforeseen directions. Sometimes, the organizer themselves is the one who changes the most while running a program.

This also leads to an undermentioned background issue regarding SLP Taipei: Who were the people who started it in 2012? The origin of the global SLP itself is clear; it was founded in Boston in 2006 by Anupendra Sharma and his father-in-law, Puran Dang. The first cohort had only seven fellows, lasted twelve months, and focused on life sciences [^12]. However, when the Taipei chapter launched, the official earliest blog only stated that "SLP was founded in 2006 in Boston, a major East Coast city with a flourishing entrepreneurial atmosphere, and by 2012 had expanded to twenty cities globally," without naming the initiators in Taipei [^11]. One of the confirmed core organizers is HsinFu Kuo, a co-founder of SLP Taipei and currently the CEO of the sports technology company bOMDIC; however, his title in the execution team introduction from 2012 to 2013 was "Finance Director at M2 Communication," which is completely different from his current position [^12]. It is not uncommon for the names of the earliest people to become vague after a program runs for over ten years, especially in volunteer-driven organizations without a corporate entity; this is another side of its reliance on "people rather than systems."

## Personal Qualities Account for Half: They Are Selecting People, Not Companies

The selection method used by SLP Taipei embodies its philosophy in the scoring rubric.

The evaluation criteria are broken down as follows: personal qualities 50%, goal planning 10%, learning and failure experiences 10%, leadership and professional achievements 10%, company size 10%, and other factors 10%[^13]. Personal qualities account for half the weight, while company size accounts for only one-tenth. This means they care more about "who you are" than "how big your company is." The admission requirements have no age or industry restrictions; you don't even need to have a company yet.

How many people apply? In the second cohort, there were over 140 applicants, which the organization called the "SLP crown from around the world," with 33 accepted initially and an additional eight admitted[^14]. This results in an acceptance rate of roughly 20%. This creates a counterintuitive picture: fees keep rising, the barrier is "are you the right person," yet over a hundred people compete for thirty spots every year.

The industries of those who are accepted are more diverse than expected. The first cohort in 2012 was composed of 40% internet startups, 40% biotech, and 20% cultural creativity[^1]; by the thirteenth cohort in 2024, the website described the industry types as "software technology, e-commerce retail, interior design, healthcare, law, and marketing" [^15]. There are food businesses like 1997 Oven Pizza, law firms, and teams involved in e-commerce warehousing and logistics. The cultural creativity category accounted for 20% early on, but its prominence seems to have faded later—this drift curve of industry composition is also a side story that hasn't been widely discussed. In the twelfth cohort (2023), there were simultaneously founders doing brand research, those in warehousing and logistics, and a line-up of entrepreneurs from various fields: KT Chu Kai-ti, Chang He-sheang of JENJAN Zhenzhan, Wu Zheyu, and about thirty other founders listed together[^16]. The selection logic that places half the weight on personal qualities results in this wide and diverse group.

> **📝 Curator's Notes**
>
> A scoring sheet that places "personal qualities" at 50% is, in fact, quietly admitting one thing: this course doesn't just sell knowledge. The content of the twenty sessions (business model deconstruction, fundraising simulation, presentation training) can be found online or in bookstores without needing to spend NT$58,000. What is truly scarce and what people are paying for is the six months spent being observed by a whole line of alumni—the experience of being accepted by thirty others who are also striving. They are not choosing those who "can be taught," but those who are "worthy of recognition by this group."

## Alumni Teaching Back

After spending time at SLP Taipei, one notices a cycle: the person lecturing on stage today might have been sitting in the audience yesterday.

Casper Chen (陳欽章) was an alumnus of the fourth cohort and later became an instructor for multiple courses, teaching organizational growth and team leadership[^17]. Boz Lu (盧永偉), an alumnus of SLP, returned to lead brand workshops. This tradition of "alumni mentoring current students" is not accidental; it has been institutionalized into a mechanism called Founder Buddy: every student in the fifteenth cohort is paired with a Buddy from the fourteenth cohort, meeting once every two months[^18]. It is a horizontal, peer-to-peer transmission across generations.

The mentors' side is surprisingly stable. Amber Chang (張毓純) of Xizi Technology has been on the mentor roster since the first cohort in 2012[^19] and continues to be involved. The group of professional mentors comes from consulting firms like Boston Consulting Group and PwC, as well as venture capital firms such as China Development Capital, PSS, ETP, and Huahui[^20]. An alumnus might become an instructor for the next cohort after graduation, and a mentor might stick with the program for over ten years. This human resource structure requires almost no cost, relying instead on the same group of people being willing to return again and again. This perfectly aligns with the concept of "skin in the game": those who invest their time later become part of the program itself.

The course pace revolves around three high-emotional intensity events. The Consensus Camp is a unique icebreaker activity at SLP, lasting two days and one night. Many student testimonials mention the moment of letting down their guard and getting to know each other in the hotel conference room[^21]; this is followed by over twenty intensive classes over six months, covering topics from Business Model Canvas (BMC) deconstruction and fundraising simulations to presentation training; finally, there is Pitch Day, which features a three-minute Founder Pitch for everyone in the morning and booth exhibitions in the afternoon. This event was formerly called "Demo Day" but has been uniformly renamed Pitch Day in recent years. It is worth noting that Pitch Day does not feature prize money, judges, or ranking mechanisms; it is simply an exhibition where six months of work is showcased. The intensive six months, the tears from the Consensus Camp, and the three minutes on Pitch Day—when combined—are what this course truly sells: the time spent binding a group together is difficult to replicate with any amount of knowledge found in a slide deck.

## How Fortunate, and "Regretting Not Setting Goals"

To see what this group has left in people over the past six months, we must listen to the students themselves.

KT Chu Kai-teh from the 12th cohort wrote after the Consensus Camp: "We are so fortunate to have met each other at such an important time in our lives!"[^22] Ken Ho (何洛) from the 11th cohort, founder of Tracle Laka, summarized his experience in one sentence: "Entrepreneurship shouldn't be a solo battle; it has more chances of success when fighting as a group." He also specifically thanked senior students Chou Dai-xiang, Chen Qin-zhang, and Ye De-wei for their guidance during his application and selection process[^23]. These are the two most common sentiments in SLP student testimonials: the sense of belonging within a like-minded community, and gratitude that "the seniors were truly helpful."

But if the collective portrait only contains praise, it becomes a promotional photo. The closest firsthand account we have that is not purely laudatory comes from Chu Chin-hua (周欽華) of the first cohort, who later started reading in Taiwan and reported on his progress. Reflecting back, he said: "Looking back now, I regret not setting a clear goal before applying... If I had a clearer goal back then... I would have gained more."[^24] This statement is hardly negative criticism; it is more like an honest self-reminder: the resources you invest yourself with, and how clearly you enter with a purpose, largely determines how much you take out. Even the best course cannot sustain someone who doesn't know what they want. Placing this sentiment into the collective portrait makes the group three-dimensional—some were moved to tears at the Consensus Camp, while others only realized later that they hadn't utilized it well.

As for which members went the farthest, there are a few named signals. Ye De-wei’s PackAge+ partnered with Acer (9th cohort) on eco-friendly recycling bins and established a subsidiary in Singapore, aiming for an IPO by 2027; Tsai Ming-ru's Yuanchu Bean Workshop reached all FamilyMart convenience stores across Taiwan in June 2026; and Cheng Bo-yuan’s home studio (10th cohort) secured over ten million in seed funding with monthly revenue of five million[^25]. We must honestly add that we could not find any named alumni cases of clear bankruptcy or failure, but this may simply be because the media rarely reports failures, not that there is a zero failure rate. Successful names are remembered; those who fold usually disappear quietly.

## A Name to Clarify: Wu Jiajun is Not an SLP Alumnus

In social communities that rely on word-of-mouth, the easiest mistake to make is including names that do not belong. We must clarify one point here.

Wu Jiajun, a co-founder of iCHEF, is occasionally listed online as an SLP alumnus, which is incorrect. A meticulous check reveals that he is an entrepreneur from the fourth cohort of the AAMA Taipei Cradle Program, which is a separate organization; searching through the complete list of alumni for SLP from the first to the thirteenth cohorts on the official website yields no mention of "Wu Jiajun" [^26]. His only confirmed connection to SLP was being invited to speak at the eighth cohort recruitment briefing, discussing general network value—"these are all critical issues, so it is better to know them early, and all the networking resources within this community are important assets" [^27]. He spoke about the community and networks, not the course itself; iCHEF's own growth story makes no mention of SLP.

This small correction brings up the landscape of non-equity startup support in Taiwan. SLP involves tuition fees, an intensive six-month curriculum, and horizontal mentorship from senior to junior members; AAMA Taipei Cradle Program is charitable and free, offering two years of one-on-one mentoring with seasoned entrepreneurs—both established around 2012 and do not take equity. Interestingly, they are like two parallel worlds: a search through public records reveals no report comparing the two, nor any entrepreneur publicly stating that they applied to both; even media coverage has been distinctly separate [^28]. They are not in a competition where one is better than the other; rather, they represent two answers that emerged from Taiwan's question of "what other startup support models exist besides equity-based accelerators (like AppWorks)."

> **📝 Curator's Note**
>
> Looking globally reveals an even more counterintuitive fact. The SLP Bangalore chapter in India was established in 2010, two years before Taipei, with a four-month course and a fee of about NT$12,000, far lower than the current NT$58,000 in Taipei—yet one of its alumni is Skyroot Aerospace, a leader in Indian private aerospace [^29]. The chapter with lower fees and shorter duration produced the most prominent single case in the network. This shows one thing: the real output of these types of programs is not companies "taught" by the course, but the people who are screened and ignited by that process. That cannot be bought with a price tag.

## Epilogue: Doing Something Long-Term Without Relying on Government or Equity

In 2012, a group of people who were themselves entrepreneurs held a course in Taipei out of pocket and pro bono. The first session cost six thousand NTD for a one-time fee, which equates to less than one hundred NTD per hour.

Fourteen years later, the same endeavor evolved into an institutionalized community event held annually, where over 100 people compete for thirty spots at a price nearing sixty thousand NTD in 2023. It was officially established as an association that year. The price has increased nearly tenfold, yet the core logic of its organization remains unchanged—entrepreneurs helping entrepreneurs; they charge your time but not your equity, and every penny is designated "entirely for the members."

It never made it onto international media, nor was it frequently featured in major publications like business magazines or _Tianxia_ [^30]; instead, it quietly spread through INSIDE, among startup gatherings, and on its own YouTube channel with thirteen subscribers, generation after generation. Ultimately, this is a slice of mutual aid within Taiwan: how a group managed to grow and sustain a small endeavor into a hard-to-find entity after fourteen years, relying not on government subsidies or capital markets, but on personal connections and the belief that "only by forming groups can we succeed."

More interviews with alumni and course snippets are collected on the SLP Taipei [official YouTube channel](https://www.youtube.com/@SLPTAIPEI).
