In October 1999, when the first onshore wind turbine spun in Hukou Township, Penghu, green energy imagination in Taiwan was still limited to scattered small-scale experiments. More than two decades later, the winter northeast monsoon of the Taiwan Strait is no longer just a fear spoken by fishermen; it has become the world's most technologically challenging and densely developed natural testing ground for offshore wind power. As of the end of 2025, Taiwan's cumulative installed capacity of offshore wind has officially crossed the threshold of 4.4GW, with green energy investment exceeding $3 billion [^2]. The island has erected over five hundred giant turbines on the sea at an astonishing pace [^8], but it has also collided with multiple waves: geological limits, restructuring of local supply chains, fishing rights conflicts, and geopolitical tensions.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:**
> Many people think offshore wind is just moving onshore windmills into the sea. But in the Taiwan Strait, this is a tightrope walk of engineering—here are the world's most severe typhoon threats, complex seabed geology, and the massive demand for clean power from global tech titans.

## The Test of Gale and Deep Water: The World's Most Severe Marine Engineering

To erect an offshore turbine, which can be hundreds of meters high, in the Taiwan Strait presents engineers with nature's harshest challenges. Although the average water depth of the Taiwan Strait is not extreme, the soft sandy seabed, frequent seismic activity, and intense typhoons that strike during the transition from summer to autumn prevent a direct replication of mature wind farm construction experiences from Europe and America.

"The technical challenges we face in deep water and complex geology far exceed those experienced in early European North Sea projects," recalled a senior project manager involved in offshore wind engineering. "Every underwater foundation piling must be precisely calculated during periods of poor sea conditions; even the slightest mistake can leave the entire construction vessel and equipment stranded."

To overcome these challenges, Taiwan's offshore wind projects have gradually advanced toward deeper waters. This required not only greater national policy support but also fundamentally changed the technical boundaries of Taiwanese marine engineering [^6]. From early exploratory demonstration farms to large-scale deployment in zone development phases, the Taiwan Strait has become a stress test site for global marine engineering technology in just a few years.

### The Next Battlefield Towards the Deep Sea: Floating Wind Technology Frontiers

As fixed subsea foundation development approaches the physical limits of water depth and geology, Taiwan's offshore wind is being forced to enter a new unknown territory—Floating Offshore Wind. Unlike traditional turbines fixed to the seabed, floating wind uses massive platforms and deep-sea mooring lines to anchor the turbine on the sea surface, allowing it to harness stronger, more stable deep-sea winds in waters deeper than 50 meters. However, this also poses brand new challenges for Taiwanese marine engineering, mooring system design, and future port infrastructure, testing whether the government and developers can achieve a balance between cost and cutting-edge technology [^6].

### Offshore Wind and Taiwan's Energy Security

In an era of high geopolitical tension globally, offshore wind is not just a decarbonization tool; it is part of national security. With over 97% of its energy reliant on imports, the lifeline of fossil fuels is easily susceptible to blockade or interference in extreme circumstances. Hundreds of giant turbines distributed off the western coast are less like power generation facilities and more like a distributed marine power grid.

However, this decentralized structure also brings new challenges for security and defense. How to protect these critical offshore infrastructures located on the sea surface during wartime, and ensure the resilience of the transmission network, has become an unavoidable strategic consideration for national security agencies and energy departments as they pursue the 13GW target by 2030 [^7].

## The Turning Point of Domestic Content Policy: From Protection to the International Track

While pursuing green energy transition, Taiwan has also ambitiously attempted to establish a complete local supply chain (Local Content), hoping that policy protection would allow domestic Taiwanese manufacturers direct entry into the red track of the global offshore wind industry. However, this multi-year localization experiment immediately faced severe tests in cost, delivery time, and international competitiveness.

"In 2019, the word 'domestic content' was almost the core focus at the negotiation table between all wind farm developers and the Ministry of Economic Affairs," noted an industry observer. "But when the global supply chain faced inflation and soaring raw material prices, overly rigid local procurement regulations caused delays in some projects."

Faced with real challenges, the Ministry of Economic Affairs made key adjustments to domestic content policies for zone development around 2025, replacing hard regulations with a more flexible ESG and performance management mechanism, forcing the local supply chain to prove itself directly through international market price and quality tests [^4] [^5]. This policy shift is not just a regulatory correction; it is a painful but necessary rite of passage: Taiwanese manufacturing must learn to compete head-on with top global suppliers without a protective umbrella.

### The Bidding Game of Zone Development and Electricity Price Tug-of-War

As Taiwan's offshore wind entered Phase II of zone development (R3.2) and subsequent draft selection adjustments, the core contradiction in the market shifted from "Can it be built?" to "Who will bear the high construction costs?" International developers have repeatedly expressed concerns about returns when facing a global high-interest rate environment, rising financing costs, and the tug-of-war between Taiwan's Feed-in Tariff (FiT) and Corporate Power Purchase Agreement (CPPA) mechanisms. Some foreign companies even slowed down investment steps during policy transitions, forcing the government to walk a tightrope between "keeping clean energy prices affordable" and "attracting sustained international capital." This evolution of the selection mechanism not only determined the financial fate of the wind farms but also directly influenced the cost curve for domestic high-tech industries to obtain affordable clean power [^2].

## Dialogue with Fishing Industry and Marine Areas: The Symbiotic Path of Fishermen and Giant Turbines

Offshore wind is not just cold steel structures and generation figures; it is directly embedded in the lifeblood of fishermen on Taiwan's west coast for centuries. Offshore areas near Changhua, Yunlin, and Miaoli have always been major centers for coastal fishing in Taiwan. When giant turbines rise from the sea, fishermen's first concern is: Where is my livelihood?

During the process of early demonstration farms promoted by Taipower, communication with local fishing associations in places like Changhua involved countless rounds of negotiation and consultation [^3]. From initial resistance and questioning to subsequent seminars and site visits, both sides gradually explored the possibility of "fishery symbiosis." Some wind farms have begun attempting to promote marine ranching and ecological restoration around the turbines, turning previously quiet areas due to fishing bans into refuges for marine life.

However, conflict and dialogue are not completely over. Finding a sustainable balance between green energy development, local fishing rights, and marine ecosystem protection remains a social cost that Taiwan must continuously face as it moves toward its 2030 target (projected at 13GW) [^6].

### Retirement and Circular Economy After Twenty Years of Service

When the first batch of offshore turbines has been operating in the Taiwan Strait for over ten years, another hidden challenge is emerging—the life cycle management and decommissioning of the turbines. The design lifespan of offshore turbines is typically 20 to 25 years, and the giant blades are often made from composite materials (such as fiberglass and resin), making natural decomposition or traditional recycling extremely difficult.

While Taiwan is accelerating wind farm construction, the handling of massive blade waste in the future, or the dismantling/on-site reuse of subsea foundations, is still in the early stages of legal and technical establishment. Establishing a local green energy circular economy will be the next long-term test for Taiwan's offshore wind, following power generation volume.

## Conclusion: The Island's Destiny Whispered by the Sea Wind

Looking out from the coastlines of Taichung or Changhua, the offshore turbines in the setting sun appear like a row of silent giant sentinels, spinning quietly. They not only continuously supply critical clean energy needed by Taiwan and its high-tech industries but also witness the survival wisdom of an island nation facing the dual squeeze of global climate change and geopolitical tensions.

The story of Taiwan's offshore wind is far from over—from initial exploration and policy collisions to moving into deeper seas. This is not just a story of energy transition; it is a contemporary marine epic about courage, compromise, and transformation.
