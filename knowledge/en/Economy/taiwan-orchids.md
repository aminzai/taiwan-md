## Flowers Don't Fly Directly from Greenhouses to Florists

In 1997, Taiwanese Phalaenopsis first achieved export success with an export value of US$3.533 million. By 2011, this figure had grown more than twentyfold to US$98.474 million. This is historical data used by the Ministry of Agriculture in reviewing the industry, not a current production forecast, but it illustrates a turning point: Phalaenopsis transformed from a rare plant exchanged among enthusiasts into an export commodity that required management, division of labor, and transportation.[^1]

The story of Taiwan did not start with one massive farm. A 2009 study using Tainan as an example pointed out that early orchid cultivation was largely small-scale, high-priced, and scattered among households. After the industry took shape, Tainan gradually became a major production area due to its climate, sunlight, land, transportation, and orchid enthusiast networks. Researchers described this transformation as moving from hobbyist orchids to agricultural industrialization, rather than simply an increase in planting area.[^6]

**Curator's Note:** The most memorable contrast in the orchid industry is that while it looks like a flower story, it is actually a supply chain story. The flower is only the last part seen; what truly determines whether it can leave Taiwan are the less romantic systems and technologies.

## Native to Taiwan Does Not Mean Automatically Leading in Taiwan

Taiwan has two native Phalaenopsis species, and the Orchidaceae family contains over four hundred species. The native habitat of the white-flowered Phalaenopsis in Taiwan is seasonal rainforests below 800 meters in Hengchun, Dawu, and Lan Ta. However, the Phalaenopsis widely circulated today is not something dug up from the wild; it is the result of long-term selection, propagation, and cultivation management.[^2]

During the Japanese colonial period, foreign orchid species were introduced to Taiwan, leading to exchanges and breeding among enthusiasts. The trend of exotic orchid cultivation emerged in the 1920s, and white-flowered Phalaenopsis from Taiwan won awards at flower exhibitions in the United States in 1952 and 1953, and again in Nantes, France, in 1956. These events established Phalaenopsis as a recognizable Taiwanese flower and helped elevate orchid culture from a personal hobby to public exhibition and marketability.[^2]

But there is another side to the same history. Professional articles record that wild orchids were once threatened with extinction due to increased collection demands. The Convention on International Trade in Endangered Species of Wild Fauna and Flora (CITES) began regulating wildlife trade in 1975, and Taiwan subsequently established systems for plant seedlings and variety rights.[^2] Industrialization was not a license to infinitely expand natural resources; it required separating wild conservation from artificial propagation.[^2]

## Lee, TAITANG, and the Industrialization Inside a Greenhouse

Starting in the 1970s, Professor Lee at National Taiwan University's Horticulture Department dedicated himself to the physiology of Phalaenopsis cultivation, flowering regulation, and tissue culture. In 1987, TAITANG established an advanced agriculture division focused on the Phalaenopsis industry. These two events are often separated into academic and corporate lines, but for the industry, they jointly addressed the same problem: how to make flowers not just bloom beautifully, but also be produced stably.[^2]

TAITANG's own 2017 review provides a more concrete corporate context. At the end of the 1970s (ROC calendar), when the sugar business needed to transform and the government promoted advanced agriculture, TAITANG proposed an export investment plan for Phalaenopsis and ornamental plants. In 1982, Phalaenopsis was successfully trialed at the Xihu Sugar Factory. After the investment plan was approved, TAITANG purchased Dutch controlled-environment greenhouses and later developed its own automated greenhouses that were lower in cost and better suited to the Taiwanese environment.[^3]

There is a detail often omitted here: TAITANG did not just buy equipment. Official reviews state that the Sugar Research Institute also established a horticulture department, creating a germplasm bank and investing in breeding, propagation, cultivation technology, preservatives, and greenhouse automation control. In other words, the greenhouse was not just a building, but a knowledge system that standardized light, temperature, water, seedling production, and shipping processes.[^3]

**Curator's Note:** TAITANG's role was not simply turning a state-owned enterprise into a flower farm; it was translating a hobby that heavily relied on individual experience into a production language that could be trained, replicated, mass-produced, and handed over.

## The Real Factory Might Be a Group of Trusting Small Farms

The industrialization of Taiwanese Phalaenopsis did not concentrate all production in one company. Research from Airiti noted that large agricultural enterprises like TAITANG could achieve vertical integration, but most private farms remained small to medium-sized operators. They formed an industry chain through vertical division of labor, spanning tissue culture, seedling production, blooming induction, and sales.[^6]

The research abstract also described the relationship between core farms and satellite farms. Core farms focused on export controlled varieties, seedling production, or overseas channels, while nearby satellite farms handled subcontracting and production stages. This cooperation was not always maintained by complex contracts; the accumulated credit, technology, and delivery records from long-term partnerships were often more important than geographical distance.[^6]

English academic research offers a similar but sharper description of this phenomenon: the Taiwanese orchid industry is composed of numerous micro, small, and medium enterprises that compete with and complement each other. Government promotion staff continuously held cultivation courses to keep the corporate network updated in varieties and technology. Researchers therefore argue that Taiwan's advantage is not just its subtropical climate, because in modern greenhouses, climatic advantages can largely be replaced by technology.[^7]

This also explains why Tainan is not just a place where "many orchids are grown." It is more like a decentralized factory: some do tissue culture, some produce seedlings, some induce blooming, and some manage exports. No single farm needs to be the largest; the entire network must be able to hand over on time.

## A Technical Negotiation of Cross-Ocean Transport

Phalaenopsis cannot survive solely by expensive air freight. The Ministry of Agriculture records that before 2005, Phalaenopsis shipped to the US was often processed as bare roots via air transport, with a survival rate upon arrival between seventy and ninety percent, and recovery periods lasting up to two months; transportation and relay cultivation costs were high.[^1]

In July 1994, Taiwan and the United States signed a plan for cultivating media plants accompanying exports to the US. In January 2005, a Taiwanese orchid farm first exported orchids with culture medium. Subsequently, operators conducted sea freight trials and preservation technology tests, allowing Phalaenopsis to travel longer sea routes with water-based media. The Ministry of Agriculture records that current sea transport to the US using culture media achieves a survival rate exceeding ninety percent after more than twenty days of transit, with a recovery period of about three weeks. A forty-foot container can transport 20,000 large seedlings at once.[^1]

The protagonist in this shift was not a single variety, but the combined change in phytosanitary regulations, preservation technology, packaging methods, container capacity, and overseas relay cultivation. What Taiwan exported was often not a blooming flowerpot, but rather the handover to another country's greenhouse to induce the flower at the correct time.

**Curator's Note:** The true internationalization of a Phalaenopsis is not when it is photographed for postcards, but when it can survive over twenty days in a shipping container and still be cared for by another group upon arrival.

## Flower Intellectual Property Grows Beyond Petals

New varieties of Phalaenopsis are not achieved in one trial. The Ministry of Agriculture points out that breeding can take six to eight years, and both breeders and seedling companies bear high costs and risks. Therefore, variety rights became part of the industry's global layout.[^5]

Variety rights follow territorial principles: infringement must be applied for and protected in the country where it occurs to form a basis for litigation or trade restrictions. For Taiwanese orchid operators, developing a new variety is only the first step. They must also determine the market location, who will propagate it, when it will bloom, and in which countries rights must be claimed.[^5]

In February 2026, the European Community Plant Variety Office (CPVO) announced expanded plant variety protection cooperation for Phalaenopsis and Cymbidium orchids between Taiwan and the EU, including _Cymbidium_ in the list of species eligible for simplified procedures. The announcement stated that the EU would accept DUS test reports for _Cymbidium_ issued by Taiwanese authorities, meaning breeders did not need to repeat identical technical trials in the EU. Plant variety rights cooperation between Taiwan and Europe began in 2016, with mutual recognition agreements for Phalaenopsis established in 2019 and renewed in 2023.[^4]

The cost of this system can also be quantified. In a 2024 announcement regarding Japan-Taiwan cooperation, the Ministry of Agriculture stated that as of November 2024, Taiwan had secured 17 foreign variety rights through international cooperation mechanisms. The same announcement noted that plant variety protection often takes two to three years from application to acquisition. This shows that variety rights are not a legal footnote at the end of an article, but an export infrastructure that breeders must invest in advance.[^8]

DUS stands for Distinctness, Uniformity, and Stability. These technical terms sound far removed from flowers, yet they are the language that Taiwan's orchids must be understood by another system when entering the global market. From breeding to export, what Taiwan sells is not just a flower color, but also proof recognizable in different jurisdictions.

## From Taiwanese Flower to Taiwanese System

Looking back, the story of Taiwanese orchids involves at least three translations. The first was enthusiasts bringing wild and overseas germplasm into cultivation culture, turning Phalaenopsis from a collectible into an exchangeable variety. The second was Lee, Kang, TAITANG, and private farms translating cultivation experience into greenhouses, tissue culture, and division of labor. The third was operators and the government integrating phytosanitary inspection, sea freight, variety rights, and DUS reports into the international market. These three translations turned a flower into export agriculture.

Therefore, the core of Taiwanese orchids is not the convenient explanation "Taiwan has good climate." English academic research has already pointed out that modern greenhouses can substitute for a considerable part of climatic advantages. What is truly difficult to replicate is the production network accumulated through long-term cooperation among small and medium enterprises, government promotion, and research institutions.[^7]

The Ministry of Agriculture's industry review shows that the export growth from 1997 to 2011 was related to media shipment to the US, long-distance sea freight, and preservation technology. However, these historical figures should not be written as a guarantee of perpetual industrial ascent. In 2024, the Ministry of Agriculture also recorded the signing of an MOU on plant variety testing reports between Taiwan and Japan, noting that variety rights review often takes two to three years or more, and mutual acceptance of testing reports saves breeders time and cost in applying for foreign rights.[^1] [^5] [^8]

**Curator's Note:** The so-called "Taiwanese light," if only measured by export rankings, misses the most important part: the light does not come from the petals alone, but is illuminated by research, factories, farmers, regulations, and international cooperation.

## Conclusion: A Flower Still Being Redefined

Today, when buying a pot of Phalaenopsis, one might only see the neatly arranged flowers on the stem. But behind that flower may be years of breeding, tissue culture and seedling production in Tainan, cultivation conditions repeatedly calibrated in a greenhouse, and documents left by customs and variety testing systems.

What is most interesting about Taiwanese orchids is that they retain two identities simultaneously: they are a culture born from enthusiast hobbies, and an industry that operates through standardization, transportation, preservation, and intellectual property rights. It appears soft, yet it requires an entire set of very rigid systems to live and arrive in the world.

The next time the blooming season arrives, perhaps one should ask a different question: instead of asking what color this flower is, one should first ask whose hands, which greenhouses, and which rules allowed it to bloom today.
