> **30-Second Overview:** In 1975, the Kaohsiung shipyard (CSBC) completed construction ahead of schedule. Fish ponds, sugarcane fields, and coastal shallow waters were converted into a 1 million-ton dry dock, and shipbuilding evolved from a single factory into an industry connecting steel, machinery, electrical systems, and ports.[^1] [^2] This large-scale industry faced restructuring in 2001, which involved layoffs and wage cuts to secure continued operation with public funds.[^3] After 2016, Taiwan's shipbuilding capabilities were extended to offshore wind marine engineering. In 2020, the Kaohsiung shipyard also undertook the commencement of a submarine domestic construction project.[^4] [^13] The focus of Taiwan's shipbuilding industry is not just what ships it builds, but whether it can keep equipment, workers, supply chains, and national needs connected across different eras.

## Why a Shipyard Started with Shallow Bays

Shipbuilding is often simplistically thought of as "welding steel plates into ships." The CSBC shipyard in Xiaogang, Kaohsiung, reminds us that the truly difficult first step was not welding, but creating a space where many professions could work simultaneously. Local publications from the Kaohsiung City Government record that the government targeted Kaohsiung Port as a site for large-scale shipyards in the 1960s, establishing the Kaohsiung Shipyard Preparatory Office in 1972, founding China Shipbuilding Corporation in 1973, and officially commencing construction in 1974. The original site was fish ponds, sugarcane fields, and coastal shallow waters.[^1]

This site selection was not merely replacing agricultural land with industrial land. It simultaneously integrated the dry dock, port, steel industry, and transportation into a single coastal location. A report from _Taiwan Guanghua_ in 1975 described the hull factory at 750 meters long and 210 meters wide, the dry dock at 950 meters long and 92 meters wide with an average depth of 14 meters, achieving a capacity of 1 million tons. Two 350-ton cranes spanned the dry dock, and there were over seventy other types of cranes in the factory.[^5]

This scale holds a meaning often obscured by the four words "Ten Great Constructions": it reordered weight, distance, and waiting time. Steel plates could be processed first at the hull factory; piping, machinery, electrical equipment, and interior fitting could be prefabricated separately before being integrated back into the dry dock. Contemporary reports stated that shipbuilding was an "integrated industry," driving precision, steel, machinery, and electrical industries.[^5] Therefore, the shipyard was not an isolated factory but an industrial system comprising suppliers, engineers, cranes, dry docks, and deep-water ports working in a single rhythm.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The story of the Kaohsiung shipyard is more than just "Taiwan can build large ships." What is more worth noting is that industrialization here was realized as a spatial problem. The shallow bay first had to become ground capable of bearing immense weight, and different trades were then arranged according to the hull manufacturing sequence. Taiwan is often remembered for exporting containers, wafers, and electronic products. The shipyard presented another capability: breaking down massive, slow objects into manageable processes and reassembling them into ships ready for port departure. When curating, the 750-meter hull factory, the 950-meter dry dock, the cranes, and the prefabrication processes must be placed in a single narrative so that readers can see industrialization not as a slogan, but as an order formed by ground, equipment, and workers.[^5]

## World-Class Scale Does Not Guarantee Market Patience

In August 1975, CSBC began constructing its first new ship: a 445,000 DWT oil tanker. The original report in _Guanghua_ stated that this oil tanker was 378.4 meters long and 68 meters wide, requiring over 60,000 tons of steel. CSBC planned an annual shipbuilding capacity of 1.5 million DWT and ship repair capacity of 2.5 million gross tons.[^5] These figures indicate that the shipyard was designed as a national industrial base, not merely a repair facility for local fishing vessels.

The history of Taiwan's shipbuilding companies is even older. The National Cultural Memory Bank records that the Taiwan Shipyard Company was reorganized in 1948, originating from the merger of Taiwan Railway Works Co., Ltd. (established in 1946 by Taiwan Ship Canal and Kaohsiung Companies) and Taiwan Machinery Shipbuilding Co., Ltd. After its establishment, the company leased Japanese reparations machinery and expanded equipment, capable of building vessels under 2,000 tons. It was reorganized into China Shipbuilding Corporation in 1978 and renamed Taiwan International Shipbuilding Company in 2007.[^6]

This trajectory places two timelines for Taiwan's shipbuilding industry together: one is the period of post-war equipment acquisition, repair, and gradual learning; the other is the rapid expansion with large dry docks and massive cranes in the 1970s. These were not natural evolutions but results driven by national investment, technology transfer, shipping demand, and industrial policy. A researcher from the History Department at National Cheng Kung University also pointed out in an article in _Tech Grand Garden_ that during the period of "national ship construction" (國艦國造) from the 1970s to the 1990s, CSBC handled most naval construction projects, but early on still had to purchase prototype ships, designs, or related technologies from foreign companies and send personnel for training.[^8]

Thus, "domestic production" (國造) was never a state achieved by pressing a button. It involved processes of imitation, cooperation, failure, and gradually acquiring design capabilities. That article also noted that after the 1990s, private shipbuilding faced pressure from the fishing vessel replacement policy, beginning to expand from fishing boats and workboats to warships, with the Guanghua No. 3 project in 1994 being built by a private joint venture company, demonstrating that ship construction capabilities were no longer solely concentrated in state-owned large shipyards.[^8]

## "Taiwan International Shipbuilding" Begins with Downsizing

On December 31, 2001, CSBC launched a revitalization plan. An official press release from the Presidential Office stated that the company first laid off nearly half of its employees and reduced wages by 35% for those remaining. The government injected 3 billion NTD in capital, and the Executive Yuan Development Fund reallocated another 3 billion NTD as low-interest loans to assist the company in resuming operations.[^3]

This data should not only be written as "the government saving a state-owned enterprise." It also reveals two sides of large industry: dry docks and equipment can persist, but orders, costs, organizational efficiency, and industrial competition do not automatically stabilize just because the factory is huge. The fixed costs of shipbuilding are high, and the market is affected by shipping trends, financial crises, defense budgets, and international competition. When a shipyard needs to survive, the cost first falls on the number and wages of workers.

On March 3, 2007, Taiwan International Shipbuilding Company was inaugurated in Kaohsiung. The Presidential Office press release linked the "CSBC Revitalization" of 2001 with the "TIS New Beginning" of 2007 as an official narrative, but the same document also retained the figures from the restructuring.[^3] This juxtaposition is more honest than simply saying "rising from failure": the company name can restart, but the working conditions of the people and the cost of public funds cannot be erased from history.

## The Same Shipyard Also Undertakes National Defense Autonomy

On November 24, 2020, the Presidential Office reported on the commencement ceremony of the submarine domestic construction project at TIS in Kaohsiung. The official article reviewed that this project had undergone over four years of preparation before officially commencing, including budget allocation, contract signing, design, and factory preparation, listing TIS, the Academia Sinica, and the Navy as jointly involved parties.[^13] This data fills a gap often overlooked in shipbuilding history: the capabilities of the Kaohsiung shipyard were not only serving civil vessels, repairs, and offshore wind but were also integrated into long-term national defense projects.

Scale must be maintained here. The Presidential Office article records the commencement, preparation work, and the political goal of national defense autonomy, which does not mean all technical achievements are complete. For Taiwan.md, the value of the photo is not to present the factory as a "proof of success," but to allow readers to see how defense industry relies on factories, sections, design, supply chains, and long-term organizational capacity. This is similar to the issue of building dry docks in the 1970s, with the difference being that today's projects carry higher expectations for agility and national security.

## From Shipbuilding to Marine Engineering: The Market Changed, Not the Sea

Taiwan International Shipbuilding's recent shift was not abandoning shipbuilding, but extending shipbuilding capabilities beyond the ship itself. Official TIS data indicates that in November 2016, TIS convened domestic industry, academia, and research units to form the M-Team for offshore wind marine engineering, with cooperation goals including offshore wind marine engineering technology, business collaboration, and marine engineering vessel construction. The Taiwan Offshore Wind Turbine Foundation and Marine Engineering Association was established on January 27, 2018.[^4]

Industry data from the National Development Council breaks down this shift even more clearly: offshore wind is not just turbines; it also includes subsea foundations, onshore power facilities, installation, transportation, operation, and marine vessel manufacturing. Vessel manufacturing is classified under ship and floating facility manufacturing. The page also lists companies like CSBC, CSC (China Steel Corporation), CSC Machinery, TIS, TIS Anti-corrosion, and others in the Kaohsiung supply chain.[^7] Shipbuilding here reemerges as an intermediary industry connecting steel structure, welding, anti-corrosion, shipping, and construction.

This does not mean Taiwan has completed a flawless green energy transition. Official data describes industrial scope and supply chain configuration, not that all businesses are profitable or every technology is fully autonomous. TIS's own materials use phrases like "promoting localization" and "establishing independent technological and construction capabilities," which precisely indicate that this is an ongoing process, not a concluded achievement.[^4]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The Kaohsiung shipyard in the 1970s turned fish ponds, sugarcane fields, and shallow bays into dry docks. Marine engineering after 2016 brought welding, heavy lifting, ship design, and offshore construction capabilities to wind farms. The submarine domestic construction commencement in 2020 forced the same shipyard to face national defense autonomy, sensitive information, and long-term project management.[^4] [^13] When curating, one cannot only display photos of completed ships, turbines, or factories; the data from layoffs, wage cuts, and public funds during restructuring must also be retained. Industrial transformation is not merely swapping a popular buzzword; it requires asking whether the workers, machinery, and organizations left over from the previous era can truly be carried into the next.

## What the Shipyard Left Behind Is Not a Victory Photo

From the Taiwan Shipyard Company in 1948, to the CSBC Kaohsiung shipyard in the 1970s, and to TIS in 2007, Taiwan's shipbuilding industry has not followed a single path. It first acquired equipment and technology, then used national plans to build massive dry docks. It demonstrated industrial capability with large oil tankers and naval vessels, and it also secured continued operation through layoffs, wage cuts, and public funds during restructuring. Today, it extends ship manufacturing into offshore wind and marine engineering.

The significance of this history is not in proving that Taiwan "can build anything." A more accurate statement is: Taiwan has practiced—and continues to practice—how to keep an industry on the island that requires steel, ports, design, welding, electrical systems, shipping, and national policy working together. Ships are launched, but what shipbuilding truly leaves behind is an organizational capability capable of dealing with massive objects, long-cycle investments, and repeated transformations. This capability deserves to be remembered, and it deserves to be questioned as to who bore the cost and for whom it ultimately served.
