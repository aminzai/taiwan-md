# Taiwan's Startup Ecosystem

> **30-second overview:** Taiwan's startup ecosystem has developed rapidly in recent years, moving from early challenges of insufficient venture capital to today's mature accelerators, venture capital firms, and government support systems. AppWorks (init.ai) manages over $38.6 million in funds and has mentored more than 600 startups; Gogoro went public via SPAC merger on NASDAQ in 2022, and Appier listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange in 2021, both serving as benchmark cases for Taiwanese startups going global. Leveraging semiconductors, ICT, and Southeast Asia as bridges, Taiwan is carving out its own position on the global startup map.

## Why It Matters

The health of a startup ecosystem reflects an economy's innovation vitality. Taiwan's manufacturing sector has faced transformational pressures for decades, and industries beyond semiconductors need new growth engines, with startups being one path forward.

Under the global wave of digitization, Taiwan's deep ICT foundation and complete hardware supply chain give local startups natural advantages in IoT, AI chip applications, and smart manufacturing. The startup ecosystem also offers young people an alternative career path different from traditional large corporations, changing Taiwan's employment structure and work culture—Taipei's Nangang and Neihu technology parks have formed visible startup clusters.

Taiwan's geographic location also creates strategic value: a flight to major Southeast Asian markets takes under 4 hours, and the deep industry networks of Taiwanese businesses in Southeast Asia give Taiwanese startups an edge over Silicon Valley entrepreneurs in cross-border expansion. This is why AppWorks lists "deep cultivation of Southeast Asia" alongside "AI" and "blockchain" as its three core directions.[^1]

## Development Timeline

### Early Stage (2000-2009)

Taiwan's early entrepreneurial environment was relatively conservative, and the dot-com bubble (2000-01) brought a cold winter that caused already limited venture capital to retreat further. The main challenges of this era included:

- Severe shortage of venture capital, with most investments in mature industries using "quasi-equity" models rather than early-stage tech startups
- Low tolerance for failure—social stigma around entrepreneurial failure discouraged many would-be founders
- Limited market size: Taiwan's domestic market was insufficient to support scalable consumer internet services
- Top engineering talent preferred joining companies like MediaTek and TSMC rather than startups

Despite this, this era laid crucial anchors for internet entrepreneurship. In 2000, Wretch (founded by National Chiao Tung University student Jian Zhiyu and his team) became one of Taiwan's earliest large-scale blog and photo-sharing platforms; in 2003, PChome Online launched its online bookstore and e-commerce services, establishing the foundation for Taiwan's indigenous e-commerce ecosystem; in 2006, Yahoo! acquired Wretch for NT$700 million. Although Yahoo! failed to integrate it well and shut down the service in 2013, this acquisition created an early precedent that "internet services can be recognized by capital markets."

The launch of the iPhone in 2007 was another watershed moment. The mobile internet opened enormous opportunities for app development, and Taiwan's strength in hardware-software integration finally found a real consumer market outlet, planting the seeds for the ecosystem's explosion in the 2010s.

### Takeoff Stage (2010-2015)

In the early 2010s, several key turning points emerged in Taiwan's startup ecosystem:

**Founding of AppWorks** (2010) marked the beginning of Taiwan's modern startup accelerator model. Founder Jamie Lin introduced Silicon Valley experience, offering free accelerator programs that selected the most promising startups every six months.

**Government policy support**: The government began to focus on the startup industry, launching various entrepreneurship subsidies and incubation programs, including the Ministry of Economic Affairs' SIIR program and the Ministry of Science and Technology's startup fund.

**Enhanced international connections**: More Taiwanese entrepreneurs had overseas experience and began targeting international markets.

### Growth Stage (2016-2020)

This stage saw rapid maturation of Taiwan's startup ecosystem:

**Diversification of accelerators**: In addition to AppWorks, internationally renowned accelerators such as SparkLabs Taipei, MOX, and Garage+ successively entered the Taiwan market.

**Increased venture capital**: The scale of domestic venture capital funds expanded, and international venture capital began to focus on the Taiwan market.

**More success stories**: Startups like 91APP, Gogoro, and Appier became benchmark companies, boosting overall ecosystem confidence.

### Optimization Stage (2021-Present)

Taiwan's startup ecosystem has entered a period of deep adjustment, with several key directions advancing simultaneously:

**AI application boom**: The AI wave triggered by ChatGPT in 2023 gave Taiwan a first-mover advantage. TSMC's advanced processes are the production base for AI chips, giving Taiwan a near-water advantage in AI startup hardware access. Local AI startups rapidly increased, focusing on enterprise AI assistants, AI-assisted manufacturing quality control, and AI image recognition.

**Deepening Southeast Asia presence**: Accelerators like AppWorks continue to increase their investment in Southeast Asia, with portfolio companies seeing rapid business growth in Vietnam, Indonesia, and Malaysia. The "Taiwan going global" model has evolved from early experimentation to a replicable methodology.

**IPO maturation**: Success stories like 91APP, Appier, and Gogoro have opened up pathways for Taiwanese startups to access capital markets in Taiwan, Tokyo, and NASDAQ, providing a reference exit map for newcomers. In 2021, the Taiwan Stock Exchange launched its "Innovation Board" and "Strategic New Board," lowering listing thresholds and not requiring profit records, providing opportunities for startups without traditional IPO paths to enter public capital markets. Gogolook (Walkie) listed on the Innovation Board in 2023 (stock code: 6902), becoming one of the first benchmark startups after the board's launch. Its Whoscall caller identification service has accumulated over 100 million downloads globally and is one of the few local apps to break into the Japanese and Korean mainstream consumer markets.

## Key Institutions and Roles

### AppWorks: Taiwan's Largest Startup Accelerator

AppWorks (init.ai) was founded by Jamie Lin in 2010 and is a benchmark institution in Taiwan's startup ecosystem, as well as one of the largest tech startup accelerators in Southeast Asia.[^1]

**Scale and Results (as of 2024)**:

- Manages funds totaling $38.6 million (including Fund I to Fund IV)
- Accelerator programs have run over 25 batches (AW#1 ~ AW#25+)
- Mentored over 600 startups, with over 2,000 ecosystem entrepreneurs
- Portfolio companies' total valuation exceeds $3.2 billion
- Representative investments include: Gogoro, 91APP, iKala, 17LIVE

**Operating Model**:

- Hosts Demo Day every six months to connect with investors
- Free six-month acceleration program including mentorship, office space, and legal/financial resources
- Focuses on three directions: AI applications, blockchain/Web3, and Southeast Asia markets
- Acceptance rate of about 3-5% per batch, highly competitive

### Other Accelerators and Incubators

**SparkLabs Taipei**: The Taiwan base of the internationally renowned accelerator SparkLabs, focusing on B2B enterprise service startups, sharing cross-border review and mentoring networks with its headquarters in Seoul, South Korea.

**MOX (Mobile Only Accelerator)**: Led by veteran entrepreneur Steven Goh in Taiwan, focusing on mobile internet and cross-border e-commerce, with mentees mostly early-stage startups targeting the Southeast Asia market, serving as a practical channel for Taiwanese startups expanding into Southeast Asia.

**Garage+**: Supported by Chunghwa Telecom's venture capital arm, focusing on 5G, IoT, and telecom application scenarios, providing opportunities for startups to connect with Chunghwa Telecom's enterprise clients, representing a rare accelerator model led by a telecom operator.

### Venture Capital Firms

**Domestic venture capital**: According to statistics from the Taiwan Venture Capital Association, the total scale of Taiwan's venture capital funds exceeds NT$100 billion. Representative institutions include init.ai (AppWorks Fund), CDIB Capital, Jardine Matheson Ventures, and the National Development Fund's co-investment platform. Domestic venture capital is relatively active in early-stage funding (seed to Series A) but more passive in later-stage large rounds.

**International venture capital entry**: After 2020, international venture capital's attention to the Taiwan market significantly increased, driven by two main factors: the "TSMC effect" made the world aware of Taiwan's strategic position in semiconductors and AI hardware; the acceleration of corporate digital transformation due to COVID-19 turned potential demand for Taiwanese B2B SaaS startups into clear demand. Sequoia Capital India (now reorganized as Peak XV Partners), SoftBank Vision Fund's sub-funds, and Singapore's Insignia Ventures have all invested in Taiwanese or Taiwan-related startups.

## Industry Characteristics and Advantages

### Technology-Oriented

Taiwanese startups generally have strong technology orientation, especially in:

- **AI and machine learning**: Leveraging Taiwan's deep ICT foundation
- **IoT and smart manufacturing**: Combining traditional manufacturing experience
- **Semiconductor applications**: Leveraging Taiwan's semiconductor industry advantages

### Cross-Border E-Commerce and Southeast Asia Markets

Many Taiwanese startups choose Southeast Asia as their primary overseas market:

- Geographic advantages and cultural proximity
- Deep networks of Taiwanese businesses in Southeast Asia
- Relatively low market entry barriers

### B2B Enterprise Services

Taiwanese startups excel in the B2B sector:

- Serving traditional manufacturing digital transformation
- Providing enterprise-level SaaS solutions
- Helping SMEs improve competitiveness

## In-Depth Analysis of Success Stories

### Gogoro: Battery Swapping Changes Urban Mobility

**Gogoro** (Power Digital) was founded by former HTC executive **Horace Luke** (Lü Xuesen) in 2011, initially entering the market with smart electric scooters. Its core competitiveness lies in the "GoStation" battery swapping network rather than simply selling vehicles. Users riding Gogoro don't need to charge themselves—just swap to a fully charged battery at roadside swapping stations, with the entire process taking less than a minute.

As of 2024, Taiwan has over 2,500 GoStation battery swapping stations, with over 50 million swaps accumulated, over 500,000 subscribers, and a market share exceeding 90% in Taiwan's electric scooter market. In 2022, Gogoro went public via merger with American SPAC company Poema Global on NASDAQ (stock code: GGR), becoming one of the representative cases of Taiwanese startups going global.[^2]

### Appier: AI Advertising Technology Lists in Tokyo

**Appier** (Pei Xing Interactive Technology) was founded in 2012 by former National Taiwan University electrical engineering professor **Chih-Han Yu** and co-founders, with AI-driven digital advertising technology as its core, providing programmatic ad placement and cross-screen audience analysis services.

In March 2021, Appier listed on the Tokyo Stock Exchange (stock code: 4180), a rare case of Taiwanese tech startups listing in Japan, with a market value once exceeding $1.3 billion, meeting "unicorn" standards. Appier has established offices in 17 markets including Japan, Singapore, and India, serving multinational clients such as Unilever and Shiseido.[^3]

### 91APP: Taiwan's First SaaS Startup IPO

**91APP** was founded in 2012 by **Rex How** (He Yingzhen), formerly of PChome Group's subsidiary, providing brand retailers with "omnichannel e-commerce SaaS solutions," integrating online stores, apps, store POS systems, and member data.

In July 2021, 91APP listed on the Taiwan Stock Exchange (stock code: 6741), becoming Taiwan's first purely SaaS-model tech startup to go public, serving over 1,000 well-known brands, and serving as a benchmark for Taiwanese B2B startups entering capital markets.[^4]

### Emerging Unicorn Candidates

Taiwan is actively cultivating potential unicorn companies, especially in AI applications, green energy technology, and precision medicine. ASUS's iKala (Ai Kara), with AI-driven cloud services and data analysis as its main business, has entered multiple Southeast Asian markets. Design e-commerce platform Pinkoi focuses on Asian original design products, with substantial user bases in Taiwan, Japan, and Hong Kong.

## Government Support System

### Main Policies

**Asia-Silicon Valley Development Plan** (since 2016): A key program of the Executive Yuan, aiming to make Taiwan a hub for innovation and entrepreneurship in the Asia-Pacific region. The plan promotes IoT industry innovation, builds international connections, advances smart city applications, and establishes "Taiwan Startup Stadium" (TSS) as a mentoring platform for startups going global.

**National Development Fund Startup Investment**: The National Development Fund directly invests in early-stage startups using government funds, or pairs with private venture capital through co-investment mechanisms to reduce early-stage venture risks and attract private capital. As of 2023, the National Development Fund has invested in over 300 startups.

**Entrepreneurship Angel Program**: The Ministry of Economic Affairs provides subsidies of up to NT$2 million for early-stage startups, targeting seed-stage teams that have not yet secured funding, representing one of the earliest points of government intervention in the ecosystem.

### Regulatory Environment

**Company Law Amendment** (2018): This revision allows startups to adopt dual-class shares, no-par-value stocks, and permits employee compensation through stock options, significantly improving the legal environment for founder control rights and employee incentives.

**Financial Regulatory Sandbox** (2018): Provides up to three years of regulatory exemption testing environment for FinTech startups, allowing innovative financial services to be tested before formal regulations are established. Dozens of companies have passed applications, covering areas such as blockchain payments and P2P lending.

**Foreign Professional Talent Recruitment and Employment Act** (2018): The "Employment Gold Card" system allows top-tier foreign professionals in specific fields to obtain work and residence permits with a single card, representing one of the most effective policy tools Taiwan has introduced in recent years to attract international talent.[^5]

## Challenges and Dilemmas

### Talent Competition

The salary gap between Taiwanese engineers and Silicon Valley widened rapidly in the late 2010s, with large multinational tech companies attracting Taiwanese talent with two to three times higher salaries. Startups' countermeasures mainly rely on stock options (ESOP), but the previous legal environment was unfavorable for ESOP tax treatment, only improving slightly after tax reforms in 2021. Technical talent (engineers) is relatively abundant, but product managers (PMs) and business professionals familiar with international markets remain scarce, representing the most common talent bottleneck for Taiwanese startups going overseas.

### Market Size and Internationalization

Taiwan's domestic market of 23 million people is insufficient to support truly scalable consumer-facing startups. This forces Taiwanese startups to adopt a "Day 1 global" approach, incorporating international markets from the very beginning, but language and brand awareness building remain the main obstacles for Taiwanese B2C startups entering European and American markets. Southeast Asia, due to cultural proximity and dense Taiwanese business networks, has become the first stop for most Taiwanese startups' internationalization.

### Late-Stage Funding Gap

Taiwan's startup funding environment has significantly improved before Series A, but late-stage funding (over $30 million) still heavily relies on overseas venture capital. Local institutional investors capable of leading late-stage rounds are few and far between, causing some promising Taiwanese startups to be forced to restructure overseas during their growth phase, with equity structures and tax residency moving to Singapore or the United States.[^5]

### Innovation Culture and Risk Tolerance

Although society's tolerance for entrepreneurial failure has improved compared to the 2000s, Taiwan still exhibits significant "failure stigma" compared to Silicon Valley or Israel. In many Taiwanese families, the stable salary of large corporations is still preferred over entrepreneurial risk, creating additional obstacles for early-stage startups when recruiting core talent.

## Future Outlook and Challenges

### Emerging Industry Opportunities

**AI Chip Applications**: TSMC's dominant position in AI chip manufacturing provides Taiwanese AI application startups with a near-water advantage for collaboration. The supply chain ecosystems of major companies like Qualcomm and NVIDIA in Taiwan give Taiwanese AI startups advantages in accessing advanced computing power and testing hardware integration solutions. Since 2023, the number of Taiwanese AI startup funding rounds has increased by over 40% year-on-year, indicating that capital has begun to concentrate in this direction.[^5]

**ESG and Sustainable Technology**: With the implementation of the Climate Change Response Act in 2023, Taiwan's large manufacturers face mandatory carbon inventory and reduction obligations, creating a large volume of B2B sustainable technology procurement demand, opening up a local market that did not previously exist for startups providing carbon footprint management, energy monitoring, and supply chain carbon tracking solutions.

**Precision Medicine and Biotechnology**: The National Health Insurance Administration's (NHIA) two decades of accumulated electronic medical records represent a rare large-scale longitudinal medical data asset globally. Combined with Taiwan's ICT strengths and complete biotech supply chain, biotech startups in AI-assisted diagnosis and personalized drug development have data advantages that are difficult to replicate in other markets.

### Ecosystem Issues to Resolve

**Funding Tier Gap**: Taiwanese startups have relatively abundant funding in the seed and Series A stages, but late-stage funding (after Series C) is severely lacking, causing capable growing companies to often seek funding overseas (Southeast Asia, Japan, United States). This is a key constraint preventing the ecosystem from continuously producing large-scale companies.

**Deepening International Connections**: Building closer cooperation with innovation centers like Silicon Valley, Singapore, and Israel is a necessary condition for ecosystem upgrading. Some Taiwanese accelerators have begun establishing informal alumni networks with Silicon Valley's renowned startup incubator Y Combinator, making it easier for Taiwanese startups to access Silicon Valley resources and market information.

**Bidirectional Talent Flow**: Attracting overseas Taiwanese talent to return and creating conditions to retain local talent is an issue the government is working on but has not yet fully resolved. The 2023 amendment to the "Startup Business Stock Options" tax regulations attempts to narrow the compensation competition gap between Taiwanese tech companies and overseas markets.

## Taiwan's Startup Positioning and Limitations

Taiwan's startup ecosystem has found three clear role positions on the global map: first, the "hardware-software integration" extending hardware advantages to software applications; second, the "bridgehead" using Taiwan as a stepping stone to enter Southeast Asia; third, the "vertical SaaS" serving traditional manufacturing digital transformation.

Each of these routes has representative cases. The hardware-software integration route is best represented by Gogoro—it integrates Taiwan's battery, motor, and IoT sensor supply chains into a replicable battery swapping system rather than simply making electric vehicles. The Southeast Asia bridgehead route is exemplified by 91APP—its omnichannel e-commerce SaaS was refined in Taiwan for reliability and then directly replicated to retail markets in Singapore and Malaysia. The vertical SaaS route is widespread across Taiwan's manufacturing industry, such as ITRI spin-off companies assisting factories with AI quality control, and factory digitalization solution providers integrating ERP and MES, though low-profile, they have actually changed the competitiveness of Taiwan's SME manufacturing sector.

However, Taiwan also faces several objective limitations that are difficult to overcome in the short term: a domestic market of only 23 million people, insufficient to allow consumer-facing startups to develop scalability; salary competitiveness gaps with Silicon Valley still causing top talent to flow overseas; language barriers making brand building in European and American markets more difficult for Taiwanese startups compared to Japanese and South Korean companies; and insufficient late-stage funding (after Series C), forcing capable companies to restructure overseas. Taiwan's ecosystem's next evolution depends on finding bypass routes around these structural limitations rather than waiting for them to disappear.

From AppWorks' success to Gogoro, Appier, and 91APP's breakthroughs in capital markets, Taiwan completed the basic construction of its ecosystem in just over a decade. The success of these pioneers is significant not only for accounting milestones but also for demonstrating how far Taiwanese startups can go, providing decomposable and learnable success paths for newcomers. The question for the next decade is whether, on this foundation, Taiwan can grow truly world-class companies: competing head-on with global market leaders and top competitors, and building sustainable competitive barriers on core products.
