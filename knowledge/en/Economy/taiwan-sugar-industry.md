> **30-Second Overview:** In 1901, the first modernized sugar mill in Qiaozitou was built in Kaohsiung. In 1907, the factory introduced a 762 mm gauge railway into the sugarcane fields, which people later called the five-foot train. Originally intended to transport sugarcane to the crushing machines, it gradually carried workers, students, pilgrims, and rural residents, becoming a local transportation system larger than the factory itself.[^1] [^2] [^3]

![Smoke stack and factory area of Qiaozitou Sugar Factory, showing an industrial landmark still recognizable today](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/51/Ciaotou_Sugar_Factory_20150130-1.jpg)

_Image source: Suicasmo, Wikimedia Commons〈File:Ciaotou Sugar Factory 20150130-1.jpg〉, CC BY-SA 4.0 International, using the original file hotlink._[^11]

## First Look at a Train, Then at a Sugar Mill

After the equipment upgrade at the Qiaozitou sugar mill in 1905, crushing capacity significantly increased. The ox carts and handcarts, which were originally sufficient for small sugarcane fields, began to fall behind the speed of sugarcane delivery to the factory. Sugarcane was not just collected into a warehouse for slow processing; it had to be delivered to the factory within a limited time after leaving the field, or the sugar content would decrease.[^2]

At that time, the factory manager Yamamoto Teijirō traveled to Hawaii to observe sugarcane transportation and eventually found the 762 mm gauge light railway on Maui. Qiaozitou initially tested vehicles pulled by water buffalo before switching to steam locomotives. This size was smaller than a standard railway and slower, but it was just right for traversing field ridges, riverbanks, and villages.[^2]

On September 15, 1907, the Qiaozitou Sugar Factory officially inaugurated Taiwan's first industrial railway. Its initial task was practical: to deliver sugarcane to the factory. However, once the tracks were laid in a locality, the transport line that originally served only the mill began to rearrange the movement of people and goods. Starting in 1909, some sugar rail lines opened for passenger and freight transport, giving the industrial infrastructure a public transportation function.[^2]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The five-foot train did not become famous as scenery first. It was first an engineering solution to keep the sugar mill supplied, and only later did it become a daily fixture used between villages.

## Before the Sugar Mill Vanished, Sugaring Was Already a Systemic Competition

Taiwan did not lack sugar before the modern sugar mills appeared. Early sugar mills were mostly small-scale, using local labor and simple equipment for production. Taiwan Sugar's own historical writings link sugarcane cultivation and sugar exports from the Dutch, Ming-Ching, and Qing dynasties, noting that there were still many old-style sugar mills across Taiwan in the early Japanese colonial period.[^4]

The Japanese government issued Sugar Industry Incentive Regulations in 1902 to encourage new sugar mills and sugarcane planting. The establishment of the Qiaozitou Sugar Factory was not a machine dropped randomly into the countryside. It required land, raw materials, workers, roads, railways, ports, and a system capable of securing sugarcane stably. A thesis from National Cheng Kung University broke this process down into four parts: sugar industry policy, domestic transport, sea transport, and Dagu Port, reminding readers that industrial expansion never happened only within the factory walls.[^5]

The efficiency of new sugar mills created competition for the old-style sugar mills. Taiwan Sugar records state that the Qiaozitou factory completed its equipment at the end of 1901 and officially started operations on January 15 of the following year. Data compiled by Wu Tsung-min and Yeh Yen-chun shows that by 1905, there were 7 new sugar mills in Taiwan, and in 1906, sugar exports surpassed tea exports, making it one of Taiwan's most important export commodities.[^4] [^15]

![Historical photo of the light railway in the Kaohsiung area, showing a sugar mill branch line entering the local landscape](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/21/Light_railway_of_sugar_industry_in_Takao.jpg)

_Image source: Pingtung Sugar Industry Headquarters〈File:Light railway of sugar industry in Takao.jpg〉, Wikimedia Commons file page marked as public domain by Japan and the United States, using the original file hotlink._[^18]

When raw material procurement became critical to whether a factory could operate, conflicts arose between cane farmers, mill owners, and corporations. This history cannot be glossed over with just "technological progress." Increased machine output also raised the question of who controlled the flow of sugarcane.[^6]

The doctoral dissertation _Sugar Industry Management and Peasant Disputes in Japanese-Occupied Taiwan_ focuses on management systems and peasant conflicts, filling a gap often overlooked by corporate histories. The sugar mill needed stable raw materials, while cane farmers faced procurement regulations, pricing, and land arrangements. Contract farming made the supply chain predictable but also drew peasants more deeply into corporate rules.[^7]

## How the Sugar Railway Brought Factory Time to Villages

The sugar railway initially dealt with the problem of "sugarcane must get to the factory quickly," but it soon addressed other people's time as well. Records from the Xi-hu Sugar Mill Five-Foot Station, preserved by the National Cultural Heritage Data Open Service Network, indicate that the sugar rail network was centered around Xi-hu, connecting Lukang, Xizhou, Beitou, and Yuanlin, among others. Besides transporting milling materials, it also served passenger transport.[^1]

Cultural records of the Huhu Sugar Mill railway bridge place the factory, the bridge, and the trunk railway on the same local transportation map. The Huhu Sugar Mill was established in 1906, and the planning of the sugar mill railway bridge for freight transport made the sugar mill a node for outward movement of goods.[^1]

These lines were not necessarily unified island-wide networks like modern railways. Research from National Cheng Kung University points out that sugar industry railways, sea transport, and Dagu Port formed a division of labor; after aggregation within the island, sugar was then connected to external markets via ports.[^5] A historical article in Taipei Times notes that at the end of Japanese rule, the total length of the sugar railway approached 3,000 kilometers, with over 600 kilometers providing commercial service. Placing this number on a map reveals how many industrial branch lines connected rural areas west of the Central Mountain Range to larger markets.[^2]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The modernization of Taiwan's countryside was not solely written by trunk railways. Many localities waited for a train carrying sugarcane before they received a train that could carry people into the city.

![Historical railway train transporting sugarcane in southwestern Taiwan, circa 1900 to 1945](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0c/Sugar_cane_train_in_southern_Taiwan.jpg)

_Image source: Wikimedia Commons〈File:Sugar cane train in southern Taiwan.jpg〉, author unknown, file page marked as public domain by Japan and the United States, using the original file hotlink._[^13]

The five-foot train also entered religious and local activities. A Taipei Times article records that after the Beigang sugar rail line opened in 1911, it was used to transport pilgrims going to Chaotian Temple. When industrial lines were used by locals for visiting relatives, attending school, working, and pilgrimage, the economic logic of the sugar mill seeped into daily life.[^2]

## Behind the Sweetness: Colonial Capital and Land Order

The modern sugar industry during Japanese rule certainly brought machinery, improved varieties, and transportation infrastructure, but its purpose and power relations must also be addressed in the same text. Corporate histories compiled by Taiwan Sugar point out that the Taiwan Sugar Company, established in 1900, received support from Japanese capital and selected Qiaozitou to establish the first modern factory in 1901. This is a story of corporate investment, but it is also a story of the colonial government hoping for Taiwan's fiscal self-sufficiency and supply to the Japanese market.[^4]

The National Cultural Heritage website positions the Qiaozitou Sugar Factory as Taiwan's first modernized sugar mill, recording its history from construction in early 1901, expansion after 1905, to near completion before 1945. This "first" status is important, but it should not reduce the land and workers outside the factory walls to mere background.[^6]

Academic research on the sugar industry and transport during the early Japanese colonial period shows that the development of new sugar mills was interconnected with regional raw material procurement, railway construction, sea routes, and the rise of Dagu Port. The sugar mill thus functioned as an industrial facility, a land manager, and a transportation node.[^5]

From the farmers' perspective, contract farming was not just a corporate management method for "stable supply." It involved what crops were grown, when sugarcane was harvested, which factory it was delivered to, and how prices were calculated. The study of peasant disputes is important because it brings sugar industry history back from the company's production ledger to the fields.[^7]

## Post-War Acquisition Was Not a Single Date

![Historical train exhibition at GuangFu Sugar Factory in Hualien, showing how industrial transport became local memory after cessation](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/53/Taiwan_2009_GuangFu_Sugar_Factory_Historical_Train_Exhibition_RD_6173.jpg)

_Image source: Fred Hsu〈File:Taiwan 2009 GuangFu Sugar Factory Historical Train Exhibition RD 6173.jpg〉, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0 Unported, using the original file hotlink._[^19]

After Japan's surrender in August 1945, the ownership of the sugar mills changed, but the factories did not resume operation as a result. The Resource Committee formed an acquisition team in late October 1945 to take over all major sugar mills previously owned by the Japanese. The acquisition process faced equipment damage, material shortages, personnel reassignment, and raw material supply issues; it required reincorporating the existing facilities, land, railways, and corporate systems into the post-war state apparatus.[^14]

A summary by Cheng Yu-feng points out that about 80% of sugar mills were damaged at the end of the war. Restoration was completed within two years through repair, material input, equipment procurement, and technical personnel deployment, with production resuming in 1946–47. This process made "the establishment of Taiwan Sugar" not just a company registration date, but a continuous effort of acquisition, repair, and re-establishing the production order.[^14]

Research by Wu Tsung-min and Yeh Yen-chun indicates that the Taiwan Sugar Company, established at the end of 1945, merged the four major sugar companies from the Japanese colonial period into one state-owned monopoly structure. The new company inherited the original land and factories and continued the raw material collection area system. Even though the management changed, sugarcane supply remained fixed around specific mills.[^15]

## Post-War Taiwan Sugar Took Over Factories, But Also Took On Problems

After 1945, war damage affected many sugar mills and facilities. Post-war writings by Taiwan Sugar state that the Taiwan Sugar Company was established on May 1, 1946, to acquire and manage the sugar industry assets left over from the Japanese colonial period. The article described the sugar industry as a pillar of post-war economic reconstruction and mentioned Taiwan Sugar's systems for repairing factories, exporting sugar, developing byproducts, and maintaining cane farmer supply.[^8]

This narrative has its historical weight, but it also requires a corporate history perspective. Taiwan Sugar linked the international sugar price increases caused by the Korean War in the 1950s, export markets, and US aid equipment to create a "golden age." On the other hand, because the industry relied on international prices, weather, varieties, land, and government protection, expanding cultivation did not guarantee long-term success.[^8]

The post-war sugar rationing system and minimum guaranteed prices attempted to fix the relationship between the sugar mill and the cane farmer in a single production chain. This system provided relatively stable raw materials and income on one hand, but it also gave Taiwan Sugar strong organizational power in rural areas. The sugar mill was not just a source of work; it could be a center for local land, credit, transport, and welfare.[^8]

Another article in the Taiwan Sugar monthly magazine discussing the century-old sugar industry classified cultural assets into agricultural, industrial, transportation, and management types. This classification actually illustrates the difficulty of preserving the sugar industry heritage: if only the smokestacks and factories are preserved, the relationships that once made the entire locality run cannot be saved.[^9]

## Sugarcane Prices Are Not Just a Price Tag

![Train inside Jiali Sugar Refinery, showing how the mill still organized space for raw materials and factory grounds via rail](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/90/Jiali_Sugar_Refinery_Plant_Train.JPG)

_Image source: Koika〈File:Jiali Sugar Refinery Plant Train.JPG〉, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0 Unported, using the original file hotlink._[^20]

The raw material procurement area system of 1905 stipulated that sugarcane within a region could not be transported out or used for other sugaring purposes without permission from the Governor's Office. Thus, sugar mills held a monopoly on local sugarcane; cane farmers could still choose their crops but had to decide between the sugar mill's announced price and the expected income of competing crops like rice. Since it could take one to one-and-a-half years from planting to harvest, the sugarcane price was essentially a gamble on the future.[^15]

The post-war rationing system attempted to rearrange this relationship. In the 1946–47 period, cane farmers received 48% of the sugar produced, and Taiwan Sugar took 52%. From the 1947–48 period onward, the ratio was adjusted to fifty-fifty. The minimum guaranteed price was introduced in the 1950–51 period, with the government and Taiwan Sugar hoping to stabilize farmers' willingness to plant sugarcane. While the numbers seemed to give farmers more product, their actual income still depended on sugar prices, milling rates, retail prices, and rice prices.[^15]

Wu Tsung-min and Yeh Yen-chun termed this issue "rice-sugar antagonism": farmers compared expected returns between rice and sugarcane, while the sugar mill was the main buyer of sugarcane raw materials. Contract farming and guaranteed prices provided stability but also tied farmers more tightly to a single processor. This is one of the contradictions worth noting in the sugar industry system, because supply chain stability does not necessarily mean that every participant has equal bargaining power.[^15]

## Who Worked in the Sugar Mill?

If sugar industry history only retains company names and railway lines, the factory appears as a self-operating machine. In reality, the milling season was a period of high-density labor. Sugarcane had to be harvested when ripe, cut, loaded onto carts, weighed, and then delivered to the crushing area according to the mill's sequence. Machinery increased the speed of sugaring, but it did not make the work in the fields easier. On the contrary, the factory clock pulled the rural rhythm into a more precise delivery schedule.

Cane farmers, cart drivers, railway workers, boiler room staff, machine repair technicians, and office clerks collectively maintained the seasonal production of a sugar mill. The wages, skills, and stability of these positions differed, yet they were all linked by the same milling season. The social sphere provided by the sugar mill was therefore hierarchical and densely interdependent.

Workers' lives also extended to dormitories, clinics, welfare associations, sports fields, and unions. The systems varied between different mills, so a single factory's welfare experience cannot be directly applied nationwide, but these spaces show how corporations entered local society. Factory management objects included not only job classifications but also the housing, medical care, leisure, and children's education of the employees' families. Thus, the sugar mill became a walled society.

## From Export Pillar to Industrial Shift

The importance of the sugar industry can be seen through its production value ratio, as well as how it exited the central position. Long-term statistics compiled by Wu Tsung-min and Yeh Yen-chun show that in 1925, sugar accounted for about 54.3% of Taiwan's main industrial output, and it remained at 56.4% in 1937. Post-war, in 1951, it accounted for about 20% of the manufacturing sector's main product value, dropping to 5% in 1965 and only 0.8% in 1978. This set of figures shows that sugar was a major component of industrial output before the war but gradually retreated into a national retained industry after the war.[^15]

The sugar industry still held significant state objectives in the 1950s and 1960s. Sugar exports could earn foreign exchange, factories provided jobs, and railways connected rural areas to markets. However, this same system was constrained by international sugar prices and transportation costs. As other producing countries increased efficiency and imported sugar entered the market, Taiwan's land and labor costs rose, gradually loosening the old advantages of the sugar industry. Corporate histories from Taiwan Sugar describe this period as a golden age; while this description shows the peak of exports and investment, it must be read alongside later industrial adjustments.[^8]

The decline of the sugar industry was not an abrupt stop in one year. Some lines reduced schedules first, some mills adjusted their production seasons, and some farmlands switched to other crops. For those living near a sugar mill, change often appeared first as waiting getting longer: the five-foot train no longer passed every day, the factory smokestack did not smoke at a fixed season, and familiar jobs did not return. Thus, industrial transitions are also felt through changes in daily rhythms.

## The Factory Stopped, But the Sugar Industry Did Not Immediately Leave

Imported sugar, international competition, structural changes in industry, and cost issues led to Taiwan's gradual exit from its former central export position. Wu Tsung-min and Yeh Yen-chun point out that the problems faced by post-war Taiwan Sugar were not only related to international sugar prices but also to simultaneous changes in export markets, exchange rate arrangements, state monopoly systems, and sugarcane procurement systems. Explaining the decline merely as "fierce international competition" misses the impact of corporate systems and policy prices on farmers and factories.[^15] After some mills closed down, localities began facing a difficult question: if a factory no longer produced sugar, could it still be part of the locality?

The Ministry of Culture's thematic materials on the sugar industry place Qiaozitou, Tulan, Xi-hu, and Huhu in two sections: "Fading Sugar Industry" and "Recreating Sugar Culture." The Qiaozitou Sugar Factory Art Village began operating in 2001, converting a historical preservation area into an artistic residency space. This approach acknowledges that the factory's production function has changed, allowing new users to establish another relationship with the old buildings.[^1]

A cultural heritage study of Kaohsiung City records that the Qiaozitou Sugar Factory Police and Security Brigade was built around 1953 and later incorporated into the sugar mill's historical preservation and reuse planning. This report lists the factory's establishment, expansion, police/security operations, building structure, and disaster prevention management, reminding us that cultural preservation must also answer who uses it, how to maintain it, and what to do in case of a disaster.[^10]

📝 **Curator's Note:** A sugar mill's second life does not automatically equal successful preservation. True preservation is allowing people to see how a factory once dominated a locality, and enabling that locality to decide what it will be next.

## The Final Stretch of the Sugar Railway

![Huhu Taiwan Sugar small train, photographed in 2007, showing the later stage where the sugar railway transitioned from a production tool to local memory](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/58/Taiwan_Sugar_Railways_train_02.jpg)

_Image source: Hung Ju Lu〈File:Taiwan Sugar Railways train 02.jpg〉, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0 Generic, using the original file hotlink._[^21]

The decline of the sugar railway was not instantaneous. When sugar mills faced fiercer international competition in the late 1970s, some factories closed, and rail branches lost their original task of transporting sugarcane. A study on the preservation of Taiwan's sugar industry culture notes that after Taiwan Sugar gradually closed its milling plants, employees had to transition from producers and managers to cultural asset researchers, maintainers, reuse planners, and guides.[^16]

This transformation involves an uncomfortable fact: those who preserve the sugar industry are often the people who once worked in the mills. When boilers, railways, and warehouses become exhibits, the original working techniques must be translated into stories that visitors can understand. Research indicates that Taiwan Sugar began surveying its sugar industry cultural assets from 2002, establishing a sugar industry museum, the Taiwan Sugar Museum, and a cultural park, but the state-owned enterprise simultaneously grapples with tensions between profit and public welfare, and regulations and bureaucratic systems.[^16]

A special issue of _Taiwan Guanghua_ published in 2026 places the 1990s for the sugar railway as its twilight years of gradual closure, placing the railway within a network that was still extending but shrinking. Therefore, the heritage of the sugar railway cannot be understood solely through tourist trains. It also includes how lines were dismantled, how land was reused, how old employees re-explained their work, and how localities decided which facilities were worth keeping.[^17]

## One Sweetness, Four Memories

![Qiaozitou Sugar Factory grounds and industrial railway, showing the spatial relationship between the mill and the railway](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c4/Ciaotou_Sugar_Refinery_01.jpg)

_Image source: SuinegMai〈File:Ciaotou Sugar Refinery 01.jpg〉, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0 Unported, using the original file hotlink._[^12]

For sugar mill workers, memory might be the shifts during the milling season, the sound of machinery, and the heat inside the factory. For cane farmers, it might be the weather before ripening, harvest time, sugarcane carts, and settlement. For those who rode the five-foot train, the railway was a route to markets, schools, temples, and neighboring towns. For those entering the sugar mill grounds today, what remains might be red bricks, railways, warehouses, and a reinterpreted industrial history.

These memories coexist; they cannot cancel each other out. The sugar industry was once an important pillar of Taiwan's exports and industrialization, and it once connected the colonial government, capital companies, rural land, and workers into one management system. It created transport, but this did not mean everyone used the transport equally. It brought factories, but this did not mean that the benefits and risks of the factory were borne by the same group of people.

So, what the five-foot train truly carried was never just sugarcane. It carried a set of requirements regarding speed, a system for land arrangement, and an order concerning who could move. Today, it rests in the sugar mill grounds; its wheels no longer deliver raw materials to the crushing machine, yet they force us to look back: who laid the tracks for Taiwan's modernization, who worked by those tracks, and who remained behind to care for the memory after the factory stopped?
