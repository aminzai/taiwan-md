> **30-second overview:** In 1967, Taiwan exported nearly 400,000 tons of bananas annually, capturing 82% of the Japanese market; by 2014, only 4,000 tons remained—about 1%. This gap is not merely the story of Philippine large-scale farms defeating Taiwanese smallholders, but also concerns a market system Taiwan once built that could bind output, quality, transportation, and price together—only to lose its handlers once the market opened.

In 1967, Flagshan banana farmers loaded string after string of bananas onto trucks bound for Kaohsiung Port. That year, Taiwan bananas commanded 82% of the Japanese market.[^1] Half a century later, during a summer in 2000, bananas at the Flagshan collection station plummeted from NT$10 per kilogram to NT$1, with fruit piled high, rotting in wait.

Bananas may look like the most ordinary fruit, yet they once constituted one of Taiwan's most expensive avenues for external trade. They elevated the credit union deposits of Flagshan to the top in the country, and grew a southern town into a hub of finance, transportation, and entertainment. The same trade route later exposed Taiwan's small farmers to the Philippine large-scale plantations, transnational brands, and shifts in Japanese consumer habits.[^3]

> **📝 Curator's note:** What makes the "Banana Kingdom" truly worth remembering is not how many bananas Taiwan once exported, but how small farmers once won in the Japanese market through a collective system against large plantations.

## The Banana Oil of Flagshan, Once the Taste of Wealth

In 2000, 79-year-old Chang Rongyao harvested bananas in a 1.5-hectare plot in Flagshan. Using an old ox-horn-shaped knife, he sliced the fruit strings, and the banana oil seeping from the cuts stained his clothes with an acid scent that could not be washed away.[^2] This detail resembles a parable: the same stain, in his youth a sign of wealth, now merely a mark of toil.

Chang remembers the bustling days of Flagshan when banana farmers, their clothes stained with oil, walked into teahouses—others knew that meant they were men of means. Flagshan once hosted the densest concentration of banana orchards in Taiwan, producing nearly a third of the nation's total. The credit union was locally called "the richest bank in Taiwan."[^2]

This prosperity did not appear overnight. Scholar Chuang Shu-chen divides the institutional history of Taiwan's banana industry into four periods: domestic trade under Japanese rule before 1946, export joint ventures from 1950 to 1963, the "55 Export System" from 1963 to 1973, and the integrated production and marketing system from 1974 to 2006.[^4] Thus, banana history is not merely "farmers plant, merchants sell," but a chronicle of how markets were organized. Taiwan Literary Research also identifies 1963, when Japan liberalized banana imports, as an institutional turning point, underscoring that changes in market rules matter more than the rise and fall of any single crop.[^10]

## A Yellow Airline from Keelung to Tokyo

Taiwan's banana exports to Japan began in the early 20th century. In 1903, Keelung merchants shipped bananas to Japan. Around 1910, Taiwan entered a phase of large-scale cultivation. By 1924, the volume of bananas exported to Japan exceeded 100,000 tons, and the following year, the Taiwan Tropical Fruit Company was established, giving the export network a fixed organizational structure.[^5]

During the Japanese colonial period, bananas first served as an export crop of colonial agriculture, later becoming the backbone of southern Taiwan's local economy. Flagshan was not thriving alone: railways and ports connected banana orchards to cities, packing facilities and collection stations transformed easily damaged fruit strings into calculable commodities, and financial institutions preserved the foreign exchange earned at the source.[^4]

After the war, Taiwan resumed banana exports to Japan in 1950. In 1963, Japan lifted banana import restrictions, and the volume of Taiwan bananas shipped to Japan jumped from 58,000 tons in 1962 to 427,000 tons in 1967. Foreign exchange earnings exceeded US$63 million that year.[^5] Agricultural authorities coordinated production and marketing systems and transportation from 1958 to 1967, and helped establish 440 banana and citrus packing facilities in production areas, responsible for collecting, grading, and packing.[^5]

Here lies an easily overlooked contrast: the Banana Kingdom was not built by any single "banana tycoon," but jointly completed by packing facilities, cooperatives, ports, transportation, and export regulations. Farmers saw strings of bananas; the system saw unbroken cold chains and price chains.

## The Banana Sheds in the Port, Filled with a Nation's Foreign Exchange Dreams

In 1956, Kaohsiung Port specially opened Dock No. 3 to handle banana exports to Japan. Between 1962 and 1963, the government used U.S. aid loans to construct approximately 2,400 square meters of banana sheds, capable of holding over 20,000 banana bunches. In 2010, this space was renamed "Banana Wharf," becoming part of local memory.[^5]

At the time, bananas were not simply picked and boxed. Farmers had to harvest at the right maturity, collection stations had to wash, grade, and pre-cool, and the export system had to coordinate with shipping schedules in the Japanese market. Ministry of Agriculture data indicates bananas are typically harvested at about seven to eight and a half on the ripeness scale, pre-cooled for one to two days in cold storage, then ripened with ethylene to maintain texture and aroma upon market arrival.[^6]

This process explains why bananas could drive an entire region's industry, not just expand farmland. It required people who could read the fruit, workers who could grade, ships that could arrive on schedule, and a system that could temporarily support farmers when prices plummeted.

What remains of the banana sheds today is not just the roof and shelves, but evidence of how a place transformed agricultural products into export infrastructure. When it was renamed Banana Wharf, its function shifted from collection to display, and the locality simultaneously faced a question: if history is reduced to tourist signage, can the living conditions of banana farmers still be seen? This is precisely the most difficult aspect of industrial cultural heritage—it must allow people to see the past, yet cannot pretend the past is the present.

> **📝 Curator's note:** After a string of bananas is picked from the tree, the real journey begins; what Taiwan once exported was not fruit, but the capability to safely deliver fruit to Tokyo.

## After 1967, the Kingdom Did Not Collapse Overnight

Taiwan bananas peaked in 1967: nearly 400,000 tons exported, holding 82% of the Japanese market. By 2014, exports had shrunk to just 4,000 tons, with market share dropping to 1%.[^1] This gap is often described as "Philippine large farms defeating Taiwan," but that explanation is only half right.

The first turning point was the scale of supply. Philippine and South American transnational plantations could manage hundreds to thousands of hectares of unified land. Taiwanese banana farmers, however, often worked plots of only half a hectare to a few hectares. Taiwanese agricultural economist Peng Zuo-kui points out that smallholder systems struggle to compete with large plantations on cost.[^2]

The second turning point was the change in the Japanese market. Japanese importers increasingly demanded smaller packaging and stable supply, while Taiwanese traders were unwilling to alter their traditional habit of selling whole strings for a few bananas. Philippine transnational companies offered uniform standards, long-term supply, and shortage compensation, sparing Japanese buyers from daily quality guessing.[^7]

The third turning point occurred within Taiwan's own system. In 2005, the government ended the cooperative's monopoly on banana exports and opened the market to multiple traders. CommonWealth's report noted that some traders signed large contracts with farmers without corresponding quality and market support. Bananas that failed to export were dumped into the domestic market, where prices fell under oversupply.[^7]

Market liberalization itself is not a crime. The problem is that once the system binding output, quality, and sales channels was dismantled, the new system did not grow fast enough to replace it. Taiwanese banana farmers gained more choices but not necessarily greater bargaining power.

## A Convenience Store Banana, Two Fates of an Industry

In 2014, Taiwan's convenience stores sold about 100,000 bananas per day. Over a year, a single retail chain's sales volume even surpassed Taiwan's total annual export volume.[^7] At first glance, this seems like bananas returning to daily life, but on the other hand, it reflects how, after the export industry shrank, the domestic retail channel became a new arena of competition.

Dole introduced single bananas into convenience stores, bringing ripeness management, packaging, and brand control into the Taiwanese market. Industry professionals at the Taiwan Banana Research Institute hold differing views: some believe transnational brands elevate the commodity value of bananas and assist new varieties in exporting. Others worry that when retail channels are controlled by foreign companies, Taiwanese farmers will be more easily replaced by cheap Philippine bananas.[^7]

This debate has no simple answer. Consumers want bananas they can buy today—uniform in size, predictable in ripeness. Farmers need income that can withstand typhoons, diseases, and price fluctuations. Both demands are reasonable, but they may not be met by the same channel.

## There Is No Single Kind of Banana in the Kingdom

"Taiwan bananas" are not an unchanging variety. The Ministry of Agriculture lists the "Northern Banana" as Taiwan's traditional representative cultivar, with a growth cycle of about 12 months. Red-skinned bananas and Formosan bananas each have different maturity periods, appearances, and market positions.[^6] These differences are not merely botanical knowledge—they directly determine whether banana farmers can spread risks from typhoons, diseases, and price fluctuations.

The fragility of bananas is also hidden in their appearance. With large leaves and slender trunks, they are easily broken by wind. Flagshan banana farmers therefore combine tissue-cultured seedlings, sucker seedlings, and root suckers in their plantings, staggering harvest times across different fields. When a banana plant is toppled by a typhoon, the loss is not an abstract "agricultural output value" for a season, but a string of fruits that took an entire year to mature.[^6]

The 1960s boom left another problem: the more concentrated the output, the greater the cost of disease and price volatility. The establishment of the Taiwan Banana Research Institute was precisely aimed at breeding, improvement, and providing new seedlings. The Banana Kingdom later did not recover by expanding land, but by rewriting "delicious" into wind-resistant, disease-resistant, and traceable conditions through research.[^5]

## The Banana Has Not Disappeared, It Has Simply Changed Its Problem

A report from the Flagshan office of the Ministry of Agriculture records another answer: today's banana farmers no longer pursue quantity alone, but attempt to improve quality through field management, natural farming, and production records. Bananas remain annual crops, each plant bearing only one bunch of fruit. After harvest, the banana plant is removed, and the next generation begins anew from suckers or root suckers.[^6]

Flagshan's industry is not merely nostalgic. Academic research indicates that the decline of bananas caused Flagshan to lose its former role as a central hub, but bananas themselves became a collective memory and cultural asset of the locality. The locality began transforming the banana industry into cultural tourism capital.[^4] This is not wrapping agriculture into a tourist attraction and calling it revival, but acknowledging that a place's prosperity once depended on a supply chain that has since changed course.

The art team Pailang Museum's "A Concise Lexicon of Banana" places bananas back into the broader context of colonialism and global commodity crops: bananas are not only edible fruit but also symbols of currency, shipping, finance, and colonial economies.[^9] This perspective reminds us that Taiwan's banana story is not an isolated local legend, but a microcosm of how an island is shaped by global markets.

Today, buying a Flagshan banana does not bring back the 1967 Japanese market share, nor does it restore an intact Banana Kingdom. What it brings is the banana farmer's long-term judgment regarding typhoons, diseases, ripeness, and prices. It is also a place's attempt to turn industrial memory into a new form of survival after losing its export peak.

> **📝 Curator's note:** The end of the Banana Kingdom is not "Taiwan will no longer grow bananas," but Taiwan must once again answer: when small farmers can no longer win through quantity against large farms, what else can make a single banana worth preserving?
