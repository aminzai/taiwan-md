# "Bapu" Ice Cream: The Horn Arrived at the Street Corner Before the Ice Bucket

![A Bapu ice cart in Taiwan's streets in 1987 with children gathered around](/article-images/food/babu-ice-cart-vendor-1987.webp)

> **30-Second Overview:** Around 1947, Fan Yang-kai in Xianglin, Hsinchu, sold ice carrying a 2.5 kg bucket after school. Decades later, bicycle vendors still used a metal horn activated by pressing a rubber ball to let "Bapu" pass through the alley entrance before delivering the ice to children. This ice was often made from starch for a Q-elastic texture, backed by processes ranging from port ice making and ice bucket cooling to weather risks and after-school odd jobs. What Bapu truly preserves is not just three-color ice, but how food meets people on the street through sound, tools, and human labor.[^1] [^3] [^8]

Around 1947, Fan Yang-kai was a third-grade student in Xianglin Township, Hsinchu County. After school, he would buy a bucket of ice from an ice factory and sell it while cycling over more than four kilometers home. The empty ice bucket weighed about 2.5 kg, which was a real burden for a child.[^1]

About seventy-five years later, in 2022, at Hama Xing, Kaohsiung, an ice product exhibition that started from fishery ice brought Bapu carts, egg ice, Zhizai ice, and old-fashioned shaved ice machines onto the same historical timeline.[^2]

These two scenes are separated by different eras but connected by a shared contrast: Bapu is light to eat, yet heavy to make. The entrance is a refreshing scoop, but behind it lie the ice factory, the ice bucket, the bicycle, the selling horn, weather forecasting, and a child's calculation of the risk associated with unsold goods.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Bapu is not just ice sold on the street. It turns the entire street into an ice shop, letting sound find customers first, before customers decide whether to approach.

## Hearing "Bapu" Before Seeing the Ice

A horn used for selling ice cream, preserved by the National Taiwan Museum of History, measures about 20.5 cm. The metallic front is curled like a French horn, and the tail connects to a black rubber ball. When the rubber ball is pressed, air is forced into the spring mechanism at the back of the metal horn, producing a short, loud sound. The Ministry of Culture's collection website places it in a post-war street scene: vendors cycling with an ice bucket and cones on the rear rack, weaving through city streets and alleys.[^3] [^8]

_Figure 2 | Ice cream selling horn, catalog number 2004.028.1601. Image source: _[Ministry of Culture's Collection Website](https://collections.culture.tw/Object?SYSUID=11&RNO=MjAwNC4wMjguMTYwMQ==)_, collected by the National Taiwan Museum of History. The page is marked "For Public Viewing Only."_

This tool is not just an old implement; it is sound design. Before the vendor even came into sight, the sound had already passed through the alley entrance. Residents could judge from which direction the vendor was approaching inside their homes, and children could run to the doorway before adults permitted them to.

The name "Bapu" embeds the sales format into the ice product's name. When people spoke of food, they were speaking of a street experience: not waiting at a fixed counter to choose, but moving, making sound, stopping, and then customers gathering around.

Another selling horn collected by the Taichung City Government Cultural Bureau, catalog number 20100060591, is described as measuring 5 cm long, 5 cm wide, and 13 cm high, with a description also pointing to the connection between the rubber ball, spring mechanism, and the "Bapu" sound.[^10] The same type of tool appearing in different collections shows that it was not an accidental accessory of one vendor, but a replicable street vending technology.

## How the Shape of the Horn Became a Business Model

Sound on the street is not decoration; it is the vendor's marketing tool. A fixed shop can be identified by signs, display windows, and regular customers. A bicycle ice cart has no fixed address; it must turn "I am here" into a signal that can penetrate walls.

This also explains the vitality of the Bapu name. It is not a direct translation of the English "ice cream" into Tâi-gí or Mandarin, but rather naming the ice product with its selling sound. The food's name thus retains the transaction scene: who made the sound, who heard it, and who walked out from home.

_Figure 3 | Side view of a selling horn. Image source is the same as Figure 2, for public viewing only._

In the early days, ice buckets were usually placed on tricycles or bicycles. The vehicle carried the ice; the horn carried expectation. Children, neighbors, or after-schoolers heard the sound and knew that a brief choice was about to happen: should they run out to buy a scoop? A special feature in the National Taiwan Museum of History's summer ice products section places the ice bucket, selling, and post-school street life within one visual narrative.[^4]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The ice scoop determines the shape of a scoop; the horn determines when a piece of public life begins. Perhaps the most important tool is not the implement that digs out the ice, but the sound that calls people together.

## Why It Is Not Ordinary Dairy Ice Cream

In daily language, Bapu is often called ice cream, but its production logic differs from Western ice cream, which uses milk, fresh cream, and dairy fat as the main structure. Related food culture articles point out that Taiwanese Bapu usually does not rely on fats for texture, but rather thickens with starch to give the product a resilient, Q-elastic quality.[^5] [^7]

The description of the tool on the Ministry of Culture's collection website also directly writes the difference into the item's description: Bapu is made from starch, resulting in a Q-textured ice cream. Foreign ice creams are often made with milk or fresh cream as raw materials.[^8]

| Production Clue  | Identifiable Feature of Bapu Ice                                                  | Why It Affects Texture                                              |
| :--------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Main Structure   | Starch, sugar, and various flavors of fruits or beans                             | Starch gelatinization restricts water movement, bringing resilience |
| Freezing Process | Simultaneous cooling, stirring, and freezing                                      | Ice crystals are broken down, texture is not just binary hard/soft  |
| Eating Sensation | The powderiness of taro, the sandiness of azuki beans, the sweetness of pineapple | Mouthfeel has a chewiness, not just the smoothness of dairy fat     |

Taking taro flavor as an example, the taro was first boiled and mashed into a paste. Tapioca starch was mixed with cold water to form a slurry before being added, then mixed with malt syrup and sugar, finally entering a low-temperature rapid stirring freezing tank.[^5]

This process should not be misinterpreted as "simpler because it lacks dairy." It is another kind of engineering: balancing ingredients in terms of sweetness, water content, starch gelatinization, and freezing speed, while ensuring the final product can be scooped with an ice scoop, placed in a wafer cup, and eaten by customers on the street.

After starch gelatinizes through heat, it absorbs water, restricting water movement. The combined action of cooling, stirring, and freezing creates a denser and more elastic texture. This also explains why Bapu does not have the heavy smoothness derived from fat like dairy ice cream, but leaves a clearer chewiness on the tongue.

## Labor in the Ice Bucket Was Never Background Noise

Fan Yang-kai's oral recollections transform "selling ice" from a nostalgic image into a job. Starting around 1947, he sold plain ice and mung bean ice during weekdays, only selling more numerous and higher-priced ice cream on holidays. On good days, he could sell out one bucket; on rainy days, he might have unsold ice. The ice factory allowed returns the next day, but if the ice was completely melted, it could not be returned.[^1]

The ice bucket was not a light prop, nor was the weather merely seasonal background. The child had to estimate the sunlight, the journey, and the customers, while also bearing the risk of non-returnable melted ice. Therefore, the refreshment of the ice existed concurrently with family income, children's odd jobs, and specific economic calculations.

The outer layer of the ice bucket Fan Yang-kai used was zinc plate, with a glass inner liner, and the mouth was sealed with cloth padding for insulation. The ice scoop was copper; when scooping, he first had to process the softer ice before pressing down the handle so that the scraper inside the scoop could deposit one scoop into the wafer cup.[^1]

These details show that Bapu's "old-fashioned" quality is not a filter, but a series of actions that must be completed by hand: carrying the bucket onto the cart, keeping the ice in a scoopable state, controlling the size of a scoop with an ice scoop, and completing the delivery in front of the customer.

After graduating from elementary school, Fan Yang-kai stopped selling ice, but the ice bucket and ice scoops remained at home, later donated to the Bitan Elementary School Artifact Museum. When an implement originally used for keeping things cold and accelerating transactions entered a display space, its function changed: it no longer delivered ice to customers, but preserved a segment of childhood labor for future generations to view.[^1]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** A child carrying an ice bucket for four kilometers is not a "cute childhood story." It is a working route that ties together the ice factory, family income, after-school time, and weather risk.

## From Port Ice Making to Street Sweets

The appearance of Bapu cannot be explained solely by "Taiwanese love eating ice." A special feature on ice products from the Kaohsiung City Government links the city's history of frozen treats back to the 1910s: the Dagu Waterway and power plant provided water and electricity for the ice making industry, while an ice factory appeared in Hama Xing Lighthouse, supplying ice for fisheries and also producing food-grade ice. By the 1920s and 30s, ice making technology gradually matured, and the variety of frozen treats increased.[^2]

Ice was not initially a dessert. It was first a foundation for fishery, preservation, and port economics before it more stably entered ice shops, streets, and homes. The Bapu cart can be understood as a microcosm of basic ice infrastructure transforming into daily consumption: freezing capacity leaves the factory, is put into a bucket on a bicycle, and finally becomes a wafer cup in a child's hand.

This transformation also explains why the history of frozen treats is related to transportation history and power history. Without stable ice making, vendors could not obtain sellable ice. Without bicycles, the ice buckets could not reach residential areas; without selling sounds, mobile vendors would struggle to let customers know they were nearby.

A food culture compilation from the Ministry of Agriculture lists Bapu alongside _niangbing_ (shaved ice), Zhizai ice, _menmenbing_, snowflake ice, and egg ice, pointing out that Taiwanese frozen treats appear, transform, or fade according to the era.[^6] Bapu is not an isolated "old-fashioned taste," but one of Taiwan's continuous answers in adjusting between materials, equipment, and consumption methods.

## The Shared Rules of Three Colors, Q-Elasticity, and One Scoop

Common Bapu features colors and flavors like taro, azuki beans, and pineapple. Their coexistence is not merely for aesthetics, but to allow a small wafer cup to contain the thickness of starch, the sandiness of beans, and the sourness of fruit simultaneously. Putting different flavors into the same cup also allows consumers not to commit fully to a single flavor.

"Three colors" is more like a ordering grammar: customers point to flavors by color, and vendors use an ice scoop to arrange different textures in one cup. This is a small-scale, low-barrier sharing. Children could choose their favorite color, and adults could select different flavors in the same cup.

Some early Bapu stalls even turned purchasing into a game. A special feature from the National Taiwan Museum of History records that some Bapu carts had airplane revolving platforms where customers placed bets. If they won, they could use a giant ice scoop to dig out a large ice ball called "Tianba Wang" (Heavenly Overlord).[^4]

This game shows that the value of Bapu is not just in taste. Waiting, betting, watching the ice scoop fall, and sharing the results are all part of eating the ice. One scoop can be eaten, but the game extends the purchase into a gathering. Vendors sold not just cold sweets, but a set of rules inviting onlookers to participate.

### From Revolving Platform to "Winning Ice"

The "pinball machine" can help modern readers quickly imagine the carts that turned buying ice into a game, but the more precise term in existing museum records is "airplane revolving platform." The National Taiwan Museum of History records that early Bapu carts had airplane revolving platforms where customers placed bets. If they won, they could use a giant ice scoop to dig out a large ice ball called "Tianba Wang."[^4]

Therefore, what was won was not just an abstract "prize": it could be more scoops, a larger ice ball, or turning a single purchase into an event worth watching. This set of rules was not the same for every cart, nor can "pinball machine" be standardized as equipment for all Bapu carts. A more reliable description is that Bapu carts integrated spinning, betting, luck, and scooping into a "Winning Ice" game.

Another visual record from Xiaoliuqu shows language of gifting different from "Tianba Wang." Photographer Li Jianlong captured _Xiaoliuqu—Bapu Grandma_ on December 10, 2011, with the photo caption stating "Handsome Guy gives 2 scoops" and "Beauty gives 2 scoops," describing how the grandmother befriended tourists with Bapu.[^12] This is a photo description from a specific vendor in a particular scene and cannot be extrapolated as a universal rule. However, it does remind us that the gameness of Bapu is sometimes completed not by mechanical devices, but by gifting, appellation, and on-site interaction.

## How Frozen Treats Circulated in Local Networks

The case of the Zhunan Cool Ice Shop brings preservation back to a still-operating establishment. The Hakka Cultural Heritage Digital Network records that this shop, established during the Japanese era and opened in front of Zhunan Train Station in 1947 (ROC Year 36), preserved ice buckets and ice scoops. In the early days, large buckets held Bapu, small buckets held Zhizai ice, and the buckets were also distributed to vendors at the train platform, general stores, and school welfare societies.[^9]

These containers show that the circulation of frozen treats once relied on a decentralized network of places for refrigeration. The ice shop was the supply side; the train station was a traffic node; platform vendors brought ice to travelers, while general stores and school welfare societies placed short-term low-temperature goods into daily life.

From this perspective, the Bapu cart was not moving in isolation. Behind it was a chain of relationships including the ice factory, the ice shop, tool suppliers, bicycle repair shops, wafer cups, and flavor materials. When any link in this chain changed, the vendor's route and costs would change accordingly.

This local network also had aspects of gender and family division of labor. Existing oral records most easily leave the image of "the grandfather selling ice," but preparing ingredients, cleaning, bookkeeping, caring for children, and maintaining the shop were not necessarily all done by the seller standing on the street. Without sufficient primary data, we cannot assume that household chores or female labor have been proven. What is certain is that the vendor seen on the street was often just one endpoint of the entire supply network that was most visible.

## From Cart to Shop: The Family Process Behind One Scoop of Ice

The mobility of Bapu does not mean it lacked a fixed production base. Taking Yongfu Ice Cream in Ximen, Taipei as an example, the first generation Wu Yong-fu initially sold three-color ice on a cart, later settled at a street corner, and then moved into a fixed shop due to urban management and changes in vendor environments. This path reminds us: the history of street frozen treats is not a linear evolution of "cart disappearing, shop replacing," but rather the same set of flavors, recipes, and interpersonal credit finding ways to continue selling in different spaces.[^13]

The family memory of Yongfu also supplements the parts of Bapu production that are easily overlooked. Taro needed to be cooked and ground into a paste before entering the low-temperature freezing machine. Azuki beans were often soaked in advance, and ingredients could only be combined with the ice base after cooling. Reports state that family members started preparing ingredients early in the morning; one pot of azuki beans might take several hours of slow cooking, and on busy days, this process had to be repeated multiple times. These processes make "old-fashioned taste" not just a flavor description, but a production technology maintained by time, heat, and physical labor.[^13]

The temperature and tools used for freezing also changed the texture. Yongfu's case involved rapid cooling in a freezing machine at around -35 degrees Celsius, which made the ice gradually denser as it rubbed against the machine wall. In the past, shaking with ice and coarse salt was sometimes used to cool.[^14] This does not mean one method is necessarily more traditional than another; rather, it shows that Bapu continuously adjusted between materials and equipment: retaining the texture brought by starch and natural ingredients while using more stable freezing tools to meet the demands of modern shops.[^13] [^14]

The ice scoop also has its own history. Cultural reports indicate that early copper scoops were handcrafted by artisans in the Changhua Flower Garden area, with different sizes serving both daily portions and differentiating the size of the ice ball after a "revolving platform" game. As the craftsmen who made the scoops gradually decreased, what disappeared was not just an old tool, but an entire technological chain from metal forming and portion control to street game rules.[^13]

## One Ice: Several Industrial Transformations

Bapu has also undergone transitions from apprenticeship inheritance to family takeover, and finally facing competition from modern brands. Reports on Yongfu Ice Cream mention that the first generation learned ice making from Japanese masters. Before the masters left Taiwan post-war, the recipes and techniques were passed to apprentices, who later transformed them into their own cart and shop business. This transmission is not an abstract "Japanese influence," but a specific transfer of technology happening in the workplace: who taught how to cook ingredients, how to control freezing, and how to judge if a bucket of ice could be sold.[^13]

Around the 1980s, European and American ice cream brands entered Taiwan, putting pressure on many local shops that relied on wholesale from ice factories. The response of Taiwanese ice was not a complete imitation of dairy ice cream but a path retaining a lighter profile, not centered on creamy richness, establishing identity through taro, azuki beans, peanuts, longan, or seasonal fruits. This choice made Bapu not just an "old taste that is preserved," but a product that reasserted its value through materials and texture in competition.[^13] [^14]

From the English version of official introductions, Taiwanese ice cream today is still placed on a continuous spectrum of traditional shops, local ingredients, and new flavor creations, rather than just being treated as nostalgic items in museums.[^15] This perspective is important: for Bapu to continue, it does not have to remain solely on a bicycle weaving through alleys. It can also retain the ability to make ingredients into shareable small portions in shops, exhibitions, local imagery, and the daily labor of new generations of operators.

## Names, Flavors, and Local Differences

"Bapu" is first an auditory word, then a food name. It condenses the sequence of the vendor pressing the rubber ball, the sound passing through alleys, and customers leaving their homes into two syllables. But this does not mean that all ice products across Taiwan use the same name, recipe, or set of sales rules. Different sources show Bapu, three-color ice, taro ice, azuki bean ice, and egg ice side-by-side; names sometimes refer to the product, sometimes to the selling method, and sometimes to a local memory of a generation.[^6] [^13]

Flavors are not fixed in a three-color pattern. The early Yongfu Ice Cream's three colors included taro, azuki beans, and egg. Egg was actually a common name due to its pale yellow appearance, possibly corresponding to vanilla flavor. Another set of local records mentions combinations like taro, azuki beans, and pineapple. To treat one color scheme as the sole authentic one would obscure Bapu's variability depending on materials, shops, seasons, and suppliers.[^6] [^13]

## Cold Chain, Hygiene, and Time Limits for One Scoop of Ice

The cold chain of Bapu is usually not maintained by a complete freezer, delivery truck, and shop equipment like modern chain ice cream. Early vendors had to compete for time using the ice bucket, inner liner, cloth padding, and the ice itself. "Cooling" here is not an abstract technical term, but a countdown from picking up goods at the ice factory to stopping at the cart every day. If the ice melted, it was not just a change in taste, but a direct loss that could not be returned to the ice factory.[^1]

Therefore, the hygiene conditions of street Bapu cannot be imagined solely by today's food factory standards, nor can they be romanticized as "more natural before." What is certain is that cleaning the ice bucket, contact with the ice scoop, preservation of the wafer cup, cooking ingredients, and sales time are all practical issues related to taste and safety. However, existing oral records and collection pages do not provide a unified hygiene standard representing all vendors in Taiwan. A more rigorous approach is to view it as a low-temperature labor constrained by equipment, weather, journey, and shop experience, rather than endorsing all old-fashioned stalls.[^1] [^9] [^13]

## Portion Size, Price, and the Social Scale of "One Scoop"

A single scoop of ice seems like a small purchase, but it is actually influenced by the size of the ice scoop, ingredient costs, weather, foot traffic, and game rules. The appeal of "Tianba Wang" lies in placing an ordinary scoop and a giant ice ball within the same transaction. Customers bought not just a fixed weight, but potentially an increased amount due to luck. The mention of "gives 2 scoops" in Xiaoliuqu imagery also shows that gifting could be a way for individual vendors to build relationships with customers, but this cannot be presumed as a uniform offer from all stalls.[^4] [^12]

Existing sources do not provide enough continuous annual price lists, so it is inappropriate to state the unit price of one era as representative of Bapu across Taiwan. What can be confirmed is that Bapu, with its wafer cups, small portions, multiple flavors, and gamified portion sizes, allowed children to participate in street consumption. "Buying one scoop" thus became a child's allowance from home, a sharing among companions, and a visible unit for the vendor to judge the day's business.[^1] [^4] [^13]

## Urban Governance and Mobile Vendors' Space

The Bapu cart needs to move, but it does not mean it can stay permanently in any location. The shop history of Yongfu Ice Cream shows that after Taipei was upgraded, the conditions for setting up a stall at a street corner changed, leading the first generation to purchase a small "stair corner" as a fixed base. This example should not be simplified into mere urban improvement or traditional decline. It shows how urban management, land use, and consumer traffic collectively change the operating space of vendors, forcing a business that relied on routes to transition to a shop.[^13]

The shift from street to shop also changes the relationship between sound and customers. Mobile vendors must actively announce "I am here," while fixed shops can rely on signs, addresses, and repeat customers. The former turns the community into a mobile market; the latter compresses the mobile market into a recognizable storefront. The disappearance of Bapu culture is therefore not just the reduction in the number of carts, but also the sound no longer being part of residents' daily schedules.[^3] [^10] [^13]

## Children's Consumption and Invisible Family Support

Bapu is often remembered as a joy after school for children, but Fan Yang-kai's case reminds us that the child might also be the vendor. Carrying an ice bucket after school, traveling between the ice factory and home, and bearing the risk of melted ice in response to weather are two sides of the same street economy as "children buying ice with pocket money." If only photos of children gathered around the cart remain, the experience of labor for children is easily excluded from memory.[^1]

On the other hand, family inheritance in ice shops is not solely carried out by those standing at the counter. Preparing azuki beans, processing taro, cleaning buckets, making ice cream cakes, and managing the shop are often distributed among different family members. However, individual reports cannot directly infer gender division of labor for all Taiwanese ice shops. A more cautious conclusion is that Bapu, as a street product, has a set of preparation, cleaning, bookkeeping, and care tasks that are easily obscured by the selling sound.[^13]

## What Images Preserve and What They Do Not

Modern images allow us to see the 1987 Bapu cart, the 2011 Xiaoliuqu vendors, and the 2014 taro ice carts in Kong Miao Old Street, Tainan. They preserve the vehicle body, flags, horns, clothing, streetscape, and the viewer's position at the time of shooting.[^11] [^12] But photos usually cannot tell us the ice recipe, daily price, complete vendor route, income, hygiene process, or the work of those not photographed. Images are important evidence, but they are not a complete life history.

Therefore, the preservation of Bapu cannot just increase the number of photos; it must also retain the image description, shooting date, location, author, license, and oral context. For a street photo, "who took it," "where it was taken," and "what the people in the frame are doing" are equally important. Only by grounding the images in specific time and place can later readers avoid piecing together different generations, vendors, and game rules into a single non-existent tradition.[^11] [^12]

## How Bapu Remains When the Street Disappears

Bapu can still be found in some tourist areas, night markets, and local ice shops, and is often encountered with ways of eating like peanut rolled ice cream.[^5] A news compilation from CCTV on Taiwanese frozen treat history also explained the difference between Bapu and ordinary dairy ice cream through the process of thickening with cassava starch using taro, azuki beans, and pineapple.[^7]

On October 29, 2014, a user named Bee in Digital Island photographed _Kong Miao Old Taro Ice_ in Zhongxi District, Tainan City: a vendor wearing a conical hat cycled past the houses opposite Kong Miao with an "Ice" flag attached to the side of the cart. The photo description specifically mentioned that the author often heard Bapu sounds in childhood and could still occasionally see grandpas selling Bapu in Kong Miao Old Street.[^11]

The value of this photo is not just that it "proves Bapu still exists." It grounds the preservation issue in a specific place: Kong Miao Old Street is not an abstract background of old tastes, but a street in Zhongxi District, Tainan City, where pedestrians, shops, flags, and bicycles intersect. When Bapu appears here, mobile vending has not completely returned to the past, but has found a new stop within the modern traffic of a tourist area.[^11]

But "still existing" does not mean the original life scene is fully preserved. When people look for Bapu in fixed shops, festival markets, or exhibitions, the ice product remains, but the rhythm of mobile vending and sound arrival may be detached.

The 2022 _Declaration to the Sun_ exhibition from Kaohsiung reassembles Bapu carts, Zhizai ice, egg ice, and old-fashioned shaved ice machines through an exhibition, allowing different generations to look back at the history of eating ice through tools.[^2] This preservation is not about sealing Bapu into a "most authentic" year, but acknowledging that it belongs simultaneously to the port, the ice factory, the school route, and today's cultural venue.

Preserving Bapu cannot just preserve the appearance of "three-color ice." If only the colors remain, the most powerful cultural structure is lost: a child carrying an ice bucket, a horn passing through the street, and a group of people temporarily gathering for one scoop.

The real challenge is not determining which scoop is the oldest, but how to preserve the recognizable relationships during transformation: the ice must have a describable texture, the cart must have an understandable labor, the horn must have a sound that reminds people of the street corner, and the story must explain who delivered it to the customer, where, and at what cost.

## The Afterglow of One Scoop of Ice

The counterintuitive aspect of Bapu is that its texture is extended by starch, but its life is sustained by movement. Starch makes the ice a recognizable Taiwanese taste; the bicycle and the horn require constant movement. On one side is the control of water by materials; on the other is the vendor's bearing of time, weather, and route.

The buckets from Xianglin in 1947, the exhibition in Hama Xing in 2022, and the 20.5 cm selling horn in the museum do not form an unbroken nostalgic straight line. There are industrial changes, disappearing labor, and processes of local memory being re-collected between them.[^1] [^2] [^3]

When we hear "Bapu" again, we can still discern what it asks us to do: leave indoors and walk toward the sound. Complete a shared choice before the ice melts. What Bapu preserves is not an unchanging ice, but a method of food meeting people on the street.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The most worthy thing about Bapu to keep is not the unprovable nostalgia of "tasted better in the past," but that path which can be seen again: from the ice factory to the bucket, the bucket to the bicycle, the horn to the street corner, and finally into the scoop.
