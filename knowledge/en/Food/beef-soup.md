At 2:00 AM, the lights are still on at the Shanhua slaughterhouse. Today is the turn for cattle that arrived the previous night to be slaughtered. The workers divide their tasks: bleeding, skinning, and butchering; the steam from the _wēntǐ_ (warm-body) beef rises outside the cold storage room. Before dawn, refrigerated trucks must deliver the meat to various beef soup shops across Tainan City. The bowl you drink at 5:00 AM contains beef that was alive only a few hours ago.

This logic is not about romance. It is logistics.

## Not an Ancient Tradition

The "legend" of Tainan beef soup is mostly a matter of the last few decades.

During the Qing Dynasty, Taiwanese farmers did not eat beef—cattle were agricultural tools; killing them meant losing the strength to plow fields. During the Japanese colonial period, the Japanese government promoted Western-style diets, and only then did the people of Taiwan gradually become accustomed to putting beef on the dining table. After 1945, a large number of Chaoshan immigrants arrived in Taiwan, bringing with them the Chaoshan logic of beef cuisine: warm, quick-blanched, and not overcooked.

What truly made "beef soup" a Tainan trademark was the Tainan Beef Festival, which began in 2004—a marketing decision that packaged existing dietary habits into a city symbol. It has been just over twenty years.

📝 Curator's Note: The widespread consumption of beef among Taiwanese people is something that only emerged during the Japanese colonial period. In the early years after the retrocession, people ate retired draft cattle or diseased cattle; later, a culinary culture centered on fresh, warm-body meat developed around the periphery of farming areas—that sense of "tradition" is memory replacing history.

## Shanhua: The Starting Point of Everything

Shanhua was once one of Taiwan's three major cattle markets, with cattle trading beginning as early as the 1870s. Today's meat market in Shanhua District is the largest cattle slaughterhouse in Taiwan.

The warm-body supply chain for Tainan beef soup unfolds here: slaughtering takes place the night before, butchering is completed in the early morning hours, and refrigerated trucks deliver the warm beef to various shops in the city before dawn. No freezing, no overnight storage; the entire process is completed within a few hours.

Approximately 95% of the cattle supplied are male dairy cows. Dairy cows have large frames but relatively less meat; they need to be raised to about 550 kg or more to meet slaughter standards, though they typically enter the facility at 600 to 700 kg. The ranches are mainly distributed around the Beigang area in Yunlin and the periphery of Shanhua, Tainan.

📝 Curator's Note: The raw material for Tainan beef soup is almost entirely dairy cows, not beef cattle. Dairy cows have a longer fattening period and different muscle fiber structures; combined with being freshly sliced while warm, this is the source of the sweetness in Tainan soup—it is not sugar in the broth, but the breed and the timing of the cow.

## The Meaning of "Warm-Body"

The two characters for _wēntǐ_ (warm-body) refer to more than just temperature.

Immediately after slaughter, the muscle fibers of warm-body beef remain intact, retaining a firm elasticity from the moment life ended. When you bite into it, there is resistance, there is sound—Tainan people describe that texture as "ka-zi ka-zi" (crunchy/snappy). After frozen beef is thawed, the fibers are broken and cell walls are ruptured; the texture is soft, making it a different kind of food entirely.

After you order, the owner personally slices the meat on the spot, places the slices into the boiling broth for a few seconds, and serves it—the center is still bright red. At this moment, there can be no hesitation—pick it up and eat immediately. If you wait a few minutes, the residual heat will overcook the meat.

## The Broth is Each Shop's Secret

The clear broth is the base, but each shop's recipe is private.

Some add onions, some add ginger, some add sugarcane or pineapple to enhance sweetness, and some use Chinese medicinal herbs as a foundation—simmering in large pots since dawn to extract their respective layers of flavor. Identifying a good bowl of soup does not depend on the beef (the warm-body process has already handled half that task), but on the depth of its clear sweetness: how long the umami lingers in your mouth after drinking the soup.

📝 Curator's Note: Tainan locals can distinguish the differences in broth between famous shops with a single sip. Shops like Liuqian Beef Soup and Ayu Beef Soup each have their devotees; the difference lies not in the beef, but in each shop's different answer to "where does the sweetness come from?"

## Closed on Mondays

Tainan beef soup shops are usually closed on Mondays—not by custom, but by logic.

The Shanhua meat market does not slaughter cattle on Mondays. Without fresh warm-body beef for the day, shops cannot open. This weekly closure pattern is more honest than any restaurant announcement: it directly reveals where the lifeblood of this dish lies. The most common mistake made by those visiting Tainan for beef soup for the first time is arriving on a Monday.

## The Difference Between the First Pot and the Evening

Some say the sweetness of the "first pot" is different from that of the evening; this is true.

After slaughtering is complete, the freshness of warm-body beef decreases over time—not because it spoils, but because the muscle fibers begin to relax. If you secure the first pot at 5:00 AM, you are drinking the sweetness of the muscle in its firmest state; if you go in the evening, even at the same shop with the same master, that level of texture is a different matter entirely.

📝 Curator's Note: The "first pot culture" of Tainan beef soup is not about prestige, but about the physics of time—the few hours of peak freshness versus the same piece of meat five or six hours later constitutes two different ingredients. Waking up early is the entry ticket itself.

## A Recognizable Form of Honesty

Tainan beef soup is an honest food.

It does not rely on sauces for coverage, nor marination for adjustment, nor cooking techniques to compensate for inferior ingredients. The broth has been simmering for hours, the beef was alive just hours ago; the bowl served to you is the truth of that specific moment. You know immediately whether it is good or not.

This is also why it can only be eaten in Tainan. It is not that the chef's skill cannot be transported; it is that the supply chain cannot be moved.
