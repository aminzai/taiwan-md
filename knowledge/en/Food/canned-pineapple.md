> **30-Second Overview:** Today when people talk about pineapples, many first think of pineapple cakes or Gold Diamond pineapples. But for a long period after the war, what truly earned foreign exchange for Taiwan was can after can of pineapple in syrup. The first pineapple canning factory appeared in Kaohsiung's Fengshan in 1902; by 1969, Taiwan's total exports reached 4.43 million cases. Later this industry lost out to lower-wage Southeast Asia and Taiwan's own fresh fruit market, leaving behind not mere nostalgia but a path running from farmland, female workers, and production lines all the way to the world market.[^1] [^2] [^6]

## Starting with a Single Can of Pineapple

If you visited a sick friend in the 1950s, you might have brought not flowers but a can of pineapple. Supplies were still tight then, and pineapple cans were treated as precious gifts. Company welfare cooperatives would purchase in bulk and resell to employees. Photos preserved in the National Archives even record the Taiwan Provincial Tobacco and Liquor Monopoly Bureau purchasing pineapple cans.[^1]

This object is small, but the industry behind it was large. It had to go through pineapples in the fields, transported to factories by rail or oxcart, workers slicing and canning, tinplate that could seal out oxygen, and finally loaded onto ships. The story of pineapple cans therefore belongs not only to the dining table, but also to the food industry and the export economy.

> **Curator's Note:** What is most worth remembering about pineapple cans is that they once turned "fruit that must be eaten quickly" into "a commodity that could wait for ships, wait for markets, wait for the nation's needs."

## 1902: A Factory in Fengshan

Taiwan's history of pineapple cultivation traces back to the Kangxi era of the Qing dynasty, but early native varieties bore small fruit with high acidity; besides fresh eating, they were also utilized for their fiber. What truly changed their fate was processing: in 1902, Japanese merchant Okamura Shotaro established Taiwan's first pineapple canning factory in Kaohsiung's Fengshan, giving pineapples the possibility of long-distance transport and overseas sales.[^1] [^3]

Okamura saw not just flavor. Pineapples are heat-tolerant, retaining flavor after heating. The climate and soil of southern and central Taiwan were also suitable for cultivation. During the Japanese colonial period, the Smooth Cayenne variety was introduced from abroad, gradually turning pineapples originally unsuited for canning into raw materials with more stable specifications.[^5] [^10]

In 1903, Taiwan pineapples won an award at the Osaka Exposition, and products began to be recognized by the Japanese market. Early cans only peeled and sliced; later processes developed for coring, removing the core, high-temperature sterilization, and cooling. Even pineapple cores were processed into candied fruit, becoming a sweet taste children could afford in times of scarcity.[^4]

This path was not smooth. Around 1930, there were too many factories with uneven quality, and Smooth Cayenne encountered wilt disease. Research indicates that in 1930, Taiwan's pineapple factories once reached 81. The 1935 formation of a joint-stock company centralized production and sales management, raising standardization, but also made Taiwanese small factories and farmers more vulnerable to price and institutional control.[^3] [^10]

## Postwar: Sweetness Connected to the National Machinery

War destroyed factories and supply chains; tinplate shortages also made canning production difficult to restore. The postwar government listed pineapples as a commodity for agricultural revival and earning foreign exchange, encouraging farmers to plant, supporting factories, and obtaining canning materials through U.S. aid. In 1955, the pineapple enterprise under the Taiwan Provincial Agricultural and Forestry Department was privatized, establishing the Taiwan Pineapple Corporation, and the industry expanded again.[^1] [^2]

What truly pushed this export road eastward was Taiwan Sugar (Tai-tang). In 1955, Taiwan Sugar established a pineapple factory in Taitung, partly to create jobs for farmers, partly to keep the Taitung Sugar Factory producing during the sugar off-season. The factory occupied 8,160 square meters, began production on July 11, 1957, under the brand "Tang-feng" or Taisuco.[^6]

From field to can, pineapples went through a precise and labor-intensive order. Farmers delivered fruit by oxcart or narrow-gauge train. After machines peeled and cored, five production lines were lined on both sides by female workers in white uniforms, caps, and gloves, trimming black spots piece by piece, then slicing, canning, adding syrup, sealing, sterilizing, and labeling.[^1] [^7]

Here is a group of figures easily overlooked: female workers. They were not background beside the production line, but the judges machines could not replace. Every slice of pineapple had to pass under their eyes and knives; the factory therefore provided shuttle buses, dormitories, and three meals, attracting women from various townships into two-shift production. CNA footage preserved from 1958 still lets us see that Taitung summer production line supported by masses of female workers.[^2] [^7]

> **Curator's Note:** If "industrialization" leaves only factories, equipment, and export figures, it misses the most important layer: whose hands turned agricultural products into commodities the world could trust?

## After Two Ships Were Rejected

What Tang-feng first faced was not output but hygiene. Taiwan Sugar sent two shiploads of pineapple cans to the United States, only to be refused entry due to excessive mold contamination. This failure forced the government to assemble the Joint Commission on Rural Reconstruction (JCRR), National Taiwan University, and other specialists into a 28-person task force, reforming the factory item by item—from window screens, water quality, and toilets to female worker hygiene training.[^6]

In 1959, Tang-feng finally passed U.S. inspection. This turning point is interesting: Taiwan did not first have "world-class" quality and then obtain the market. It was only after cargo ships were rejected that hygiene standards, factory equipment, and labor processes were remade into a system capable of exporting.[^6]

Numbers quickly rose. Taiwan's pineapple can exports grew from 830,000 cases in 1957 to 1.74 million cases in 1959. In 1969 they reached 4.43 million cases. The Taitung Sugar Factory pineapple factory set a record of 850,000 cases annual output in 1964, becoming one of Taiwan's largest pineapple factories.[^6] [^7]

Research and official historical statistics use different calibers; some record "total Taiwan exports," others "single factory annual output," and they cannot be added together. What is certain is that from the 1950s to the early 1970s, pineapple cans linked agriculture, production lines, ports, foreign exchange, and eastern Taiwan development into a single industrial chain.[^1] [^6] [^8]

## World's Third, Then Switching Paths

In the late Japanese colonial period, Taiwan's pineapple processing was once the world's third-largest center. Postwar it also stood among the front ranks of canned pineapple exports. The Ministry of Culture introduces the Dashu Taifang Pineapple Canning Factory as Taiwan's surviving Japanese-era pineapple canning industrial architecture. In the 1930s Taiwan had 81 pineapple factories; after the war only 4 remained. Restored in 2017 and reopened in 2018, the pineapple factory now displays over 50 historical can labels.[^3]

The industry later lost to two changes. The Philippines, Thailand, and other places had lower production costs, while Taiwan's wages rose. Simultaneously, Taiwan's own consumers began preferring fresh, high-sweetness, bred pineapple varieties. Academic research indicates that from 1956 to 1976 pineapples were mainly used for export cans, peaking around 1971, after which the industry turned toward the domestic fresh fruit market.[^5] [^8]

After 1986, breeding work's focus clearly shifted to fresh-eating varieties. A 2011 study records that in 2008 Taiwan planted approximately 10,613 hectares of pineapples, with fresh fruit output of 452,060 metric tons, and Tainong No. 17 held over 90% market share. Pineapples did not leave Taiwan's soil; they just changed from "export cans" to "domestic fresh fruit."[^11]

This also explains why today's pineapple memories often skip cans and jump straight to pineapple cakes, Gold Diamond pineapples, and the New Year's "wang-lai" (prosperity comes). The pineapple cake article can explain how a souvenir globalized. Pineapple cans leave another question: how does a country, when resources are limited, turn a fruit into factories, jobs, and foreign exchange?

## A Can's Resume: From Orchard to Port

| Places a Can of Pineapple Must Pass Through         | What It Does in the Industrial Chain                                              |
| --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Orchards in Fengshan, Dashu, Yuanlin, and Taitung   | Provide pineapple raw materials suitable for processing or contract farming       |
| Peeling, coring, and slicing production lines       | Turn seasonal fruit into standardized contents                                    |
| Female workers' trimming stations and sampling labs | Supplement the black-spot and hygiene judgments machines cannot make              |
| Tinplate cans and syrup                             | Extend shelf life, letting commodities wait for rail, ports, and overseas markets |
| Can labels and export orders                        | Connect a local factory to consumers in Japan, the U.S., and Europe               |

This table looks like a factory process, but it is also a map of Taiwan. Fengshan and Dashu explain the industry's early starting points; Yuanlin and Changhua explain the density of central Taiwan raw materials and factories; Taitung shows how the postwar state used food processing as a tool for eastern development.[^1] [^3] [^6]

Tinplate is especially critical. Fruit rots, but iron cans stretch time. Yet tinplate also plugged Taiwan into international politics: wartime steel embargoes caused canning material shortages, and postwar U.S. aid became a key condition for restoring production.[^1] [^2]

Therefore, the "sweetness" of pineapple cans is not the whole story. Syrup only enters the can at the end; before that came variety selection, farmer contracts, worker training, equipment investment, and export inspection. Before food reaches the table, it has already undergone an order jointly maintained by state, enterprise, and labor.

## Cans Haven't Disappeared, Just Become Heritage

National Cheng Kung University research places Taiwan's canning industry in a longer industrial history: canning factories increased from 10 in 1946 to 249 in 1978; after 1990 the production system disintegrated, and today only a few old factories still produce traditional tinplate cans. Pineapple was the starting point of this industrial chain, later extending to mushrooms, asparagus, bamboo shoots, water chestnuts, ponkan oranges, tomatoes, and aquatic products.[^9]

So pineapple cans are not merely "an old flavor eliminated by the times." They let us see that Taiwan's industrialization once grew inside a fruit: farmland had to be reorganized, varieties improved, tinplate obtained, female workers trained, products had to pass foreign inspection, and only then could a place prosper because of a single factory.

Today walking into the Dashu pineapple factory, one sees exhibitions, labels, and restored red-brick architecture. In Taitung, the Tang-feng factory leaves memories of local development and labor. Cans are no longer Taiwan's most important pineapple commodity, yet they demonstrated for later fresh pineapples, pineapple cakes, and other processed foods one thing: once food is made into a form that can be preserved, transported, and inspected, it begins to participate in a society's destiny.

Pineapple cans also paved the way for other foods. NCKU research notes that after mushroom canning trial production succeeded in 1957, mushrooms, asparagus, bamboo shoots, water chestnuts, ponkan oranges, tomatoes, and aquatic products successively joined the processing system. Cans were no longer just pineapple's single story, but became a factory language of the postwar "using agriculture to nurture industry, using industry to develop agriculture."[^9]

This language still hides in today's food: what you see in convenience stores are expiration dates, weights, and inspection labels. In old factories you see tinplate, conveyor belts, and worker positions. Half a century separates them, yet both answer the same question: how to let a local taste, after leaving its place of origin, still be recognizable, tradable, and remembered.
