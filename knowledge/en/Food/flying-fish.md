> **30-Second Overview**: Every February or March, Dasao men from Orchid Island sail out in silver helmets and torches to summon flying fish that migrate with the Kuroshio Current; the flying fish festival encompasses about eleven rituals, running from the summoning ceremony to the final consumption ritual after Mid-Autumn Festival. During this period, the catching, cooking, sharing, and storage of flying fish are governed by strict taboos—this fish-centric system has been registered as an intangible cultural heritage by the Ministry of the Interior.

On the coast of Orchid Island in March, night torches burn upon black volcanic reefs. Dasao (Yami) men sail out in outrigger canoes, their torches reflecting on the sea surface—this is not a tourist performance, but the most important event of their year: summoning the flying fish that migrate with the Kuroshio Current every spring [^1] [^2].

One flying fish dictates when the Orchid Islanders can catch, who can eat, how it must be cooked, and when eating must cease. The rules one must follow when eating a single fish are more numerous than any cookbook, and the origin of these rules is a dream.

## The Dream: A Code Narrated by Fish

In the oral tradition of the Dasao people, descendants of the creator ancestor "Shi Sheng Ren" live by the sea. After the tide recedes, seafood remains on the sand, which they all throw into one pot to cook. They eat everything and develop a strange skin condition that is intensely itchy. In desperation, the fish deity "Hei Chi" (Black Wing) appears in a dream and instructs him item by item: what types of flying fish there are, how each type is caught, how it is killed, dried, prepared, eaten, what must not be done, and which rituals must be performed [^2].

The structure of this myth is far stricter than ordinary folklore—it is not "god created man," but rather "the fish promulgated the rules, and humans follow them to avoid sickness." The reason recorded by the Ministry of the Interior when registering the Yami (Dasao) flying fish festival as an intangible cultural heritage is precisely this sentence:

> Catching flying fish is a major event in the year for the Yami people; the entire focus of life revolves around the activity of catching flying fish, and flying fish are even an expression of faith and worldview. Therefore, it can be said that the tribe and the flying fish coexist.[^2]

📝 Curator's Note: Most human cultures follow the pattern "humans eat fish," but the Dasao culture follows "the fish makes a contract, and humans keep the promise." This is one of the few cultures in the world where the prey itself legislates.

## Three Types of Flying Fish: Hierarchy on the Dining Table

Flying fish are not just one type of fish. The "flying fish" in Taiwan's waters is mainly from the genus _Cheilopogon_. The Dasao people divide them into three categories based on tradition and practice, with a strict hierarchy that prevents mixing [^3]:

| Dasao Name                                    | Fish Species                  | Order of Appearance               | Status and Consumption Method                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| :-------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------- | :-------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Black-finned flying fish (_mavaeng so panid_) | _Cheilopogon nigricans_       | First to arrive, fewest in number | Most noble, largest size; caught by small boats during the day using shrimp as bait for Ghost Knife fish; after drying, **absolutely must not be roasted over fire**, as legend says eating it causes skin disease. |
| White-finned flying fish (_sosowo'en_)        | _Cheilopogon unicolor_        | Pioneer species                   | Lured at night with torches; after cutting and drying, **it can be roasted over fire**.                                                                                                                             |
| Red-spotted flying fish (_papata'on_)         | _Cheilopogon spilonotopterus_ | Most numerous                     | Attracted by the light of torches in groups; also caught during the day using shrimp bait; both dried and roasted.                                                                                                  |

The Black-finned flying fish is the most tabooed fish: after being dried into jerky, it absolutely cannot be roasted over fire for consumption, or the skin will become diseased [^3]. The methods of eating a single fish vary greatly due to its different types—the hierarchy is embedded in ichthyology.

The Orchid Islanders have a logic of gender and age distribution for all fish: fish are divided into "elderly fish," "male fish," and "female fish." The rough-skinned, smelly "bad fish" (_ra'et a among_) is prioritized for men to eat, while the flavorful, fine-fleshed "good fish" (_oyoda among_) is preferentially given to women and the elderly [^4] [^5]. Beyond flying fish, the dining table is also a mirror of society.

## The Kuroshio Current: The Clock of Flying Fish

These taboos function because the flying fish keeps time itself. Flying fish do not actually fly—they glide from the water surface using their wing-like pectoral and pelvic fins, achieving a glide lasting about one minute each time, with a maximum recorded distance of over 400 meters. Leaping out of the water is not a special trick, but an escape maneuver to avoid underwater predators such as Ghost Knife fish, tuna, and jacks [^12]. There are eighty-one species in the family Molidae; twenty-six species have been recorded in waters around Taiwan, among which the Black-finned, White-finned, and Spotted flying fish are mainly caught on the main island of Taiwan and Orchid Island. The Red-spotted flying fish appears almost exclusively in the waters around Orchid Island, making Orchid Island the most complete stage for flying fish culture in all of Taiwan [^12].

Every spring and summer, flying fish undertake a reproductive migration north with the Kuroshio Current, passing through the eastern coastal waters of Taiwan during the window from March to June—this is the most important protein source for the Orchid Islanders throughout the year [^13] [^14]. The temperature and timing of the Kuroshio Current determine the start and end of the fishing season. The Dasao people do not have a spring on a calendar; their spring is called _Rayon_, meaning "flying fish season." Since flying fish lay eggs attached to seaweed and floating debris, the fine threads in the egg membrane cause the eggs to clump into buoyant masses—later, fishermen in Northeast Taiwan utilized this characteristic to set up straw mats on the water surface to harvest eggs, developing another form of flying fish egg fishing [^15]. The Dasao taboo system is essentially a precise calibration against this Kuroshio Current clock: arriving too early or eating too late constitutes a breach of contract.

## Eleven Rituals in a Year: The Flying Fish Festival is Not "One Day"

What outside tourists call the "flying fish festival" is actually an entire seasonal chain of rituals. The traditional Dasao calendar, _Ahehep no tao_ (Dasao night calendar), uses flying fish catching to distinguish seasons: _Rayon_ is the flying fish season (January to April in the calendar); _Teyteyka_ is the end of flying fish catching (May to August); and _Amyan_ is the future season for flying fish (September to December) [^2]. From the solar calendar perspective, February through September are the seasons for consuming flying fish [^2].

The entire flying fish season has about eleven rituals, divided into three major stages [^2]:

| Stage                     | Time Period       | Representative Ritual                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                            |
| :------------------------ | :---------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Large Boat Ceremony Stage | February to March | Summoning Ceremony (_Mivanwa / meyvanwa_): Men from each tribe go to the sea in the early morning, wearing silver helmets and facing the ocean to summon flying fish; sacrifices are made, and stones are marked with blood.                                                                     |
| Small Boat Ceremony Stage | Around April      | Ghost Knife Fishing Ceremony (_Papataw_): A daytime fishing ceremony for small boats after the official start of the season.                                                                                                                                                                     |
| End-of-Season Stage       | June to September | Storage Ceremony (_Mamoka_): Flying fish jerky and sweet potato are cooked together and shared by the whole family; before eating, family members sing blessings for every fish; Final Consumption Ceremony (_Manoyotoyon_): The last time flying fish jerky is eaten after Mid-Autumn Festival. |

On the day of the Summoning Ceremony, the tribe's boat captain roasts a chicken, and men use their index fingers dipped in chicken blood and pig blood to chant an invitation to the flying fish, then smear the chicken blood onto black stones. After the ritual, elders begin reminding everyone of that year's taboos [^5]. The Dasao Summoning Ceremony is held by each tribe individually: around late February every year, the Red Head tribe in the southernmost location holds it first, and the other tribes follow in a clockwise direction [^12]. In the early morning of the Summoning Ceremony, men wear silver helmets (which were originally war equipment but are now ceremonial attire) and summon schools of fish from their outrigger canoes toward the sea; the silver helmets flash in the firelight—this code promulgated by the fish is recited anew every year by those wearing the silver helmets [^16].

## Dedicated Pots, Whole Fish: The Seven Precepts of Eating Fish

After the flying fish enters the pot, the taboos continue. By comparing data from the Ministry of Agriculture's food and agriculture education materials with Wikipedia entries, a set of quite strict dietary rules can be compiled [^3] [^6]:

The first batch of caught flying fish must be boiled and eaten on the same day. Before the Small Boat Fishing Ceremony (April to May), flying fish can only be boiled; they cannot be roasted or deep-fried—legend says eating fire-roasted flying fish causes skin sores throughout the body [^3]. The fish is seasoned with sea salt for cooking. Flying fish cannot be cooked with other foods, as mixing them causes sickness, so dedicated pots and bowls must be used. The Dasao traditional society had no refrigerators; this rule was also a form of hygienic wisdom to prevent contamination [^3]. When processing the body, three cuts are made on the side to divide it into three pieces; legend says that eating the fish eyes can prevent the fish from "escaping" and not returning to Orchid Island waters. If dried fish is hung upside down, even if corrected later, it cannot be eaten [^6].

Drying the fish itself is an art: viscera and eyes are removed, cleaned, salted for preservation and pest control, and then hung on bamboo poles in the sun [^3]. The hanging methods of each tribe conceal a direction—Lang Island, Dongqing, and Ya'in hang them upright by stringing through the eyes, with the fish belly facing the house and the back facing the sea. Red Head, Fishermen, and Coconut people tie the fish by the belly to make it look like it is flying, with the head facing the house and the tail facing the sea [^3].

The list of taboos is longer than imagined. The oral rules also include: one cannot eat fish caught by another tribe; fish caught oneself can only be exchanged within the tribe, not gifted across tribes; before eating fish, it must not be washed near a stream or on the coast; one cannot start eating flying fish at home before other fish are finished, or misfortune will strike; during fishing, the whole family is forbidden from consuming clams, ginger, beans, and citrus [^14]. The Red-spotted flying fish is a type that is slightly smaller and has two whiskers on its lower jaw, feeding on zooplankton such as copepods—on the dining table, it is the most accessible entry species, suitable for both drying and boiling [^14]. The Spotted flying fish follows another path: it is usually not dried into jerky but cooked directly after scaling [^12]. Each type of fish has its own processing trajectory; even the decision of "whether to dry it" is predetermined.

The life history of the stored flying fish jerky is part of Dasao architecture: the eaves of underground houses and semi-underground houses, and the firewood racks next to the houses, all serve as display racks for the dried fish—after the flying fish season ends, uneaten jerky is hung in these places to be eaten by pigs and dogs, or saved as a reference for the spring of the following year [^1] [^16].

## Stopping Consumption Requires More Ritual Than Starting It

The end of the flying fish season is more meticulous than its beginning. The Storage Ceremony (_Mamoka_) is held at the end of the last month of the fishing season: flying fish jerky and sweet potato are cooked together, shared by the whole family; before eating, family members sing to bless every fish with health and longevity, after which the fins and tails are removed from the jerky and stored in ceramic jars [^5]. Around Mid-Autumn Festival, the Final Consumption Ceremony (_Manoyotoyon_) is held, which is the last time flying fish is eaten all year [^5].

After the Final Consumption Ceremony, no one may eat flying fish. Any dried flying fish that cannot be finished cannot be discarded casually—it must be hung on a firewood rack next to the house for pigs and dogs to eat [^1]. The prohibition is lifted only after the Summoning Ceremony in the spring of the following year. The "service time" of one flying fish is precisely mapped out by ritual; over-seasoned jerky is an expired contract.

📝 Curator's Note: "Stopping consumption" was designed as a festival, which is an ancient version of resource management—in Dasao culture, flying fish is not an ingredient but a guest who reports annually.

## Transformation Under Tourism Pressure, From Orchid Island to Hualien

Flying fish culture is not exclusive to the Dasao people. From April to June every year, the Yami people along the coasts of Hualien and Taitung also catch flying fish: they cut open the fish, remove the viscera, soak them in saltwater for several hours, and then smoke roast them for four to eight hours using charcoal, adding flavor with moonflower stems [^3]. In Orchid Island, tourism is changing the boundaries of this system—after the establishment of the Orchid Island Flying Fish Culture Center in 2005, night catching by torches transformed from a tribal ritual into an activity on the tourist itinerary [^6]. A documentation project commissioned by the Marine National Park Management Office in 2014 interviewed thirty-seven members from each tribe because the community worried that core knowledge existed only in the memories of the elders [^7].

Scholar Xia Man-lanbo put this world into the fish schools within Cold Water Mountain—in her writing, the relationship between the Orchid Islanders and flying fish was never "catching fish," but a mutual gaze lasting over seven hundred nights [^8].

One fish gave an island its own calendar. When tourists' torches illuminate the coast of Orchid Island, the real question is: can those rules, which began with a dream and were narrated by the fish, still take effect in the hands of the next generation?
