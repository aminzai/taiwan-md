> **30-Second Overview:** The story of Formosa Chang is about a bowl of "rice born from scolding." In 1960, Yunlin carpenter Chang Yen-chuan set up a street stall at Taipei's Shuanglian Market. His signature braised pork rice was criticized by the head chef of the Black Beauty Grand Restaurant as being tasteless; instead, this criticism birthed quality ironclad rules: "rice must be newly harvested, meat must be sliced, and soy sauce must be pure-brewed." The second generation, Chang Yung-chang, registered his father's nickname as a brand, learned from McDonald's, abandoned franchising for a fully direct-operated model, and sent braised pork rice flying on China Airlines in-flight meals. As of 2026, the signature small bowl of braised pork rice has maintained its 39 TWD price for eight years, while the brand's greatest competitors are actually 7-Eleven and FamilyMart.

## The face on the sign is a nickname given by customers

When speaking of Taipei's braised pork rice, one cannot bypass the red-and-white lightbox of Ningxia Night Market. The smiling face on the sign—with wrinkles on both cheeks and a thick beard—is actually a portrait of the founder, Chang Yen-chuan. It began as a nickname passed down by customers, which the second generation registered as a trademark; today, it hangs above over seventy stores in Taiwan and in Shibuya, Tokyo. The shop started in 1960 as "Shuanglian Braised Pork Rice" opposite the Shuanglian Police Station on Minsheng West Road. In 2010, its braised pork rice flew to 40,000 feet on China Airlines' US West Coast routes; the contrast between this street-side folk food and a trans-Pacific flight is a microcosm of Formosa Chang's sixty-six years. Three counter-intuitive facts exist within this bowl of rice. First, the brand name "Bearded Chang" (Húxǔ Zhāng) was not chosen by the founder, but was a nickname given to a vendor who was too busy to shave [^1] [^2]. Second, what transformed this shop was not the praise of a patron, but a public scolding from a famous chef: "How can you even sell rice like this?"[^3] [^4]. Third, in 2012, it faced public backlash due to a two-yuan price increase, leading the Taipei City Government to suspend its food promotion partnership[^5]; yet fourteen years later, it used the "small bowl of braised pork rice frozen at 39 TWD for eight years" as a defense that became a media talking point[^6].

## The Carpenter's Cart: From Planes to Braising Pots (1960–1979)

In 1960, Chang Yen-chuan, a carpenter from Yunlin making a living through manual woodworking, saw mechanized sawing gradually replacing hand planes. He decided to hang up his tools and set up a cart at Taipei's Minsheng West Road Shuanglian Market, selling braised pork rice, braised pig's trotters, savory congee, and _tong-zai mi-gao_ (potted rice cake) [^1] [^3]. The stall's schedule was nearly masochistic: the couple woke up at 12:30 PM to prepare ingredients, operated from 5:00 PM until 2:00 AM, and went to the market to shop at 4:30 AM before finally sleeping—averaging only three to four hours of sleep per day. In a seminar record from the Kinmen County SME Service Center, General Manager Chang Shih-chieh stated bluntly: "Because they averaged only three to four hours of sleep, they even had no time to shave, so customers gave them the nickname."[^7] This origin story, verified by the person involved, is more persuasive than any marketing textbook.

Business gradually improved. In 1971, due to the widening of Minsheng West Road, the stall moved to Ningxia Road near the Jiancheng Circle. In 197le, a major house fire destroyed almost all their property, leaving only the clothes on their backs, a few utensils, and that single cart [^3]. The Chang couple did not retreat; they continued operating, using a pair of spatulas to "re-fry" their livelihood back into existence.

### A Quality Revolution Born from Scolding

What changed the fate of Formosa Chang was a "picky" customer. At the time, the head chef of the Daodaocheng Black Beauty Grand Restaurant frequently visited. One day, after eating, his expression was grim, and before leaving, he dropped a remark: "What kind of braised pork rice is this? How dare you sell such a thing?" He then provided three suggestions: **rice must be newly harvested, meat must be sliced, and soy sauce must be pure-brewed** [^4]. These three sentences became the ironclad quality rules for Chang Yen-chuan for the next fifty years—meat was no longer processed through a grinder but instead selected from the best cuts, with blood vessels and lymph nodes removed and cut into strips; shallots were peeled by hand; and soy sauce was switched to pure-brewed. For three years, the chef continued to eat and continue to correct; Chang Yen-chuan accepted it all. It was only years later, while attending his wedding, that Chang Yen-chuan discovered that this regular customer who ate there every day was actually the head chef of the Black Beauty Grand Restaurant [^4].

> 📝 Curator's Note:
> The value of this story lies in its rarity—there is almost no other brand in the history of Taiwanese street food that completed a quality upgrade under the pressure of "being scolded by customers." Formosa Chang wrote "accepting criticism and listening to the voice of the customer" into one of its six core management obsessions [^8], essentially institutionalizing that original scolding into corporate culture.

## From Stall to Storefront: "A Beggar with Ambitions" (1979–1993)

Chang Yung-chang had been helping his father at the stall since age six—pushing the cart, greeting customers, washing dishes, and even carrying his newborn fifth brother, Chang Shih-chieh, on his back. After completing military service, he found he could not live without the stall—within a year of discharge, his weight dropped from 60kg to 48kg [^2]. He proposed to his father that they rent a storefront; Chang Yen-chuan was initially unwilling, even calling his son "a beggar with ambitions" [^3]. The father and son argued for two years, until in 1979, when the hardware store behind the stall closed, Chang Yen-chuan finally reluctantly agreed to rent a storefront at 40,000 TWD per month (split half-and-half with another vendor), which is today's Ningxia store [^3] [^9].

The returns on this transformation were almost immediate. Within three months of moving into the storefront, daily revenue jumped from 7,000–8,000 TWD to 12,000 TWD, and after six months, it reached 25,000 TWD. Chang Yung-chang then invested hundreds of thousands more to install unprecedented equipment for a braised pork rice stall—"air conditioning for a street stall"—and within a year, daily revenue reached 100,000 TWD [^3] [^2]. He subsequently introduced cash registers, disposable utensils, illuminated menus, and central kitchen pre-preparation. In 1983, he applied for ISO quality certification—all of which followed the 1984 opening of McDonald's first store in Taiwan. The Chang father and son visited McDonald's thirty to forty times; what shocked him most was not the burgers, but the "lack of an oily atmosphere" and the standardized rhythm of ordering, serving, and cashiering [^3]. In 1986, Formosa Chang repositioned itself as "Chinese-style fast food," registered his father's nickname as a trademark, and completed the transformation of Taiwan's first modernized braised pork rice brand. In 1993, it reorganized into a joint-stock company [^9].

> 📝 Curator's Note:
> Copying McDonald's system to sell braised pork rice may seem wild, but Formosa Chang copied the "rhythm" rather than the "content": the "four fasts" of service—fast greeting, fast ordering, fast serving, and fast clearing—are essentially the operational efficiency of the fast-food industry. Applying this to Taiwanese street food was a rare instance of cross-industry learning in Taiwan's dining scene during the 1980s [^2].

## Foot-and-Mouth Disease and Franchising Lessons: Shrinking from Sixty Stores back to Full Direct Operation (1993–2000)

After reorganizing as a joint-stock company, Formosa Chang expanded rapidly through franchising in the mid-1990s, with franchised stores once exceeding fifty [^2]. On March 20, 1997, Foot-and-Mouth Disease (FMD) broke out in Taiwan; everyone turned pale at the mention of "pork," and demand for braised pork rice plummeted. Chang Yung-chang frantically introduced chicken rice and various non-pork items to mitigate the crisis, but the supply system fell into chaos, and the number of stores declined steadily. A 2012 record from the Kinmen County SME Service Center provides exact figures: "The FMD outbreak on March 20, 1997, impacted up to sixty franchised stores; by 1999, there were no more franchised stores" [^7]. All sixty franchised stores were either reabsorbed or closed—from then on, Formosa Chang became a fully direct-operated brand, sacrificing scale for consistency in quality [^3] [^7]. This crisis also accompanied a family split; Chang Yung-chang parted ways with some of his brothers, suffering heavy losses, and the "Formosa Chang" brand was ultimately taken over by the eldest brother's lineage [^2].

> 📝 Curator's Note:
> The lesson of FMD was validated twenty years later. The wave of food safety collapses in Taiwan's bubble tea and braised pork rice franchises during the 2010s confirmed Formosa Chang's decision: for a street food brand where quality is the lifeline, if the cost of expansion is paid via food safety, the math simply does not work. Full direct operation is an expensive choice, but it is the only capital that allows Formosa Chang to still dare to raise prices.

## Selling to the Whole World (2000–Present)

After stabilizing its core business through full direct operation, Form форма Chang began expanding outward. In 2000, it partnered with Japan's MIC company to expand in Shibuya, Tokyo; around 2003, it entered mainland China; in 2010, it signed a contract with China Airlines, bringing pork rib braised pork rice to the US West Coast in-flight meals [^9] [^10] [^11]. The work in the Japanese market was particularly arduous: Japanese customers initially did not dare to order "braised pork rice," thinking it was made with fish; Formosa Chang broke the ice by adding a note to the menu stating it was "braised minced pork served over white rice" and adopted a localization strategy of air-freighting core ingredients while adjusting sauces locally [^2].

Today's Formosa Chang has a central kitchen that has passed triple international food safety certifications: FSSC 22000, ISO 22000, and HACCP. In 2023, it opened its new corporate headquarters in New Taipei City [^9], and in 2019, it completed a digital transformation including a membership app, KIOSK self-ordering, and digital payments [^9].

### The Two-Yuan Price Hike Controversy and the 39 TWD Freeze

The pricing history of Formosa Chang is a microcosm of Taiwan's inflation. In the early days, a bowl of braised pork rice was 3.5 TWD; prices gradually rose after moving to storefronts. On September 16, 2012, the small bowl price moved from 33 to 35 TWD, and the large bowl from 64 to 68 TWD—this increase of only two or three yuan exploded into a public outcry, with media criticizing that "folk food is not folk at all," causing sales to drop by over ten percent within two weeks, even prompting the Taipei City Industry Development Bureau to suspend its food promotion partnership with Formosa Chang. At a press conference, Chang Yung-chang apologized publicly alongside senior executives; his original words still carry a sense of grievance and self-reflection:

> "We must respect our customers, and this is an act of sincerity toward them... the price adjustment of Formosa Chang braised pork rice has caused discomfort, dissatisfaction, and misunderstanding among the general public." — Formosa Chang Chairman Chang Yung-chang, Press Conference on October 3, 2012 [^5]

The brand eventually reverted the price to its original level, and this controversy became a case study in Taiwan for brand crisis communication [^5] [^12].

Interestingly, the same issue appeared with an entirely different narrative over a decade later. In January 2026, Formosa Chang announced price adjustments for certain items; the media misread "Small bowl fragrant braised pork rice increased from 66 to 71 TWD" as "39 TWD small bowl braised pork rice price hike." Formosa Chang issued clarifications for several days: since the 39 TWD pricing was set in October 2017, despite fluctuations in raw materials and labor costs, the price has not been raised for eight years [^6]. During an interview in 2020, Chang Yung-chang had already discussed this contradiction—labor wages must rise according to the law, and "taking care of employees is more important than taking care of my son"; furthermore, his brand's greatest competitor is not any braised pork rice shop, but "7-Eleven and FamilyMart" [^8]. The price of a single lunch box at a convenience store serves as the entire reference system for Formosa Chang's pricing ceiling; this statement clarifies the competitive landscape of a street food brand more clearly than any market research report.

> 📝 Curator's Note:
> The two price waves form a perfect contrast: in 2012, a 2-yuan increase led to being targeted by public opinion, but in 2026, when the 71 TWD bowl was misunderstood as a braised pork rice hike, netizens stepped up to defend it, saying "that 71 TWD bowl is actually a lunch box." The "39 TWD freeze" credit accumulated by the brand over fourteen years became its strongest bulletproof vest.

## The Deeper Meaning of the Sign: Turning Father's Face into a Brand

The Formosa Chang logo is not the work of a visual design firm, but a sketched portrait: a smile, wrinkles on both sides, and a thick beard—the face of Chang Yen-chuan. When Chang Yung-chang registered it as a trademark in 1986, he essentially fixed his father's lifelong labor into a visual brand asset: the son turned his father's nickname into a brand, and then turned himself into the heir of this face [^1] [^3]. And the corporate vision set in 2006—"to sell braised pork rice so that the whole world knows"—has traveled from stores in Shibuya and China Airlines in-flight meals to today's Taiwan stores and overseas branches [^3, 9].

> 📝 Curator's Note:
> Braised pork rice has frequently topped CNN's list of must-eat foods in Taiwan [^2], but in the English names for Taiwanese braised pork rice, the brand most often pronounced directly by foreign tourists is "Huzuzhang," with its nasal and guttural Taiwanese pronunciation. A brand that does not even have a Chinese proper name—"Bearded Chang" was never anyone's real name—has instead become the easiest name for this dish to be pronounced correctly internationally.

## Conclusion

In 1960, a carpenter sold rice from a cart opposite the Shuanglian Police Station, earning a name because he was too busy to shave. In 2026, his son's company uses a central kitchen with triple international food safety certification to deliver that same bowl of braised pork rice to Taipei, Kinmen, and overseas. Between those two points lie a major fire, a chef's harsh scolding, the FMD crisis of sixty franchised stores, and a series of price hikes that were retracted due to backlash. The bearded face on the Formosa Chang sign has never changed—what has changed, again and again, is the entire system behind this bowl of rice. Next time you are queuing at Ningxia Night Market, you might wonder: is this bowl of rice, frozen at 39 TWD for eight years, truly worth it?
