# Hakka Food Culture

> **30-Second Overview:** Hakka cuisine is characterized by being "savory, rich, and aromatic," carrying the history of the Hakka migration and their life wisdom. From the aroma of _leicha_ in Beipu Old Street to the wild ginger flower zongzi in Neiwang, from the richness of preserved meat with mustard greens to the simplicity of Hakka stir-fry, every dish tells a profound story of the Hakka people's connection to the land.

## Why It Is Important

Hakka cuisine holds a unique place within Taiwan's diverse culinary landscape. During their long migration, the Hakka developed distinct food preservation techniques and cooking methods, much of which continues to influence Taiwanese cuisine today. The "savory richness" of Hakka dishes reflects the nutritional needs of working people and embodies the Hakka philosophy of thriftiness and making full use of every resource.

## Overview

Walking into Beipu Old Street in Hsinchu, the air is always filled with the fragrance of _leicha_. This mellow aroma comes from repeatedly grinding sesame seeds, peanuts, and tea leaves in a ceramic mortar. The origin of Taiwanese _leicha_ is said to be the Hakka people who migrated from the Hepo area in Guangdong after the war, bringing their mortars and pestles along on their escape; at that time, it was merely a domestic dietary habit[^1]. The _leicha_ shops in Beipu Old Street are much younger: the first dedicated _leicha_ shop in Taiwan only opened in the temple courtyard of Beipu Old Street in March 1999[^2].

The formation of Hakka food culture is inseparable from the migration history of the Hakka people. During their long journey, the Hakka had to learn how to survive in different environments and how to maximize limited ingredients. This survival wisdom—"making a living off the mountain one lives on, and water one lives by"—shaped the fundamental characteristics of Hakka cuisine: "using local ingredients" and "making full use of everything."

## Classic Dishes

Hakka cuisine excels with several representative dishes, each embodying specific historical contexts and local wisdom.

### Leicha: A Thousand-Year Legacy of Three Senses Soup

_Leicha_, also known as "Three Senses Soup," is traditionally said to have originated during the Three Kingdoms period when Zhang Fei attacked Wuling; soldiers fell ill from an epidemic. An herbal physician presented a secret recipe made by grinding raw tea, raw ginger, and raw rice into a paste and boiling it for medicinal use, giving the soup its name[^3]. In the daily life of the Hakka people, _leicha_ is served as a snack to guests and is also considered a health drink[^4].

Traditional _leicha_ requires specialized mortars and pestles. The mortar is usually made of pottery with fine carvings on the inner wall; the pestle is often made from durian or tea tree trunks, which are hard and possess a natural fragrance. During preparation, tea leaves, sesame seeds, peanuts, and other ingredients are placed in the mortar and ground clockwise using the pestle until all materials are reduced to a fine powder.

The _leicha_ culture in the Hsinchu area is particularly developed; each township, from Hukou and Zhudong to Beipu, has its own specialty. The _leicha_ of Beipu Old Street is a must-visit experience for tourists, as many shops not only offer ready-made _leicha_ but also allow visitors to experience the grinding process themselves[^5]. The _leicha_ in Beipu was developed commercially in conjunction with promoting cultural industries; after the first shop struggled financially, over sixty shops appeared on the old street[^4].

With the changing times, _leicha_ has also had an iced version. A field record from a study of Hakka cuisine in 2013 shows that vendors in Neiwang Old Street, Hsinchu, were already selling _leicha_ slushies[^5], turning what was traditionally drunk hot into a summer treat.

**Nutritional Value and Consumption Taboos**
_Leicha_ is rich in protein, Vitamin E, and unsaturated fatty acids, offering good nutritional value. However, because its main ingredients are nuts, it is relatively high in calories; patients with diabetes and those looking to lose weight should consume it in moderation. The _leicha_ originally brought by Hepo was savory, but when promoted in Beipu, the salty flavor was not well-received by tourists, leading to the modification into the sweet drink commonly seen today[^1].

### Wild Ginger Flower Zongzi: A Fragrant Memory from the Mountains

In Neiwang Old Street, Hengshan Township, Hsinchu County, there is a memorable zongzi—the wild ginger flower zongzi. This mountain flavor embodies the Hakka wisdom of coexisting with the natural environment.

Wild ginger flowers (originally from India and widely naturalized in Taiwan) bloom from July to October[^7]. It is said that in earlier times, transportation to Neiwang was difficult, so residents used wild ginger flowers abundant near the Yuluo Creek as a seasoning. Later, they dried and ground the rhizomes into powder, mixing it with mountain pepper, wild mushrooms, black pork, pickled Hakka radishes, and glutinous rice to steam into oily rice (_youfan_). This was then wrapped in wild ginger leaves and steamed to become the wild ginger flower zongzi[^6]. Peng Rui-yun, who promotes this dish in Neiwang, uses dried wild ginger rhizome powder instead of MSG: the rhizomes must first be salted for five or six weeks to remove spiciness before being dried and ground[^7].

The production process of wild ginger flower zongzi reflects the ecological concept of the Hakka people. The leaves of wild ginger are naturally non-toxic, making them eco-friendly wrapping material while also adding a natural fragrance to the zongzi. During steaming, the unique fragrance of the wild ginger permeates the glutinous rice, creating a distinct layer of flavor.

Wild ginger only blooms in summer and autumn, yet the wild ginger flower zongzi is not limited by the flowering season. In a 2009 TVBS interview, there were already over ten wild ginger flower zongzi shops on Neiwang Old Street; just one small stall in front of Peng Rui-yun's house could sell over a thousand pieces per day. The wild ginger, which was only seen during the flowering season, became highly sought after year-round to supply the leaves for wrapping[^7].

### Hakka Stir-fry: The Ultimate Expression of Commoner Wisdom

Hakka stir-fry is one of the four "Four Stews and Four Stir-fries" in Hakka cuisine. The "Four Stews and Four Stir-fries" were originally associated with seasonal rituals, and older generations of Hakka people did not eat them every day; they were more common during festivals[^5].

Its origin is largely linked to sacrificial offerings. The three sacrifices offered by the Hakka gods often included roasted chicken, pork, and dried squid. After the offering, women would shred the pork and soak and shred the squid, then quickly stir-fry them with locally grown scallions and soy sauce to make a savory dish suitable for rice[^8]. Therefore, the main characters are pork belly and dried squid; tofu, celery, garlic sprouts, and chili peppers are supporting roles. Hakka people in the north call it "stir-fried meat" (_chao rou_), often adding celery. Those in the south call it "stir-fried squid" (_chao yiu_), using more garlic sprouts[^8].

### Pickling Culture: The Alchemist of Time

The pickling techniques of the Hakka people are superb. Preserved products such as mustard greens, preserved cabbage, salted pork, and pickled radishes not only extend food preservation but also create unique flavors.

**Mustard Greens (Fucai)** is a product resulting from the fermentation of Chinese cabbage, possessing a unique sour aroma and being an indispensable ingredient in many Hakka dishes. The same head of Chinese cabbage can become pickles, _fucai_ (mustard greens), or preserved mustard greens (_meigan cai_) depending on the degree of drying and the amount of salt used[^5]. **Preserved Cabbage** is the result of drying and pickling Chinese cabbage, resulting in a savory and rich taste, making it a classic pairing with braised meat. **Salted Pork** is pork preserved by salting and air-drying; it can be stored for a longer period and is an important source of protein for the Hakka people.

The development of these pickling techniques is closely related to the living environment of the Hakka people. In an agricultural society, preserving food without refrigeration was a vital survival skill. The pickling techniques developed by the Hakka not only solved preservation problems but also created rich flavor variations.

## Food Philosophy

Hakka food philosophy did not arise in a vacuum; it is a direct product of migration life and laboring environments.

### Savory, Rich, Aromatic: The Culinary Code of Laborers

Hakka cuisine is famous for being "savory, rich, and aromatic." This flavor profile directly stems from the Hakka's agricultural working environment. Since the Hakka often engaged in manual farm labor requiring a large amount of physical energy, their diet tended to be heavy-tasting and high-calorie to replenish the salt and energy lost during work.

"Savory" is to replenish salts lost while working; "rich" is to provide high calories; and "aromatic" is to stimulate appetite through various spices and seasonings so that sufficient nutrients can be consumed. This dietary characteristic reflects the pragmatic attitude of the Hakka people and their accurate understanding of bodily needs.

### Using Local Ingredients: Living in Harmony with Nature

The migration history of the Hakka created their strong adaptability. Whether settling in mountainous areas, plains, or coastal regions, the Hakka fully utilized local ingredients to develop dishes with regional characteristics.

In mountainous areas, the Hakka gathered wild vegetables and mushrooms to make mountain vegetable dishes; on the plains, they made full use of crops to develop various rice-based cultures; and in coastal areas, the Hakka also incorporated seafood elements to create different flavors.

### Frugality: An Eating Ethic Without Waste

The thriftiness of the Hakka people has deeply influenced their food culture. Very little is wasted on the Hakka dining table. Leftovers are reseasoned into another dish; bones are boiled for soup; and vegetable leaves are made into pickles. This philosophy of "making full use of everything" is both a pragmatic choice under economic pressure and a respect for food and natural resources.

## Modern Inheritance and Innovation

Hakka food culture faces the dual challenges of generational discontinuity and market transformation in the 21st century.

### Dialogue Between Tradition and Modernity

In modern society, Hakka food culture faces the challenge of inheritance and innovation. On one hand, younger generations are gradually losing knowledge of traditional Hakka cuisine; on the other hand, Hakka cuisine is seeking a new positioning in the modern dining market.

Many Hakka restaurants have begun attempting to modernize traditional Hakka dishes by reducing oil and salt content, improving presentation, or even incorporating elements from other cuisines. Although these changes have caused some controversy, they have also opened up new paths for the dissemination of Hakka food culture.

### The Double-Edged Sword of Tourism Culture

The commercialization of Hakka food culture brings both opportunities and challenges. _Leicha_ in Beipu and wild ginger flower zongzi in Neiwang are famous tourist experiences. This development has, on one hand, increased the visibility of Hakka culture, but on the other, it may lead to excessive commercialization, losing its original cultural essence. Field researchers in Neiwang in 2013 observed that most local businesses were leaning towards economic gain, and restaurant inheritance was not always rooted in Hakka culture[^5].

Finding a balance between commercial development and cultural preservation is an important task that modern Hakka food culture must face.

## Cultural Significance and Value

The value of Hakka food culture far exceeds the cuisine itself. It is a carrier of the historical memory of the Hakka people, a symbol of cultural identity, and a crystallization of traditional wisdom. In an era of globalization, these unique culinary cultures appear even more precious.

The "savory richness" of Hakka dishes reflects the resilient character of the Hakka; the pickling culture demonstrates their life wisdom; and the _leicha_ culture showcases their social traditions. These food cultures not only enrich Taiwan's gastronomic map but also add significant color to Taiwan's multiculturalism.

By tasting Hakka cuisine, we can not only enjoy sensory satisfaction but also feel the cultural background and life wisdom of an ethnic group. This is the most precious value of Hakka food culture.
