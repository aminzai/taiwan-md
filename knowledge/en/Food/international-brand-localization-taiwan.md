> **30-Second Overview:** When international brands enter the Taiwan market, they bring not only globalized products but also integrate Taiwan’s unique food culture and consumer habits into their offerings through deep localization strategies. From McDonald’s corn soup and KFC’s Portuguese egg tarts to Pizza Hut’s boundary-pushing specialty pizzas, 7-ELEVEN’s braised delicacies and OPEN Little General, and even the preference for salmon in conveyor-belt sushi, these cases demonstrate brands’ respect and flexibility toward the Taiwan market. Furthermore, Starbucks’s specialty stores, Costco’s status as the world’s top-performing store, MOS Burger’s rice-based food culture, IKEA’s culinary miracle, and Carrefour’s transformation after its acquisition and name change collectively illustrate how international brands create unique success formulas through cultural integration.

In the process of global expansion, international brands often face the challenge of balancing standardization with localization. In Taiwan, this challenge has spawned many unique products and services, bringing these brands closer to the lives of Taiwanese consumers and unexpectedly creating many “Taiwan-exclusive” experiences that surprise foreigners. These localization strategies are not only commercial successes but also reflect Taiwan’s rich and diverse food culture and consumer habits.

## McDonald's: The Appeal of Warm Corn Soup and Local Sweet Potatoes

As the global leader in fast food, McDonald's has demonstrated significant flexibility in its localization within the Taiwan market. Most notably, its menu features **Corn Soup** and **Golden Sweet Potato Fries**. Corn Soup is unique among McDonald's offerings worldwide; its origins can be traced back to 1978, when a local fast-food restaurant named "McDonald's" (麥當樂) in Taiwan already served "Chicken Velvet Corn Soup" [^1][^6], before McDonald's officially entered Taiwan in 1984. After entering the Taiwan market, McDonald's retained and optimized this product to cater to the local habit of "eating rice with soup," making it a classic side dish option for Taiwan's McDonald's [^6].

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The localization of corn soup is not just an adjustment in taste but also a reflection of a deep understanding of Taiwanese dietary habits. A bowl of hot soup carries the expectations and comfort that Taiwanese people seek in a main meal.

Additionally, McDonald's Taiwan has launched **Golden Sweet Potato Fries** made from locally sourced red-fleshed sweet potatoes from the Tainung No. 64 variety [^1]. This product not only satisfies Taiwanese consumers' love for sweet potatoes but also demonstrates the brand's connection to local agriculture in its ingredient sourcing. These localized products have successfully transformed McDonald's from a purely "American fast food" chain into a dining option with a more distinct "Taiwanese flavor."

## KFC: Dominance of Egg Tarts and Innovation with Paper-Wrapped Chicken

KFC’s localization strategy in Taiwan is epitomized by the massive success of its **Portuguese egg tarts**[^7]. This dessert is even jokingly referred to as "an egg tart shop delayed by fried chicken" [^7]. The introduction of Portuguese egg tarts stemmed from the egg tart craze that swept Taiwan in the 1990s; KFC introduced the recipe from Macau’s Margaret (Ma Chia-li) in 1998, quickly making it a signature item[^7]. This strategy successfully expanded KFC’s brand image in Taiwan from a mere fried chicken specialist to a dining option that places equal emphasis on "desserts and main courses," with egg tarts becoming one of the primary reasons many consumers visit KFC.

Beyond egg tarts, KFC in Taiwan also launched the **Paper-Wrapped Chicken series**. This cooking method, which uses paper wrapping to bake and lock in juices, is relatively rare in KFC outlets globally but aligns with the Taiwanese preference for tender and juicy textures[^2]. Additionally, products like **Sweet Potato QQ Balls** and **Sweet Potato Fries** utilize locally sourced sweet potatoes and incorporate elements of Taiwanese night market snacks, further deepening KFC’s local connection in the Taiwanese market[^2].

## Pizza Hut: Challenging the Limits of the Palate with Creative Pizzas

Pizza Hut's localization strategy in Taiwan stands as the most experimental and topical among international brands. From the "Cilantro Century Egg & Pork Blood Cake Pizza" to the "Stinky Tofu Pizza" and "Bubble Tea Pizza," these creative products that challenge consumers' taste buds have repeatedly sparked heated discussions on social media[^3][^8]. For instance, the Cilantro Century Egg & Pork Blood Cake Pizza, launched in 2021, set a record on its launch day with Google keyword searches exceeding 100,000 in a single day[^3].

Behind these seemingly "peculiar" flavors lies the "e-commerce mindset" and "WOW Pizza strategy" driven by Pizza Hut General Manager Liu Hong-zheng (from Hong Kong)[^3]. Liu Hong-zheng believes that in a market where food delivery platforms are prevalent, such as in Taiwan, Pizza Hut is essentially an e-commerce entity. Therefore, "Talkability," "Traffic," and "Transaction" have become the key 3T strategy[^3]. By continuously launching localized products with high topicality, even if consumers do not necessarily purchase these special flavors, the brand can effectively enhance its online volume and attention, thereby driving overall sales[^3]. This strategy not only breaks the established impression among Taiwanese people that "pizza is only eaten during festivals" but also successfully shapes Pizza Hut into a brand full of innovation and vitality[^3].

## 7-ELEVEN: Practitioner of Lifestyle Proposals and the Charm of OPEN Jiang

As a representative of Taiwan's convenience store culture, 7-ELEVEN's localization has long transcended the mere realm of products, penetrating deeply into the daily lives of Taiwanese people. Beyond the widely known **tea eggs** (with annual sales exceeding 100 million units), 7-ELEVEN has recently launched "Star-Grade Braised Delicacies," standardizing Taiwan's traditional night market braised food culture to provide consumers with 24-hour convenience[^4][^9].

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** 7-ELEVEN's success lies not only in the diversity of its products but also in how it transforms convenience stores into a platform offering "lifestyle proposals," meeting the dual needs of Taiwanese people for convenience and local cuisine.

Another successful case of localization is its exclusive mascot, **OPEN Jiang**. Developed in 2005 by 7-ELEVEN's parent company, Uni-President, and the Japanese advertising agency Dentsu, OPEN Jiang features a complete character setting and backstory. Through merchandise, themed events, and other means, it has successfully integrated into Taiwan's pop culture, even generating NT$1 billion in annual business value for 7-ELEVEN[^4][^10][^11]. OPEN Jiang's success demonstrates the immense potential of brand mascots in the Taiwanese market and their importance in establishing emotional brand connections.

## Conveyor-belt Sushi: Taiwan's Salmon Frenzy

In recent years, rapidly rising conveyor-belt sushi brands in the Taiwan market, such as Sushi郎 (Sushilan) and Zang Sushi, have demonstrated interesting localization phenomena, specifically the Taiwanese consumers' abnormal obsession with **salmon**. Compared to Japan's domestic market, the variety and sales volume of salmon items in Taiwan's conveyor-belt sushi restaurants are typically far higher than those of other fish species[^5]. This phenomenon even sparked the "Salmon Incident" in 2021, when Sushi郎 held a name-change discount event, attracting over 300 people across Taiwan to change their ID names to "Salmon" in order to enjoy free sushi[^5][^12].

Taiwanese people's preference for salmon can be attributed to the common perception that it offers "high cost-performance and a rich, fatty texture," as well as the fact that salmon was the most widely available raw fish option when Japanese cuisine was first promoted in Taiwan[^5]. Data shows that Taiwan's largest salmon importer, Sushizan, imports approximately 2,500 metric tons of salmon annually, accounting for about 20% of the total imported volume in Taiwan[^5][^13]. This love for salmon has prompted conveyor-belt sushi brands in the Taiwan market to launch more diverse salmon products to meet consumer demand.

## Starbucks: Local Aesthetics of the "Third Place"

Starbucks' localization strategy in Taiwan is reflected not only in its beverages but also deeply in its "third place" concept. Taiwan Starbucks has created distinctive stores by renovating historical sites or integrating local landscapes, such as the Baoan store located in the Ye Jintu Historic House in Dadaocheng, the Jiu Hetong Ting She store in Hukou, Yunlin, and the Lizida Store in Hualien[^14][^15]. These unique stores serve not only as coffee venues but also as carriers of local culture and history, reducing the gap between international brands and the local environment while enhancing consumers' spatial experience[^15].

Additionally, Starbucks actively promotes holiday gift boxes with Taiwanese characteristics, such as mooncakes for the Mid-Autumn Festival and zongzi for the Dragon Boat Festival, as well as products made with local ingredients, such as local tea series and taro Mont Blanc[^14]. The introduction of these products aims to cater to Taiwanese consumers' emphasis on holiday customs and their preference for local flavors, further deepening the brand's connection to Taiwanese culture.

## Costco: The Perfect Blend of Global Store King and Localized Service

Costco, the American wholesale club, has achieved great success in Taiwan, serving as a model for the localization of international brands. Taiwan's Costco stores, particularly the South Taichung branch, once held the title of "No. 1 in Global Profit," while the Neihu and Zhonghe branches have consistently ranked in the global top ten[^16][^17]. With over 4 million members in Taiwan, estimated annual membership fee revenue reaches NT$5.4 billion based on the NT$1,350 annual fee, confirming its business model where 80% of profits come from membership fees rather than product gross margins[^17].

Costco's localized services in Taiwan are also quite prominent. For example, it features gas stations that are rare globally, with the North Taichung branch having the highest fuel sales volume in the entire country[^18]. Additionally, although Costco primarily focuses on large-portion products, Taiwan branches have added more cooked food and snack options favored by locals, adapting to the local family structure[^17]. This strategy precisely targets the Taiwanese consumer psychology of "seeking high cost-performance" and "family shopping," leading to significant success in the Taiwan market.

## MOS Burger: The Eastern Fast-Food Representative of Rice Culture

Originating from Japan, MOS Burger has successfully highlighted its characteristics of "Eastern flavors" and "tech-driven service" in the Taiwan market. The most representative product is the **Rice Burger**. Taiwan's MOS Burger launched the "MOS Yakiniku Pearl Burger" in 1991, using local Taiwanese rice as the main ingredient, successfully integrating rice culture into fast-food products and making it a signature brand item[^19][^20].

MOS Burger also actively promotes the use of local ingredients. For instance, it was the first in Taiwan to receive the "Taiwanese Pork Certification Mark" in 2020, emphasizing the use of products with the Taiwanese Rice Mark[^19]. Compared to other American fast-food brands, MOS Burger is closer to Taiwanese people's reliance on refined fast food and rice culture, successfully establishing a unique position in the market.

## IKEA: The Restaurant Delayed by Furniture and Creative Marketing

In Taiwan, the Swedish furniture brand IKEA has firmly established its image as a "restaurant delayed by furniture." Food and beverage revenue accounts for a staggering 12% of IKEA Taiwan's total sales, nearly three times the global average of 4–5%, ranking it first worldwide[^21][^22]. Notably, the annual sales volume of its **ice cream** priced at just NT$10 has surpassed one million units, accounting for 30% of its food revenue[^23]. This "lead chicken strategy" attracts foot traffic through affordable food, not only enhancing the customer shopping experience but also creating a psychological impression among consumers that "IKEA products are all cheap"[^24].

IKEA's localized marketing in Taiwan is also full of creativity. For instance, its **shark (BLÅHAJ)** plush toy sparked a craze in Taiwan, and the brand introduced unique seasonal flavors tied to local festivals, such as wasabi ice cream and soy sauce paste ice cream[^25]. These strategies successfully transformed "furniture shopping" into a "family dining outing," catering to Taiwanese people's high interest in dining out.

## Carrefour: From International Brand to Deep Local Roots as "Wan Jia Fu"

The French hypermarket brand Carrefour's localization strategy in Taiwan has undergone significant transformation, shifting from aggressive acquisitions to brand rebranding. In 2020, Carrefour acquired Wellcome and Jasons supermarkets for NT$3.2 billion, a move aimed at strengthening its "community supermarket" footprint to compete fiercely in Taiwan's retail market[^26][^27]. Through these acquisitions, Carrefour was able to rapidly expand its store network, penetrate deeper into Taiwanese communities, and provide services more closely aligned with residents' needs.

Carrefour also actively promoted its "Impact Center" initiative, collaborating closely with local small-scale farmers to promote local ingredients and free-range eggs, demonstrating its support for local agriculture[^28]. However, following the completion of the transaction by the Uni-President Group on June 30, 2023, which officially secured 100% equity in Taiwan Carrefour, the company became purely Taiwan-based[^29][^30]. This change in ownership led to a shift in the brand name. Due to the expiration of the brand licensing agreement with French Carrefour, Taiwan Carrefour resolved to terminate the license on December 19, 2025, and officially rebranded starting **July 1, 2026**[^31][^32][^33].

The hypermarkets will be renamed **Wan Jia Fu (Prosperity Plaza)**, while the supermarkets will be renamed **Le Jia Kang (Uni-Prosperity)**[^34][^35]. This rebranding is more than just a sign change; it symbolizes Carrefour's formal integration into the Uni-President retail system, creating deeper synergies with brands like 7-ELEVEN and Watsons. It also marks a shift in brand image from "French brand" to "local brand," emphasizing the auspicious meanings of "Wan Jia Fu" and "Le Jia Kang," which better align with the cultural preferences of Taiwanese consumers[^36][^37].

## Conclusion

The localization strategies of international brands in Taiwan represent a culinary revolution that blends global vision with local insight. From McDonald's corn soup and KFC's Portuguese egg tarts to Pizza Hut's creative pizzas, 7-ELEVEN's braised delicacies and OPEN Little General, as well as the salmon frenzy surrounding conveyor-belt sushi, these cases are not only commercial successes but also vivid reflections of Taiwan's unique food culture and consumer habits. Furthermore, Starbucks' themed stores, Costco's status as the world's top-performing store, MOS Burger's rice-based culinary culture, IKEA's dining奇迹 (miracle), and Carrefour's local acquisitions and brand transformation collectively illustrate how international brands on this island have created a unique formula for success through cultural integration and business innovation. Through continuous innovation and adaptation, these brands have proven that only by deeply understanding and embracing local culture can one stand out in a fiercely competitive market and forge deep emotional connections with consumers.
