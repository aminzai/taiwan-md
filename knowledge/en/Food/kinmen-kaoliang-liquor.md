Drought-resistant sorghum was integrated into the arrangements for frontline supply and local production; later, the distillery grew up amidst martial governance, the monopoly system, and local governance. Today, when people pick up a bottle of Kinmen Kaoliang, they are drinking not just a 38% or 58% proof spirit, but the process by which a place rewrote military institutions into memories of daily life. [^1] [^2] [^3]

## Starting with a Distillery

In 1952, Kinmen was still a frontline island following the Chinese Civil War. The official history of the Kinmen Distillery records its founder as Kinmen Defense Commander Hu Lien. On February 1, the following year, the Jiujiang Distillery officially began operations, with Zhou Xinchun as its first director. [^1]

This starting point is far from romantic. On a frontline island, supply chains are not just a matter of cost; they are inextricably linked to military risks, local production, and market circulation. Research on the sales of Kinmen Kaoliang to Taiwan looks back at this supply and market chain, starting from its 1953 market launch and the 1977 policy turning point. [^3]

Kinmen’s liquor did not grow naturally from an "ancient tradition." It is more akin to a policy solution devised by a martial government facing material shortages, binding land, the military, alcohol, and local markets together. This is the aspect of Kinmen Kaoliang most easily overlooked: its first identity was not as a souvenir, but as a supply commodity.

> 📝 **Curator’s Note:** A bottle of liquor may appear to be a consumer choice, but looking back, it reveals the traces of how a military regime arranged land and taste. The "local flavor" of Kinmen Kaoliang was not initially determined solely by the local community.

## Ye Huacheng and the Appropriated Technology

In this history, Ye Huacheng is a name that cannot be omitted. Local accounts of Kinmen state that Kinmen Kaoliang Liquor began with Ye brewing in an old house on Dalu Road, after which General Hu Lien took it under management and expanded it into the Jiujiang Distillery. This is the origin narrative in local memory, and it should not be directly expanded into a conclusion of "sole inventor." [^4]

Ye Huacheng’s story is not equivalent to "a genius invented Kinmen Kaoliang." Kinmen already had folk liquor production; technology did not start with just one person. A more accurate statement is that he transformed existing folk brewing activities into a technical node that the military could see, take over, and scale up. The National Taiwan Museum of History lists pre-1952 folk liquor production in Kinmen and post-war regulatory changes as research topics, reminding us that before the distillery was established, the island already had its own brewing context. [^5]

This memory carries obvious controversy: the same event can be described as "local technology being seen by the state," or as "private enterprise being requisitioned under an authoritarian environment." This article does not whitewash the latter into the former, nor does it push all details into a single conclusion. What is certain is that today’s Kinmen Distillery is not a purely military venture detached from civil society.

## From the Island to Taiwan

After the distillery began operations in 1953, Kinmen Kaoliang was still subject to market and institutional constraints. Official data from the Kinmen Distillery records that in 1977, the distillery was incorporated into the state monopoly sales system, allowing Kinmen liquor to officially enter the Taiwan market more easily. This change pushed products that primarily served stationed troops and the island’s interior toward a larger consumer network. [^1]

The 1977 turning point was not just about "increased sales volume." It allowed Kinmen Kaoliang to leave the local scale of the frontline and become a recognizable bottle in banquets, gift-giving, and family gatherings on the Taiwan main island. The bottle began to carry information beyond the liquid itself: from Kinmen, from a martial governance zone, from a place packaged by the state monopoly system. [^3]

The National Taiwan Museum of History lists pre-1952 folk brewing and post-war liquor regulations as research topics, reminding us not to write the history of Kinmen Kaoliang solely as the corporate history of a single distillery. Bottles and liquor systems actually connect local society with state management. [^5]

## The Martial Governance Ended, the Distillery Remained

In 1992, martial governance in Kinmen ended, and the Kinmen Distillery was transferred to the Kinmen County Government. In 1998, it was reorganized into Kinmen Liquor Industry Co., Ltd. [^1] These two years shifted the history of Kinmen Liquor from military administration to local governance: the distillery was no longer just a production unit in the frontline system, but became a public asset that the county government had to manage and local residents continued to expect.

This transformation made liquor more like Kinmen’s "common property," but also brought new problems. When a company belongs to the local government and is placed within local memory and tourism narratives, the success of liquor is no longer just about brand success. Local lectures place Ye Huacheng’s former residence, traditional settlements, and Kaoliang liquor on the same walking tour route, showing that the distillery has been integrated into Kinmen’s local memory and tourism narratives. [^4]

This also creates a different relationship between Kinmen Liquor and other martial governance relics in [Kinmen County](/en/geography/kinmen-county/): some history is preserved as heritage sites, while others enter wine cellars, bottle labels, and banquets. Kinmen does not leave all martial governance history in museums; instead, it places part of it into daily consumption. This transformation is creative but carries risks: if the experience is reduced only to "drinking in tunnels," the history of war and military control may be replaced by consumer scenery.

## Who Drinks, Whose Taste Counts as Kinmen

Viewing Kinmen Kaoliang within local consumer culture yields another answer: it is not just liquor provided by the military, but also one re-used by residents in banquets, gift-giving, and daily gatherings. English reports observe that Kaoliang liquor has long been linked to drinking occasions for older men, masculinity, and political gift-giving culture. These clues indicate that while institutions provide the framework, local life determines what meaning ultimately remains. [^6]

This perspective is important because it means "Kinmen Kaoliang is a liquor invented by the military" is no longer the end of the story. Institutions can dictate how liquor is produced, who can sell it, and which flavors are promoted, but they cannot fully determine how residents ultimately use it. Some drink it at banquets, some use it for gifts, some view it as a memory left by elders, and some do not drink it at all, instead choosing to reject high-alcohol content and male-dominated drinking table culture.

The same English report describes Kinmen Kaoliang as a common high-proof spirit in Taiwan, noting that 38% and 58% are the two most recognizable strengths. These observations provide cultural clues but should not be misread as the shared experience of all Taiwanese or all Kinmen people. [^6]

> **Before Opening the Bottle, Look at the Label:** The taste of Kinmen Kaoliang is not just on the tongue. Alcohol content, porcelain bottle design, gift-giving occasions, and who sits at the table all tell you what kind of social relationship this bottle belongs to.

## Local Finance in a Bottle

Official data from the Kinmen Distillery refers to the period after 1992 as the distillery’s "prosperity period" and "transformation period." The official English page also records that in 1992, the distillery was transferred to the Kinmen County Government, and in 1998, it became a company. [^1] These institutional changes show that the locality of Kinmen Liquor is not just about natural conditions like "good Kinmen water and weather suitable for sorghum," but also about who owns the distillery, how profits return to the local community, and how the local government uses the brand as a governance tool.

Kinmen Kaoliang later became one of the most recognizable products in Kinmen, integrated into narratives of distillery tours, local souvenirs, and cultural walking tours. Local articles place the distillery, settlements, Ye Huacheng’s former residence, and sorghum culture within the same narrative, making "drinking" one way to understand the Kinmen landscape. [^4] But this path cannot only speak of success: the stronger the public nature of the distillery, the more the local community needs open discussions about its operations, profit distribution, labor conditions, and agricultural supply, rather than ending with just the term "golden hen" (cash cow).

From an industrial perspective, there is another part of Kaoliang liquor that is not easily seen: brewing relies not just on branding, but on a complete set of technologies including koji making, solid-state fermentation, distillation, aging, and water sources. Commercial articles summarize the koji making, brewing, blending, and aging processes of Kinmen Kaoliang, but their product recommendations reflect the perspective of the business. More reliable details about workers come from reports by the Overseas Community Development Council on Chen Qiliang, a koji-making technician. [^7] [^8] If we only remember Hu Lien and Ye Huacheng, we write the history of liquor as a story of generals and entrepreneurs. Putting the koji-making worker back in allows us to see how a bottle is made day by day.

Chen Qiliang entered Kinmen Liquor Company at age 19, learned koji making from master craftsmen, served for 41 years and 6 months, and then returned to the company as a consultant. This length of time reminds us that so-called "traditional craftsmanship" is not an abstract ancient method, but work learned, repeated, and passed on to the next person within the factory. [^8]

The locality of Kaoliang liquor is therefore not just in the raw materials. It requires drought-resistant crops in the fields, microorganisms and tactile sense in the koji, and workers who can recognize changes in the factory. Only then does it leave Kinmen as a bottle visible to everyone. [^7] This path connects agriculture, industry, and family dining tables.

Thus, the taste of the distillery has never been one person’s secret recipe, but the result of long-term collaboration: raw materials, koji, time, equipment, and human judgment are all indispensable. [^7]
This is also the hardest part of a local brand to replicate. Every process leaves Kinmen’s land and human time in the liquor. [^7]
What the distillery leaves behind is not just a formula, but the workers’ judgment of time and quality.

## How the Bottle Remembers a System

The bottle of Kinmen Kaoliang is also worth looking at separately. The official timeline places 1977 in the "development period," and places the end of martial governance in 1992 and corporatization in 1998 into the "prosperity period" and "transformation period." These names are not neutral chronological labels; they are the narrative method the distillery chose for itself when shifting from military administration to a local enterprise. [^2]

Research collected by the National Taiwan Museum of History discusses the 1977 "bringing into the light" policy within the context of sales to Taiwan. This allows us to see that the market was not a naturally open door, but a channel that appeared only after institutional changes. Kaoliang liquor could enter Taiwan not just because consumers liked it, but because it could finally circulate under a different set of monopoly rules. [^3]

Therefore, the liquor bottle can be both a gift for relatives and friends and a miniature model of the system. The origin, brand, year, and commemorative imagery on the bottle bring an offshore island to the center of the dining table. And when people drink it, they often do not need to know the entire administrative history behind it, yet they still drink in the atmosphere of Kinmen, the martial governance zone, and certain family gatherings.

> 📝 **Curator’s Note:** The history of Kinmen Kaoliang does not end when it shifts from "military" to "local." The true transformation is that a system designed for the frontline was ultimately used by local people to preserve their own identity.

## From Frontline Supplies to Local Memory

What is most worth leaving behind about Kinmen Kaoliang is not the easily repeated label of "Taiwanese National Liquor," but the contradiction on its body: it was established under military control, yet reinterpreted within local life. It was once a solution for frontline costs, later becoming the reliance for local finance and tourism. It was used by the state to represent a certain order, and by Kinmen people to remember their home.

Thus, Kinmen Kaoliang is not a linear brand story of success from 1952. It has Ye Huacheng being seen, and folk technology being appropriated. It has the market opening in 1977, and the constraints of the monopoly system. It has local autonomy after the end of martial governance in 1992, and the reality that the local community cannot easily lose distillery revenue. [^1] [^3]

Next time you see that transparent bottle, porcelain bottle, or commemorative bottle at a banquet, you might first recall a more concrete image: an island originally used for defense, allowing drought-resistant sorghum to grow in the land. A distillery established for frontline supply ultimately became a name that Kinmen people cannot bypass when discussing the local future. After the liquor is drunk, what remains is not a single answer, but how Kinmen slowly made a history arranged by the state into their own daily life.
