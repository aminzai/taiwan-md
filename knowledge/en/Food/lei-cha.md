> **30-second overview:** The sweet lei cha you drink in Beigun is not necessarily the ancient original of all Hakka lei cha. Research data shows that after the war, Hakka immigrants from the Hebei region of Guangdong Province brought the mortar, pestle and salty food habits to Taiwan; after the local tourism policy was launched in 1998, Beigun transformed it into a local product that can be experienced, packaged and taken away. [^1] [^2] The most interesting thing about this bowl of tea is that it preserves memory while admitting that it has already changed.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** This photo first puts "Beigun sweet lei cha" back in a specific place, rather than letting it drift into an abstract Hakka symbol; the photo can see a bowl of tea, and the article continues to ask: who brought it to Beigun, who changed it.

In March 1999, a lei cha-themed specialty shop appeared in the temple fair of Beigun's old street. This time point is important: lei cha was not invented that day, but it began to be treated as a local culture that could leave the family and let strangers pay to experience it. The research summary collected by the Hakka Affairs Commission calls it the first lei cha specialty shop in Taiwan, and attributes its later success to the cultural story, operation experience, policy support and industrial operation being in place at the same time. [^1]

There is the first counter-intuitive point here: **Beigun's most famous lei cha is not an ancient tradition that has never changed, but a food that was rediscovered and re-packaged after the war.**

### Don't rush to think of lei cha as a sweet drink

The verb "lei cha" is very specific: put tea leaves, grains, beans, nuts or herbs into a mortar, grind them into a paste with a pestle, and then dilute with hot water. The Beigun version described by the National Cultural Memory Bank is based on green tea or high mountain tea, peanuts, sesame and five grains, and then add green tea powder and millet when brewing. This is the sweet, thick, nutty form that most tourists are familiar with today. [^2]

But the same name, in other places, may be a bowl of salty soup. The cultural lecture data of the Overseas Chinese Affairs Commission lists the traditional salty lei cha that may use ginger, five-spice, cilantro, stir-fried greens, shrimp, radish and millet, and finally soak the rice food. The sweet version commonly seen in Beigun and Miaoli adds sugar, almonds or five-grain flour, which is more like a snack. [^3]

The local skills data of Hakka Cloud also describes lei cha as a food that can be adjusted according to seasonal materials: peanuts, sesame, sunflower seeds, barley, buckwheat can be put into the mortar, ginger, red onions and cilantro can also be added. Tea leaves are the indispensable core. This material flexibility is closer to the livability of lei cha than a fixed recipe. [^4]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Calling Beigun sweet lei cha the "only orthodoxy" is convenient, but it will erase the salty, spicy, rice-soaking and local differences in the same Hakka food tradition. Lei cha is not a single answer, but a way that can put the food on hand into it.

### A mortar, following people across the sea

The time of lei cha's entry into Taiwan provides relatively specific but still need to be kept in the range of clues. The research compiled by the Electronic Journal of the College of Hakka Studies, National Central University pointed out that around 1948, that is, in 1948, Hakka immigrants from the Hebei region of Guangdong Province brought the mortar, pestle and food culture to Taiwan. The article also reminded that at that time, lei cha was mainly still a family food, not yet a cultural experience that everyone can sign up for on the old street today. [^5]

The focus of this entry story is not "which year is the real start", but how lei cha followed a group of people into a new living environment. The food that was originally prepared for family members and guests at home had to face new tea leaf supply, local taste, work rhythm and the memory of the next generation. Materials can be replaced, salty and sweet can be replaced, but the social relationship of "grind together, eat together" is still preserved.

There are many origin stories of lei cha. Hakka TV has sorted out the stories of Zhang Fei leading troops, migration and refuge, and ancient tea cakes, but it also clearly pointed out that these commonly heard stories cannot be used as confirmed history. The safer way is to put lei cha into the long context of southern Chinese food, Hakka migration and local food skills, rather than specifying a dramatic single starting point for it. [^6]

This is also why the article should not say "lei cha originated in a certain year and a certain general". A legend can explain how people want to remember food, but it cannot replace files, fieldwork and cross-regional comparison.

### Beigun turns home-cooked flavor into an action

Around 1998, Beigun began to follow the "one township, one characteristic" tourism idea, and included lei cha, which was only circulated in some families, into local leisure activities. The field interviews recorded by the Electronic Journal of National Central University pointed out that in the early days of trying to launch salty lei cha, the taste was not necessarily easy to be accepted by ordinary tourists, and later adjusted to the direction of sweet taste and health-preserving drinks. [^5]

Hakka TV's program introduction also adopted a similar narrative: Beigun turned the original thick and filling salty soup into a sweet tea that is easier to be accepted by the market, and became an "lei cha town" under the promotion of local tourism. The program also kept a necessary question: some people think this is commercial packaging, but without this packaging, lei cha may not be seen by more people. [^6]

The policy context of 1998 and the opening of the specialty shop in March 1999 are not the same date. The former is the background of local development, the latter is the industrial node recorded in the research summary. It is wrong to force the two together to "invent lei cha in 1998" to make the story smooth.

> 📝 **Curator's note:** Cultural industries are most easily written as a straight-line story of "government preservation, residents benefit". Beigun lei cha is more like a negotiation: the local area needs a sign that can be understood, tourists need an experience that can be completed in an hour, shopkeepers need a taste that can be operated continuously, and tradition must find a place among these conditions.

### Why "self-grinding" is more important than drinking a cup

If lei cha is just putting the powder into a bag and diluting it with hot water, its difference from ordinary instant drinks will be reduced. But the experience of Beigun's old street puts the mortar, pestle, materials and the rotation of the arms back on the scene. Tourists are not only given a cup of drink, but also arranged into a segment of ordered labor: first grind the hard peanuts and sesame, then let the tea leaves and grains mix into the paste base, and finally add water, season and sprinkle millet.

The traditional craft data of Hakka Cloud records that the traditional preparation method does not strictly limit all ingredients, and the tea soup that is just brewed is very hot, and the host will sprinkle millet on the surface to remind the guests to eat slowly. This detail shows that lei cha is not something that can be replaced by the four words "health drink". It is simultaneously a tool, a gesture, a waiting time and a way of hospitality. [^4]

In Singapore, BBC Travel also recorded another living scene of Hakka lei cha rice: rice is placed next to vegetables, peanuts, dried fish and other ingredients, and the green tea soup is ground by herbs, sesame and peanuts. Family members are assigned to process the materials to make cooking itself a shared time. This is not the direct proof of Beigun's recipe, but it helps us to see that lei cha in the Hakka community is often not only "what to drink", but "how to do it together". [^7]

### Salty, sweet, spicy: not who replaces who

The popularity of Beigun sweet lei cha made many people know lei cha for the first time, and made "lei cha is sweet" become an intuition. But there are still different directions in the local cultural data: the preserver of Sanmin, Qiu Wen Shan, brought ginger, cilantro, red onion and broth back to the mortar, and made spicy and salty versions. The data also records that these products are made to differentiate the market in areas where lei cha shops are dense. [^4]

This example is very much in the flavor of Taiwan: tradition is not only one choice - either intact or commercialized destruction. It can also grow another local version through new shops, new materials and new consumption scenarios. Of course, the new version should not be masqueraded as the only ancient way. It needs to make its own changes clear.

Industry articles often describe lei cha as moving from Hakka daily life to DIY, instant noodle powder, pastry, shaved ice and overseas promotion. These changes do explain that lei cha can move out of the original family scene, but the claims about health effects, cultural heritage status or overseas market size should be checked one by one back to government data or research, and cannot be taken as evidence just because "health preservation" sounds reasonable. [^8]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** A tradition can survive does not mean it has never been changed; a more honest way is to say that it leaves traceable old traces in each change. Beigun's sweetness is one of the new routes, not to eliminate the other salty, spicy and rice-soaking versions.

### From hospitality to tourism experience

Lei cha is very suitable for entertaining guests, because it does not require the host to prepare a set of expensive and fixed menu. The mortar can put the grains, beans, nuts and herbs that are available at home, and the guests are not passive waiting for a cup of tea to be served, but enter a process that can see the materials, smell the fragrance and wait for the grinding. The National Cultural Memory Bank describes Beigun lei cha as a food brought by immigrants, convenient to obtain materials and suitable for hospitality. This "convenience" is not a cheap meaning, but the reason why it can survive in different families and places. [^2]

On the old street, the hospitality relationship is re-arranged. The shopkeeper needs to disassemble a piece of housework that may take a long time and may not be suitable for strangers to participate in into steps that tourists can understand. The weight of the pestle, the order of materials, the sweetness, all become a cultural lesson that can be completed in a short time. This is commoditization, but also translation: to translate a living skill of a community into someone who has no Hakka family experience.

This translation will of course lose something. The tacit understanding between family members, the familiarity with salty taste, who is responsible for washing the mortar, which material is left from the last season, it is not easy to put into a DIY process card. But if there is no such a new scene, it is also difficult for outsiders to know that lei cha is not just a sweet powder with hot water.

The research summary of the Hakka Affairs Commission breaks down the success factors of Beigun's industry into cultural stories, cultural experiences, cultural policies and business methods. This decomposition is worth noting, because it does not treat culture as packaging, nor does it treat business as pollution, but acknowledges that both occur in the local industry at the same time. [^1]

When reading these data, you should also pay attention to their respective positions. The National Cultural Memory Bank is more like a description card of local objects, the research summary is concerned with how the industry is formed, the Electronic Journal of the Academy puts the field interviews and documents together, and Hakka TV uses the program narrative to tell the controversy to the public. They are not alternative testimonies. Only by putting different sources together can we avoid using a shopkeeper's experience to represent all Hakka villages.

For example, the materials and methods of Beigun sweet lei cha cannot be directly used to define the lei cha of East China, Guangdong or Singapore. The tea soup with vegetables and rice written by BBC is another version of a Hakka community in the city to preserve and transform. The Sanmin spicy lei cha recorded by Hakka Cloud is another creation of a local shop in Taiwan. They all prove that "lei cha can change" is not "all lei cha is the same". [^4] [^7]

This difference also makes the word "Hakka representative" heavy. Representation is not to push a taste into the only standard, but to let more people know that there are multiple local routes under a name. The real preservation includes the preservation of differences themselves.

For Beigun, the locality of lei cha is still hidden in the place: the old street, the temple fair, the shopkeeper and the farmers' association plan, which makes a family food have a fixed viewing position. After leaving Beigun, it is taken to other Hakka villages, overseas classrooms and urban dining tables, and becomes a new local version. This is not a one-way road from "tradition" to "modern", but the continuous re-takeover of different communities.

Therefore, the future of lei cha is not only whether it can sell more packaging, but also whether the shopkeeper and teacher are willing to explain the difference: is it sweet or salty? Is it Beigun's formula or the shopkeeper's creation? Is it family memory or tourism process? The clearer the explanation, the more consumers can put a cup of tea back to its real history.

### What a bowl of tea leaves behind

Today, when you come to Beigun to drink lei cha, the easiest thing to take away is a bag of powder, a DIY photo, or a label of "Hakka characteristics". It is more difficult to take away the time difference: a family food that came to Taiwan around the war with immigrants, through the local tourism policy of the 1990s and the industrialization of the specialty shop in 1999, became a Hakka symbol that can be recognized throughout Taiwan. [^1] [^5]

This history has not weakened the cultural value of lei cha, but made it more worthy of understanding. It reminds us that tradition is often not an object that is passed down from ancestors without any damage, but a method that is repeatedly adjusted by people in order to live in a new place, be understood by others, and be willing to approach the next generation.

Therefore, lei cha does not need to be ancient with a myth, nor does it need to be useful with the word "health preservation". Its most precious place is that a set of living skills that can still be learned, changed and explained.

When the beginner first holds the pestle, the wrist does not know how to turn, the person beside him usually tells him not to be in a hurry. This small teaching action is closer to the cultural core of lei cha: someone passes the method to the next person.

So, the next time the pestle turns in the mortar, you can first not be in a hurry to ask "is this the only orthodoxy". You can ask more specifically: what tea is used in this bowl? Why is it sweet? Who brought it here? Who changed it into an experience after 1998? And the salty taste that has not been served on the tourism table, has it ever fed anyone?

What lei cha really leaves behind is not only the fragrance of tea leaves and peanuts ground, but also a very Hakka method: put what you have on hand together, grind slowly, and let strangers also sit down to eat a bowl.

If this bowl of tea has a most reliable conclusion, it may not be where it started a few hundred years ago, but that it is still someone willing to spend time grinding today. Tradition is therefore not far away, but at the next time someone puts the pestle in your hand.

And this is exactly what Beigun lei cha is most worth remembering: it is not an answer waiting to be preserved intact, but a question that is still being used.
