At four a.m., the fish ponds of Qigu are still dark. Workers in rubber boots open the pond gates, and the fish schools surge at the sound of water. Milkfish cannot tolerate low temperatures, so winter requires heating equipment; the summer harvest season begins at dawn—if the fish are not harvested, the porridge stalls in downtown Tainan will have to open their “windows” the next morning. This supply chain from Qigu to Guohua Street ran its first leg more than three hundred years ago.

This fish has nothing to do with Koxinga.

## The Most Popular Story Is Also the Least Accurate

Folklore says: when Koxinga arrived in Taiwan, his troops ran low on food. By the sea at Anping they saw a fish, and he asked his aides, “What kind of fish is this?” The Hokkien word for “what” was misheard as “milk‑eye,” and the name stuck.

The tale appears at temple entrances, in textbooks, and on tourism brochures—emotional, visual, and easy to remember. Yet historians have long pointed out the problem: the Dutch East India Company brought milkfish aquaculture techniques from Indonesia during the Dutch period (1624–1662), at least thirty‑seven years before Koxinga’s 1661 arrival. The late‑17th‑century _Taiwan Fu Zhi_ (1694) already records milkfish farming, with the first sites around Lukangmen—today’s Anping area. Over four hundred years.

📝 Curator’s note: The legend that “Koxinga brought milkfish” illustrates a point: a good story can outlive historical evidence. The Dutch introduced the technique; Koxinga seized the naming rights.

## Qigu: The Geography of Fish Ponds

Today Tainan remains Taiwan’s largest milkfish production region, accounting for roughly 50 % of the nation’s output. The core is Qigu District—over 4,500 hectares of aquaculture area and more than 6,000 individual ponds, the most in the country.

Qigu’s terrain is key: low‑lying coastal land, silty substrate, moderate salinity—naturally suited for ponds. It is about a forty‑minute drive from downtown Tainan, yet the roads are flanked by endless water surfaces, egrets perched on pond embankments, and the Taiwan Strait beyond.

From Anping to the porridge stalls on Guohua Street the straight‑line distance is under fifteen kilometres; even from Qigu it does not exceed forty kilometres. This short supply chain is the material basis for cheap, fresh milkfish breakfasts in Tainan.

📝 Curator’s note: Qigu not only farms milkfish; it is also one of the world’s most important wintering sites for the black‑faced spoonbill. Wet‑land conservation and fishery interests have long been in tension here—ponds are not merely the starting point of food, but also a field of ecological politics.

## The Anatomy of the Whole Fish

Tainan residents eat milkfish without waste. This stems from the breakfast logic of dockworkers: each part has its proper place.

- **Fish belly (abdomen)** – richest in fat, best pan‑fried; the skin is crisp while the flesh stays soft, giving two textures in one bite.
- **Fish fillet (back meat)** – ideal for porridge; fresh, sweet, slightly elastic, and aromatic without overpowering.
- **Fish skin** – high in gelatin, perfect for soup; drinking it leaves a slight stickiness on the lips—that’s collagen, not an additive.
- **Fish intestines** – deep‑fried until crunchy; they demand utmost freshness and are the hardest to replicate outside the region.
- **Fish head** – braised or stewed; gelatinous and suited to slow cooking.
- **Fish roe** – seasonal, either pan‑fried or marinated; a delicacy known only to regulars.

Every cut is precise because each part deserves individual treatment.

📝 Curator’s note: The “part‑by‑part” culture of milkfish is essentially a zero‑waste worker‑diet logic. When you order at a Tainan porridge stall you are not just choosing ingredients; you are expressing a different understanding of a single fish. Tourists tend to order the “mixed” option; regulars have clear preferences.

## Dawn‑Time White Porridge

At 5:30 a.m., the porridge stall separates the various parts, briefly blanches them, and places each into a small dish for the customer to add to their white porridge.

This self‑service system is not a culinary invention; it is efficiency. Dockworkers eat breakfast with no time to wait for a chef to decide what goes into their bowl. The ingredients sit there; you know what you need to supplement or avoid—if you’ve expended a lot of energy, add more fish intestines; if you drank heavily the night before, take extra clear porridge with fish‑skin soup.

The logic of three‑century‑old aquaculture still lives in today’s morning rhythm.

## The Name Remains Unresolved

Scholars have yet to reach a consensus on the origin of the name “milkfish.”

Some argue it derives from a Dutch or Malay transliteration; others trace it to the Taiwanese term _sè‑bó_ (細目), referring to the fish’s fine‑spotted eyes; still others suggest that workers in various farming locales coined their own names, which later became standardized in Taiwanese.

The story of Koxinga asking “what fish?” remains the most widely spread version. Sometimes the most memorable story is not the most accurate—but it has given the four‑century‑old aquaculture industry a name that appears on every breakfast table.
