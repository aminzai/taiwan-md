# Roast Meat Bento: How a Craft Brought by Hong Kong Chefs Became the Taiwanese "Three Dishes and One Meat" Meal

At noon, a small line of people usually forms in front of the glass display case at a roast meat shop. Some point to the glossy char siu, some ask if the roasted pork is crispy today, and others simply say: "Three Treasures Rice, with an egg." This action seems so routine that we rarely ask: why did a roast meat dish that matured in Guangdong and Hong Kong become one of the most convenient lunches in Taiwan?

The answer lies not just in the meat, nor just in immigration. It lies in one entrepreneur, a batch of cross-sea chefs, and the Taiwanese stubbornness regarding "how many dishes a bento must have."

## First, Deconstruct "Roast Meat": It Was Never Just One Dish

"Roast meat" (shaolao) is often treated as a shop name or a bento category, but it is actually the collective term for two sets of techniques. "Roast" (shao) primarily refers to roast meats made with open fire or high-temperature baking, such as char siu (barbecued pork), roasted pork (siu yuk), and roast duck. "Cured" (la) relates to marinating, air-drying, and preservation, such as Chinese sausage (lap cheong) and cured meat. The **siu mei** (roast meat) commonly used in Hong Kong is closer to "a general term for roasted meat products," not a fixed dish in a Taiwanese bento shop. [^1]

This distinction is important. Because when roast meat left its original culinary environment, it brought not only a set of grilling techniques but also the shop language of "meat hung for display, freshly cut, and sold by portion." The glass display cases, hanging meat hooks, and chopping sounds familiar in Taiwan later were all a production method that put the craft directly on the street for people to see.

## 1967: A Roast Meat Shop Puts Hong Kong Flavors into Taipei’s Daily Life

Sun Dong Yang’s official history places the story’s starting point in 1967. That year, Mai Hsing-fu operated "Dongyang Roast Meat Shop" on Wuchang Street in Taipei. This shop later became the predecessor of Sun Dong Yang. According to the company’s self-account, Sun Dong Yang established itself with roast meat and meat floss products from the beginning, incorporating Hong Kong-style roast meat into its storefronts and souvenir business. [^2]

This starting point has an easily overlooked aspect: roast meat did not enter Taiwan first in the form of high-end restaurants, but was linked early on with retail, takeout, gift boxes, and ritual needs. In other words, it was never just tasted at the dining table; it was also taken home from stores, given as gifts, and even brought into another kind of ritual.

This gave it the conditions for popularization. High-end Cantonese cuisine requires seats, time, and a complete menu. Roast meat, however, can complete a transaction between a piece of meat, a knife, and a bento box. It retains the drama of displaying craft but does not require customers to understand the entire Cantonese culinary context.

## 1988: Sun Dong Yang Turns "Chefs" into an Industrial Chain

What truly changed the density of Taiwan’s roast meat was 1988. Sun Dong Yang’s official account states that in the 77th year of the Republic of China (1988), it was the first to recruit roast meat chefs from Hong Kong, launching Hong Kong-style roast meat operations in Taipei’s Zhongcheng District, and believes this action drove the trend of Taiwan’s roast meat culture. [^3]

A StoryStudio report provides a more detailed folk version: from the late 1970s to the early 1980s, Taiwan was not only receiving scattered Hong Kong chefs who came for marriage or personal relationships, but enterprises were also recruiting roast meat chefs from Hong Kong. Sun Dong Yang sold roast meat bento, roast meat gift boxes, and roast meat for worship or sacrificial offerings at its stores. Some chefs, after their contracts ended, partnered with Taiwanese friends or asked Taiwanese people to help with renting shops and opening businesses. [^4]

The key here is not that "Sun Dong Yang invented Taiwan’s roast meat." A more accurate statement is: the enterprise turned an industry that relied on individual craft into a network that could be learned, replicated, and disseminated.

One chef brings one flavor. A batch of chefs may bring an entry point for an entire industry. As the Taipei market became crowded, they moved to other counties and cities. StoryStudio’s report records this path of diffusion outward from Taipei, noting that chefs would use their leave to return to Hong Kong to find old colleagues, relatives, and people with skills, forming a cross-border interpersonal network of "one pulling another." [^5]

## Why Did Hong Kong’s Roast Meat Ultimately Become a Taiwanese Bento?

Because Taiwan did not accept everything as written.

In Hong Kong, the common way to eat roast meat can be roast meat rice, noodles, congee, or a la carte platter. In Taiwan, it was placed into bento logic. White rice became the base, meat became the main dish, and there had to be several side dishes to make people feel "this is a meal." Cabbage, braised eggs, pickled vegetables, tomato and egg stir-fry, or other home-style dishes may not belong to a fixed set of Hong Kong roast meat traditions, but they have found their own place in Taiwanese bento.

Taiwan’s Ministry of Finance industry classification also defines "bento and buffet shops" as stores that provide fixed dishes such as bento and lunch boxes, or allow customers to select dishes in a self-service manner. Its characteristic is pre-payment before eating, with relatively affordable product prices. [^6] This institutional definition explains that bento is not merely a container, but a method of serving food: fixed, fast, takeout-friendly, and making customers feel the portion and variety are worth it.

Therefore, the "Hong Kong style" of Taiwanese roast meat shops has never been an unchanged specimen. On one hand, it retains the marinating and grilling of char siu, the crispy skin of roasted pork, and the poaching of oil chicken. On the other hand, it must answer Taiwanese customers’ questions: Is there enough rice? Are there too few side dishes? Can the sauce be mixed with rice? Can the price serve as a weekday lunch?

This is why the "Three Treasures Rice" is so representative. It compresses decision fatigue into one name: char siu, roasted pork, oil chicken, or duck meat, allowing customers to gain multiple textures simultaneously, and allowing shops to place different cuts and methods in the same bento. The Three Treasures Rice is not the only answer to Hong Kong tradition, but rather the way Taiwanese bento culture organizes roast meat.

## The Sound of a Knife: Roast Meat Shops Also Brought Street Performance

What is most easily remembered about roast meat shops may not be the flavor, but the sound. StoryStudio records the Hong Kong chefs’ calls when chopping meat, the back of the knife tapping the cutting board, and the action of embedding a large cleaver into the board. The report’s explanation is that these actions are related to the dense concentration and mutual competition of Hong Kong roast meat shops in certain districts: the sound is both calling customers and announcing to neighboring shops "there is business here." [^5]

This story may not represent every roast meat shop, nor is there sufficient data to prove that all chefs follow the same tradition; but it provides a good perspective for observation. Roast meat shops do not hide the kitchen in the back; they make chopping, hanging meat, and part of the meal service public to people on the street. The glass display case is a showcase, and the cutting board is like a stage; what customers buy is not just meat, but a craft that can be seen and heard.

## An Immigration Story That Cannot Be Simplified to "Everyone Came After 1997"

The video lists the immigration wave around Hong Kong’s handover in the 1990s as one of the reasons for the increase in roast meat shops. This direction has intuitive appeal, as 1997 is indeed an important dividing line in Hong Kong’s history. However, based on currently available data, it is insufficient to write "Hong Kong roast meat chefs immigrated to Taiwan in large numbers" as a conclusion with clear numbers, a single time point, and direct causality.

A more stable statement is: the expansion of Taiwan’s roast meat simultaneously includes enterprise recruitment, individual migration, marriage, and introductions by relatives and friends. StoryStudio’s local memory emphasizes the recruitment in the late 1970s and early 1980s, and the subsequent shop-opening network formed by chefs and acquaintances. Academic research places the changes of Cantonese cuisine in Taiwan and Taiwanese cuisine in Hong Kong within the social, economic, and identity shifts between China, Taiwan, and Hong Kong. [^7]

This slower, less dramatic version is closer to how immigration truly changes a city. Culture usually does not arrive in batches on a single day; it is first demonstrated by a few people, then given space by enterprises, then introduced by acquaintances to the next person, and finally becomes a streetscape that "was always here."

## When "Hong Kong Flavor" Finds Its Own Price in Taiwan

Today, the price difference in Taiwanese roast meat shops is large: traditional street-corner shops can be a weekday lunch, while department store or airport brands may become more expensive meals. Sun Dong Yang officially describes Mai Ji Roast Meat as a brand continuing from 1967, and in 2022, it re-launched with a Japanese-style counter-style refined roast meat restaurant. This shows that the same craft can move between affordable bento, souvenirs, and fine dining. [^8]

On the other hand, in 2024, the number of business locations for Taiwan’s "bento and buffet shops" broke through 10,000, reaching 10,176, with annual sales of approximately NT$31.52 billion, according to statistics mainly reported by media citing Ministry of Finance data. This set of numbers covers the entire bento and buffet sector and does not equal the number of roast meat shops. But it still provides a useful background: bento has become a massive daily market capable of容纳ing various immigrant flavors, local tastes, and chain models. [^9]

Roast meat has stayed not because Taiwanese suddenly became very knowledgeable about Cantonese cuisine, but because it found an extremely stable exchange: chefs provide technology, enterprises provide channels, bento provides price and speed, and customers fix this cross-sea flavor with their daily meal.

Next time you stand in front of a roast meat shop, consider that row of hanging meat as a short immigration map. Char siu and roasted pork point to the cooking traditions of Guangdong and Hong Kong; Dongyang Roast Meat Shop points to the commercial streets of 1960s Taipei; Sun Dong Yang in 1988 points to how enterprises brought chefs into Taiwan; the white rice, braised eggs, and green vegetables beside the glass case explain that this craft has learned to complete a meal in the Taiwanese way.

So, why are there roast meat shops everywhere on the streets of Taiwan? Not because Taiwan was merely waiting for an外来 food to be transplanted, but because after someone brought the craft, Taiwan’s market, bento, and daily life took over and rewrote it again.

What you finally eat is neither a pure Hong Kong roast meat nor a Taiwanese bento that has lost its origin. It is two histories stacked together: a craft that crossed the sea, and an island’s ability to turn foreign flavors into the daily.
