> **30-Second Overview:** Sweet potato balls (also known as QQ eggs) are the most representative "hollow" snack in Taiwan's night markets. Their lineage traces back to the Tang Dynasty's "Ludui," yet in the 1970s, an engineering accident in New Taipei City imbued them with a sweet potato soul through the spirit of frugality. The deliciousness of sweet potato balls stems from the mechanical miracle of "pressing": through precise oil temperatures of 130°C and repeated squeezing, solid dough is made to drain oil and absorb air, expanding into crispy, hollow golden spheres. This serves as a microcosm of Taiwan's civilian culture—flexible, resilient, and full of surprises.

In any night market in Taiwan, you can always hear the rhythmic "clang, clang" sound, the rhythm of iron ladles colliding with the oil pot. Following this is a pot of solid purple and yellow dough, which expands to twice its size like an inflating balloon in just a few minutes. This is not magic, but a familiar yet extraordinary civilian snack for Taiwanese people: the sweet potato ball.

### Echoes of History: From "Ludui" to the Frugal Accident Beneath Zhongzheng Bridge

Sweet potato balls did not appear out of nowhere. From the perspective of dietary archaeology, their lineage originates from the "Ludui" (also known as fried dough), which was popular in the palaces and among the folk during the Tang Dynasty [^5]. This snack, made of glutinous rice dough coated with sesame seeds and fried until hollow, subsequently evolved into the familiar "sesame ball." However, the transformation of sweet potato balls in Taiwan carries a strong local character.

1. **The Accident Beneath Zhongzheng Bridge**: In the 1970s, when the government was regulating the riverbed of the Xindian River in New Taipei City, an excavator damaged a sweet potato field beneath the Zhongzheng Bridge in New Taipei. An elderly retiree who farmed there watched the broken, unmarketable sweet potatoes scattered on the ground. Unable to bear the waste, he steamed them, mashed them into a paste, mixed them with inexpensive tapioca starch available at the time, formed them into small balls, and deep-fried them [^1][^2].
2. **The Starting Point of Commercialization**: In the late 1970s, this snack began to be commercialized at the Zhonghua Road Night Market and Zhongxiao Night Market in Taichung, subsequently sweeping across the entire island [^4]. It replaced the "glutinous rice" of traditional sesame balls with "sweet potato," a stronger symbol of local Taiwanese identity, and reduced the size, making it a convenient snack for walking.

📝 Curator's Note: The soul of the sweet potato ball lies not in what it "is," but in what it "is not"—it is a cultural alchemy that transforms surplus materials into sensory pleasure, representing the most resilient expression of Taiwan's "sweet potato complex."

### The Miracle of Physics: The Mechanical Game of 130°C and 15 Seconds

The most fascinating scene of sweet potato balls is undoubtedly the stall owner holding a special large strainer, continuously "pressing" the spheres in the oil pot. This seemingly repetitive physical labor is actually precise physical control.

- **Starch Gelatinization and Shell Setting**: When the dough touches the high-temperature oil at 130°C, the surface starch gelatinizes rapidly, forming a shell that is elastic and airtight [^7][^19].
- **The "Sky Lantern Effect" Inside**: The moisture inside the sweet potato ball vaporizes due to heat, causing the volume to expand. The owner's squeezing action is not to flatten it, but to "drain oil and let air in."
- **The Critical 15 Seconds**: In the later stages of frying, the owner must repeatedly squeeze at the critical 15 seconds. Each squeeze expels some of the internal hot air and residual oil; upon release, fresh air enters and expands again due to the heat [^10][^19].

"Frying sweet potato balls is like releasing sky lanterns," a stall owner who has been operating in the night market for over twenty years once described, "You have to give it pressure for it to know how to fly." [^8]

### The Invisible Pushers of the Industrial Chain: The Century-Long Evolution of Tapioca Starch

The reason sweet potato balls can achieve a "hollow and chewy" texture lies not in the sweet potato, but in the "tapioca starch" (cassava starch).

1. **Introduction During the Japanese Colonial Period**: In 1902, the Japanese government introduced new varieties of cassava to Taiwan for trial planting, opening the history of Taiwan's cassava industry [^9].
2. **Shift in Processing Technology**: After the Nationalist Government came to Taiwan, tapioca starch became the pillar of Taiwan's food processing industry. From the pearls in bubble tea to the dough of sweet potato balls, all rely on this starch with high viscosity and low protein content [^9][^10].
3. **From Handmade to Standardization**: Most sweet potato ball stalls today no longer start with steamed sweet potatoes; instead, they use "pre-mixed powder" formulated from tapioca starch, sweet potato starch, and modified starch. This ensures stable expansion in the 130°C oil pot, marking the transition of sweet potato balls from homemade family recipes to the era of industrial SOPs [^18][^20].

### Sweet Potato Balls vs. QQ Eggs: A North-South War on Texture

In Taiwan, this golden small ball exhibits significant "North-South differences," which are reflected not only in names but also in the pursuit of texture.

| Feature                 | North: Sweet Potato Ball                        | South: QQ Egg                                                   |
| :---------------------- | :---------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Naming Habit**        | Commonly called "Sweet Potato Ball"             | Commonly called "QQ Egg" in Kaohsiung and Pingtung [^12]        |
| **Core Texture**        | Pursues "large and hollow," with a crispy shell | Pursues "chewy elasticity," retaining some solid interior [^14] |
| **Visual Presentation** | Usually larger in volume, lighter in color      | More delicate in volume, darker golden yellow [^15]             |

This difference even sparked online heated discussions, with northern students being corrected when ordering "sweet potato balls" in the south, or southerners feeling lost when they couldn't find "QQ eggs" in Taipei [^12]. Regardless of the name, the layered sensation of "crispy outside, soft inside" remains a shared gustatory memory for Taiwanese people.

### Price and Branding: Commercialization Controversies of Civilian Cuisine

As sweet potato balls have evolved from street stalls to chain franchise systems, this small ball has fallen into debates over price and value.

In the early days, 20 or 30 NTD per serving was the norm for sweet potato balls. However, with rising rent and labor costs, some influencer-famous shops have pushed the price of large portions to between 50 and 100 NTD [^18][^20]. This shift has triggered polarized evaluations: one faction of consumers believes this is the inevitable result of branding and quality stability; another faction laments that sweet potato balls are gradually departing from the definition of "civilian" food.

📝 Curator's Note: When the price of a sweet potato ball begins to catch up to that of a pork chop rice bento, it challenges not only the consumer's wallet but also the connection between this snack and the history of the "frugal accident." When we bite into this hollow sphere, are we consuming a texture, or are we consuming a street scene that is disappearing?
