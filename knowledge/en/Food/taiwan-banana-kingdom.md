> **30-Second Overview:** In 1967, Taiwan exported nearly 400,000 metric tons of bananas annually, capturing 82% of the Japanese market; by 2014, exports had shrunk to just 4,000 metric tons, or about 1%. This disparity is not merely a story of Philippine large-scale farms defeating Taiwanese smallholders; it also concerns a system Taiwan once established that linked volume, quality, transportation, and pricing, which was later abandoned after market liberalization.

In 1967, Qishan banana farmers sent bunches of bananas into the Port of Kaohsiung. That year, Taiwanese bananas held an 82% share of the Japanese market. [^1] Half a century later, in the summer of 2000, the price of bananas in Qishan’s collection center dropped from NT$10 per kilogram to NT$1, with piles of fruit waiting to rot. [^2]

Bananas may appear to be the most ordinary of fruits, yet they once served as Taiwan’s most expensive channel for foreign exchange. They propelled the Qishan Farmers’ Association’s deposits to the top of the nation and enabled a small southern town to develop financial, transportation, and entertainment industries. That same channel later exposed Taiwan’s smallholders to Philippine large-scale plantations, multinational brands, and shifting Japanese consumer habits. [^3]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** What truly matters about the "Banana Kingdom" is not how many bananas Taiwan once sold, but how smallholders once relied on a collective system to outperform large plantations in the Japanese market.

## The Scent of Wealth in Qishan’s Banana Oil

In 2000, 79-year-old Chang Jung-yao harvested bananas in a 1.5-hectare field in Qishan. He cut the bunches with a worn, horn-shaped knife; the banana oil seeping from the cuts dripped onto his clothes, leaving an indelible sour smell. [^2] This detail serves as a kind of fable: the same stain once signaled wealth in his youth, but now only signifies hard labor.

Chang Jung-yao remembers that in Qishan’s most bustling days, banana farmers would walk into tea houses wearing oil-stained clothes, and others knew it was the mark of a respectable profession. Qishan was once the place with the densest concentration of banana farms in Taiwan, accounting for nearly one-third of the national output. The local Farmers’ Association was known locally as "the richest bank in Taiwan." [^2]

This prosperity did not appear out of nowhere. Scholar Chuang Shu-tzu divides the Taiwanese banana industry’s system into four phases: pre-1946 domestic trade under Japanese rule, the 1950–1963 export joint venture system, the 1963–1973 55-export system, and the 1974–2006 single production-and-sales system. [^4] Banana history is thus not just about "farmers growing, merchants selling," but a history of how markets were organized. Taiwanese archival research also identifies the 1963 liberalization of Japanese banana imports as a turning point in the system, indicating that changes in market rules were more critical than the rise and fall of a single crop. [^10]

## A Yellow Route from Keelung to Tokyo

The story of Taiwan’s banana exports to Japan began in the early 20th century. In 1903, Keelung merchants transported bananas to the Japanese mainland. Around 1910, Taiwan entered a phase of large-scale cultivation. By 1924, exports to Japan exceeded 100,000 metric tons; the following year, the Taiwan Fruit Company was established, and the export network began to take on a fixed organizational structure. [^5]

During the Japanese colonial period, bananas first served as an export crop for colonial agriculture, later becoming the backbone of southern Taiwan’s local economy. Qishan did not prosper in isolation: railways and ports connected the groves to cities, packing houses and collection stations transformed fragile, easily bruised fruit into calculable commodities, and financial institutions preserved the foreign exchange earnings for the producing regions. [^4]

After World War II, Taiwan resumed banana exports to Japan in 1950. In 1963, Japan lifted import restrictions on bananas, causing Taiwan’s banana exports to Japan to jump from 58,000 metric tons in 1962 to 427,000 metric tons in 1967. Foreign exchange earnings that year exceeded $63 million. [^5] Agricultural authorities coordinated production and sales systems and transportation between 1958 and 1967, assisting in the establishment of 440 banana and citrus packing houses in producing areas, responsible for collection, grading, and packaging. [^5]

Here lies an easily overlooked contrast: the Banana Kingdom was not built by a single "Banana King," but by packing houses, cooperatives, ports, transportation, and export rules. Farmers saw a bunch of bananas; the system saw an unbroken cold chain and price chain.

## The Banana Shed in the Port, Holding a Nation’s Foreign Exchange Dream

In 1956, the Port of Kaohsiung specially opened its Third Wharf to handle bananas bound for Japan. Between 1962 and 1963, the government used US aid loans to construct a banana shed of approximately 2,400 square meters, capable of holding over 20,000 banana baskets. In 2010, this space was renamed the "Banana Pier," becoming part of local memory. [^5]

Bananas at that time could not be packed immediately after harvest. Farmers had to harvest at the appropriate maturity level; collection centers had to wash, grade, and pre-cool the fruit; and the export system had to align with Japanese market shipping schedules. Data from the Council of Agriculture indicates that bananas are typically harvested at about 70–80% maturity, pre-cooled for one to two days, and then ripened with ethylene gas to maintain texture and aroma upon arrival. [^6]

This process explains why bananas drove local industrial development rather than merely increasing farmland area. It required people who could read the fruit, workers skilled in grading, ships that could dock on time, and a system capable of temporarily supporting farmers when prices plummeted.

What remains of the banana shed today is not just a roof and shelves, but evidence of how a place transformed agricultural produce into export infrastructure. When it was renamed the Banana Pier, its function shifted from collection to exhibition, and the locality simultaneously faced a question: if history becomes merely a tourist sign, can the production conditions of banana farmers still be seen? This is the most difficult aspect of industrial cultural heritage—it must allow people to see the past, without pretending the past is the present.

## After 1967, the Kingdom Did Not Fall in a Day

Taiwanese banana exports peaked in 1967: nearly 400,000 metric tons, capturing 82% of the Japanese market. By 2014, exports had shrunk to 4,000 metric tons, with market share dropping to 1%. [^1] This disparity is often described as "Philippine large farms defeating Taiwan," but that only tells half the story.

The first turning point was supply scale. Philippine and Central American multinational plantations could manage hundreds to thousands of hectares of land uniformly. Taiwanese banana farmers’ land was often only half a hectare to a few hectares. Taiwanese agricultural economist Peng Tso-kuei pointed out that the smallholder system struggled to compete with large plantations on cost. [^2]

The second turning point was the changing Japanese market. Japanese importers gradually demanded smaller packaging and stable supply, but Taiwanese traders were unwilling to change their habit of selling whole bunches for just a few bananas. Philippine multinational companies, however, offered unified specifications, long-term supply, and compensation for shortages, allowing Japanese buyers to stop guessing quality daily. [^7]

The third turning point occurred within Taiwan’s own system. In 2005, the government ended the monopoly of fruit cooperatives on banana exports, opening the market to multiple competing traders. Reports from CommonWealth Magazine indicate that some traders signed contracts for large-scale planting without corresponding quality and market support. Bananas that could not be exported returned to the domestic market, causing prices to fall amid oversupply. [^7]

Market liberalization itself is not a crime. The problem was that once the system linking volume, quality, and sales channels was dismantled, a new system did not emerge with equal speed. Taiwanese banana farmers gained more choices, but not necessarily greater bargaining power.

## A Convenience Store Banana, Two Industrial Destinies

In 2014, Taiwan’s convenience stores sold approximately 100,000 bananas daily. Annually, the sales volume of a single chain outlet even exceeded Taiwan’s total annual banana exports. [^7] On the surface, this seemed like bananas re-entering daily life; on the other hand, it meant that after the export industry shrank, domestic channels became the new competitive arena.

Dole brought individual bananas into convenience stores, introducing maturity control, packaging, and brand management to the Taiwanese market. Industry figures at the Taiwan Banana Research Institute held differing views: some believed multinational brands increased the commodity value of bananas and assisted in exporting new varieties; others worried that as channels were controlled by foreign companies, Taiwanese farmers would be more easily replaced by low-priced Philippine bananas. [^7]

This debate has no simple answer. Consumers want a banana they can buy today, with consistent size and predictable ripeness. Banana farmers need income that allows them to survive typhoons, diseases, and price fluctuations. Both are reasonable, but they are not necessarily served by the same channel.

## No Single Banana in the Kingdom

"Taiwan bananas" are not a single, unchanging variety. Data from the Council of Agriculture lists the "Tai-Chiao" (North Banana) as Taiwan’s traditional representative variety, with a growth period of about 12 months. Red skin bananas and Luzon bananas each have different maturity periods, appearances, and market positions. [^6] These differences are not just botanical knowledge; they directly determine whether banana farmers can diversify risks from typhoons, diseases, and price fluctuations.

Bananas’ vulnerability is also hidden in their appearance. Mature plants have large leaves and fragile stems, making them susceptible to wind damage. Qishan banana farmers therefore planted a mix of tissue-culture seedlings, sucker seedlings, and ratoon shoots, staggering production periods across different fields. When a banana plant was blown down by a typhoon, the loss was not an abstract "agricultural value" for one season, but a bunch of fruit that took a full year to produce. [^6]

The peak of the 1960s left another question: the more concentrated the production, the greater the cost of diseases and price fluctuations. The establishment of the Taiwan Banana Research Institute was precisely to breed, improve, and provide new seedlings. The Banana Kingdom later recovered not by expanding land, but through research that rewrote "delicious" into conditions of wind resistance, disease resistance, and traceability. [^5]

## Bananas Did Not Disappear, They Only Changed the Question

The Council of Agriculture’s Qishan report records another answer: today’s banana farmers no longer pursue mass production alone, but attempt to improve quality through field management, natural farming methods, and production traceability. Bananas remain annual crops, with each plant producing only one bunch. After harvest, the banana stalk is removed, and the next generation begins anew from suckers or ratoon shoots. [^6]

Qishan’s industry is not just about nostalgia. Academic research indicates that the decline of bananas caused Qishan to lose its past status as a functional center, but bananas themselves became the locality’s collective memory and cultural heritage. The locality began transforming banana industry into cultural tourism capital. [^4] This is not merely packaging agriculture as a tourist attraction to claim revival, but acknowledging that a locality’s prosperity once depended on a supply chain that has since changed course.

The art team Pailang Museum’s _A Concise Lexicon of Banana_ places bananas back into a broader context of colonial and global commodity crops: bananas are both edible fruit and symbols of currency, shipping, finance, and colonial economy. This perspective reminds us that Taiwan’s banana story is not an isolated local legend, but a microcosm of how an island is shaped by global markets. [^9]

Buying a Qishan banana today does not buy you the 1967 Japanese market share, nor a Banana Kingdom that can be restored intact. It buys you the farmers’ long-term judgment regarding typhoons, diseases, ripeness, and prices. It buys you a locality’s attempt, after losing its export peak, to turn industrial memory into a new way of survival.

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** The end of the Banana Kingdom is not "Taiwan no longer knows how to grow bananas," but rather that Taiwan must re-answer: when smallholders cannot win over large farms by volume, what else can make a banana worth keeping?
