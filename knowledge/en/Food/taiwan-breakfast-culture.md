# Taiwan Breakfast Culture: Twenty Thousand Shops Selling the Same Breakfast, and No One Owns It

> **30-second overview:** The breakfast shop that Taiwanese people walk into every day has no author. _Danbing_ (savory pancake with egg), _tieban mian_ (griddle noodles), and _da bini_ (large sweetened soy milk) all have no inventor to speak of — the methods have no owner, so the methods spread through every alleyway, and no one was harmed in the process. Only the signboards differ: Lin Kun-yen opened Mei Wei Mei in 1981, later losing control of his own shop name's trademark, with one signboard splitting into a street full of Mei You Mei, Hong Ye, and Morning Beauty Mei Zhi Cheng. As for those standing in front of the griddle every day: there are over twenty thousand breakfast shops across Taiwan, yet the official industry classification doesn't even have a category for "breakfast shop."

6:10 a.m. The griddle has been sizzling for half an hour. On the left, the egg pancake is spread open, the yolk sliding in, the edges curling into a ring of brown. On the right, the fried noodles are flipped once in the black pepper sauce. The sealing machine presses down, _snap_—a cup of iced milk tea is sealed, and condensation immediately beads on the plastic cup wall.

This sequence is playing out simultaneously on every street in Taiwan right now. Step outside for five minutes and you'll pass four signs that look almost identical yet somehow different, four shop owners who don't know each other, their menus nearly word-for-word the same.

## The Breakfast Shop on the Alley and the One Three Streets Over Sell the Same Breakfast

You run into this every day, you just never get told why. Merry's, Merry's again, Hongye Hamburg, Good Morning McDonald's — four signs, four different companies, four different franchise systems, four different bosses. Yet walk in and order, and what you get is basically the same: a hamburger smeared with mayonnaise, a sandwich cut into triangles, an egg pancake, a plate of black pepper stir-fry noodles, and an iced red tea or milk tea.

Even the details match: drinks come with film seals instead of lids, the hamburger is wrapped in thin plastic so you bite through the bag, indoor dining still gets you a pair of disposable utensils, and behind the counter they're shouting "handsome" and "beautiful." Nobody wrote a manual mandating any of this, yet walk into a shop in another city or county and it's still the same routine.

Industry research firms estimate that by 2025, the number of breakfast shops across Taiwan will exceed twenty thousand[^mirai]. Twenty thousand shops, one nearly identical menu, one nearly identical set of moves. This thing has no headquarters, no standard operating procedures, no single institution overseeing it.

Stranger still, none of it has an identifiable author. How scallion pancakes became powdered-egg pancakes is passed down by word of mouth with no written record. Who first put stir-fry noodles on a breakfast counter — even the Wikipedia entry carries a "needs additional citations" banner at the top. Who first printed those lame jokes and horoscopes on drink film seals — after checking with food media, the conclusion is "currently untraceable"[^fengmo].

Highly standardized, yet without an author. This is the most counterintuitive thing about Taiwan breakfast. Normally, a product specification replicated across twenty thousand stores would be backed by a powerful owner: a brand, a patent, a franchise agreement, a central factory. Taiwan breakfast is the opposite — it gets copied into every alley precisely because nothing along the way was defended.

## Breakfast Has Always Been Here — It’s “Buying Breakfast on the Way Out” That’s New

A widely circulated claim holds that in agrarian Taiwan, people ate only two meals a day: they rose with dawn to work the fields, and the “noon meal” was their first real meal, with breakfast only appearing after the Japanese introduced a regimented three-meal schedule. It sounds plausible, but the documentary record doesn’t support it.

A Japanese colonial-era folk custom record, _Taiwan Folk Customs_ (《臺灣風俗誌》), notes that “for breakfast people mostly ate congee; even those doing physically demanding labor rarely ate rice”[^fengsu]. The word “breakfast” is already present in this account — it just wasn’t substantial, regular, or proportional to the intensity of labor. The claim that “breakfast was brought by the Japanese” doesn’t hold up here. What the fifty years of Japanese rule truly introduced was regularity, along with Western options like bread and coffee, which entered schools, government offices, and a minority of households.

What has always existed is the breakfast meal itself. What’s new is the act of “buying breakfast on the way out,” and its starting point can be traced to a very small place.

Around 1955, in the area around Zhongzheng Bridge in Yonghe, Taipei County, three neighboring shops were all selling soy milk: “Yonghe Soy Milk,” “Donghai Soy Milk,” and “Sihai Soy Milk”[^setn-three]. The outcome didn’t match the signs. The shop flying the “Yonghe Soy Milk” sign eventually closed, and so did Sihai Soy Milk. The survivor was “Donghai Soy Milk,” which, after going through ownership disputes, changed its name and became the World Soy Milk King we know today[^setn-three]. The place name lived on; the earliest sign bearing it did not.

Who the founder was depends on whom you ask. The World Soy Milk King’s official website names only one person: “the first-generation boss, Mr. Li Yunzeng, from Shandong Province,” locating the shop at Yonghe Zhongzheng Bridge, where overseas-born veterans had gathered[^soymilk]. Wikipedia, the _Liberty Times_, and SETV credit both Li Yunzeng and Wang Jiun-jie as co-founders[^wiki-yonghe]. The popular romantic version — “bridge construction workers came every day” — doesn’t line up chronologically: the main structure of Zhongzheng Bridge was completed in 1937, eighteen years before that soy milk stall[^bridge].

What truly took the soy milk shop beyond the breakfast hour was a decision made twenty years later. The official timeline reads: “With economic development, the population enjoying nightlife in the Taipei area kept growing, and there were more people drinking soy milk for late-night snacks than for breakfast. Starting in 1975, first-generation boss Li Yunzeng decided to operate twenty-four hours a day, setting the first precedent in the entire province for a soy milk shop running around the clock.”[^soymilk] 1975 was the year Taiwan’s little-league teams kept winning in Williamsport, and the whole island stayed up through the night to watch the broadcasts[^llb]. From then on, soy milk shops kept their lights on beyond breakfast hours, becoming among the first food establishments in Taiwan to operate twenty-four hours a day.

The very items being sold here are themselves a record of migration. Scrambled pancakes and fried dough sticks are northern Chinese noodles that arrived with the waisheng immigrant wave after 1949, and the customers at the Zhongzheng Bridge stall were precisely these veterans who had settled in Yonghe[^soymilk]. For many people born in Taiwan in the 1950s through 1970s, this very combination was the original prototype of breakfast, along with the boss at the market who spoke in the local dialect.

![Scrambled pancakes, fried dough sticks, and a bowl of freshly ground soy milk](/article-images/food/taiwan-breakfast-shaobing-youtiao-soymilk-2007.webp)
_Scrambled pancakes, fried dough sticks, and soy milk — the prototype combination of post-war waisheng noodles entering Taiwan's early morning. Photo: Yun Huang Yong / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0_

By the 1980s, dual-income households were becoming increasingly common, and the meal that had once been prepared at home began to be outsourced to the neighborhood. A time slot became a business, relying on the fact that many people were all too busy during the same period. From day one, this business had no owner: three shops shared a single place name, and no one thought those four characters needed to be registered.

## When the Pastry Skin Starts Shipping from the Factory

The story of the egg pancake is a visible lowering of the entry-barrier curve.

The earliest version was scallion pancakes. After 1949, mainland Chinese immigrants brought northern Chinese wheat-based foods into Taiwan, where rice-based foods were dominant. Scallion pancakes were among the first to arrive. In an era of material scarcity, eggs were the best nutritional supplement, so adults would slip an egg into a scallion pancake — this was the first generation of egg pancakes. Later, vendors improved the texture by adding potato starch and sweet potato starch to the flour, creating a batter that could be poured directly onto the griddle without kneading[^setn-danbing].

The dough becoming batter was a crucial step. Kneading dough is a skill; pouring batter is not.

```tw-versus
Batter egg pancake | Pastry skin egg pancake
Flour mixed with water into batter, no kneading required | Dough made with boiling water, starch pre-gelatinized
Pan-fried to a soft, slightly moist texture | Pan-fried to a crispy, firm texture that doesn't easily deform
Shop pours batter spoon by spoon onto the griddle | Factory-made product ships ready-to-cook, just tear open and fry
Source: everylittled (Roomie) explanation of batter egg pancake vs. pastry skin egg pancake production differences
```

Northern Taiwan tends to use ready-made pastry skin, while southern Taiwan prefers the soft and tender batter version, with a slightly sweeter taste[^fenjiang][^nanbei].

The real step that brought the barrier crashing down supposedly happened in 1994, when factories began producing ready-made egg pancake skins. Breakfast shops no longer needed to prepare ingredients themselves — just tear open and fry[^setn-danbing].

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> "In 1994, factories began mass-producing egg pancake skins" — this is written in both the Chinese and English Wikipedia entries for Danbing. Tracing back the citations, both ultimately point to the same 2019 lifestyle news article, and before that, there's nothing. No named manufacturer, no guild records, no second independent witness. A "fact" collected by both Chinese and English encyclopedias has only one source. This itself is a microcosm of this meal: every key moment in its history left no formal record, because at the time, no one thought they were doing something worth recording.

The barrier collapsed even more thoroughly on the soy milk side. In 1985, Lin Bing-sheng established Hongqi Food, opened a shop in Taipei's Shilin District, and used machinery to mass-produce concentrated soy milk, selling to breakfast shops, bakeries, schools, and supermarkets[^wiki-yonghe]. Before that, selling soy milk required soaking beans, grinding the slurry, and mastering the heat. After that, one bucket of concentrate plus water equals a pot of soy milk.

The other items on the menu were never invented in Taiwan to begin with. Salted soy milk is a traditional breakfast item native to the Jiangnan and Shanghai regions, part of the "Four Treasures" of Shanghai breakfast culture alongside youtiao (fried dough sticks), large pancakes, and rice sausage — it only picked up local ingredients like dried radish and dried shrimp when it arrived in Taiwan[^wiki-xian]. There's a scientific explanation for the moment it condenses into curds: vinegar brings the main protein in soy milk close to its isoelectric point at around pH 5, reducing the net charge on colloidal particles and their mutual repulsion, causing them to aggregate[^pansci].

The rice sausage's framework comes from Jiangsu and Zhejiang's zongzi (glutinous rice dumplings), with the filling later developing into a combination of meat floss, dried radish, and pickled vegetables[^wiki-cifan]. Rice milk is made from Penglai rice mixed with peanuts and white sesame, with a recorded formula — but tracing its origins, it turns out to be "not uniquely Taiwanese" when the same "rice milk" product from China was found[^mijiang].

![Close-up of Taiwanese egg pancake cut into pieces and served in a plate](/article-images/food/taiwan-breakfast-danbing-closeup.webp)
_Egg pancake is the most native creation on this menu, and also the item with the most thoroughly collapsed barrier. Photo: Joy / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0_

So anyone could open a shop: no need to know how to grind soy milk, no need to know how to knead dough — just one griddle, one set of refrigerators, and a batch of frozen pastry skins, and the door opens. Since the method was never owned by anyone, the method went to every alley.

## Courts Decide: Anyone Can Hang This Sign

In 1981, Lin Kun-yen opened a shop on Taipei's Dadun Road called Mei Wei Mei. Inspired by TV footage of American baseball fans eating hot dogs, he figured hamburgers could be sold the same way in Taiwan. Before opening his storefront, he tried a few things: first he set up a stall selling hamburgers at Taipei Municipal Baseball Field (today's Taipei Dome), which didn't work out, then he pushed a breakfast food truck near Taipei Municipal University of Vocational Studies, and once business picked up, he opened a physical storefront[^baoshiguang].

The three characters "Mei Wei Mei" were cobbled together: a friend lived near a bakery called "Mei Tian Mei," and at the time many supermarkets and shoe stores liked using the name "Mei Er Lien." He combined the two[^baoshiguang]. McDonald's didn't enter Taiwan until 1984, so the timing was just coincidental — the two events had nothing to do with each other.

He never applied for a trademark. Starting in 1988, the "Mei Wei Mei" trademark entered a series of administrative lawsuits. Media reports said a franchisee had registered the trademark first, turning the founder into the infringer[^baoshiguang]. A different version from an intellectual property law firm gave another account: the earliest person to file the "Mei Wei Mei" trademark application was Juju Food, in 1986[^goldkeen]. The two stories don't match up on either the people involved or the years[^judgment-note].

The two versions did agree on one thing: the outcome. In the mid-1990s, a series of decisions by the administrative courts classified "Mei X Mei" as a weak trademark: the distinctiveness of these characters had long been diluted by countless similar signs on the street, so adding two characters in front didn't constitute infringement[^wiki-mem][^court-name].

![Second-generation Mei Wei Mei Datong Store sign and facade](/article-images/food/taiwan-breakfast-meiermei-datong-2012.webp)
_The "second generation Mei Wei Mei." Adding characters to the sign is exactly what the weak trademark ruling left behind. Photo: Mei Wei Mei Second Generation / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0_

One sign had split into an entire row. Mei You Mei, Juju Mei Wei Mei, Rui Lun Mei Wei Mei, Hong Ye Hamburger, and Morning Mia Cereal each grew into their own franchise system[^wiki-mem]. The similar signs you see when you step outside are downstream from this ruling.

The cost fell on real people. On the other side of the judgment, Lin Kun-yen lost control over his own shop name. He and several early partners had once protested at the "Extra-Party Notary Office"[^managertoday].

The other thread begins in Yonghe. In 1982, Lin Bing-sheng, then working in real estate brokerage, filed an application with the Ministry of Economic Affairs' Bureau of Standards Inspection and Quarantine, successfully registering "Yonghe" plus "Soy Milk" as a trademark[^wiki-yonghe][^ebc-lin]. Three years later he founded Juhui Food and turned concentrated soy milk into a nationwide business[^lin-year-note]. This line had no capital connection to the three shops at Zhongzheng Bridge in 1955 — just the same place name used by two different generations of people.

Later, such trademarks could no longer be registered. The full revision of the Trademark Act, promulgated by presidential decree on June 29, 2011, locked the combination of place name plus food name into Article 29, Paragraph 1, Item 1: any mark consisting solely of a description of the quality, purpose, ingredients, origin, or other characteristics of the designated goods lacks distinctiveness and cannot be registered[^law29]. This revision took effect on July 1, 2012[^lawhistory].

This business later crossed the Taiwan Strait as well. In 2019, Beijing Haidian Court handled a "Yonghe Soy Milk" infringement dispute, and the plaintiff was the very rights holder of these four characters in China. The court determined that "soy milk" was a generic term and "Yonghe" was the name of a county-level administrative region, so the trademark owner had no right to prohibit others from using them legitimately. However, the defendant's use of similar font designs on the storefront and tableware still constituted infringement, and the court ordered compensation of 33,000 yuan[^people-ip]. This judgment drew a line around legitimate use, but the trademark itself remained valid.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Placing these two cases side by side makes it easy to read as a neat coincidence: thirty years apart, across two jurisdictions, both signs ended up as words anyone could use. Legally, however, they follow entirely different theories. Taiwan's "weak trademark" was acquired — Mei & Mei originally had distinctiveness, but it was diluted by rows of identical signs on every street. The Haidian case in China was innate — a place name plus a generic term was never distinctive to begin with. The only thing they share is the outcome. Most trademarks remain effectively protected; these two are exceptions, not the rule.

The cost was not borne by Lin Kun-yen alone. Another lawsuit, unrelated to trademarks, concerned the shop's real estate: in 1955, Li Yun-zong and Wang Chun-jie opened shops at Zhongzheng Bridge and later, their descendants sued over property rights. In 1997, the parties agreed to split ownership equally, with the Li family paying NT$3.5 million monthly rent to the Wang family. The Pingtung District Court ruled in 2012 that the Li family owed NT$3.3 million in rent[^ettoday].

The case was ultimately upheld by the Supreme Court, with Li Qing-yu and others ordered to pay Wang Ning-ruo NT$17.8 million in principal, plus over NT$5.78 million in interest for six and a half years, totaling over NT$23.58 million[^ltn]. The four characters "Yonghe Soy Milk" eventually became usable by anyone, while the two families who first set up shop at the bridge spent over a decade in court over a building.

The sign couldn't be held, so every street grew its own version.

## The Fire Burned All Night, and the Shop Still Wasn't Open

Next to the Sanshui Temple on Xinsheng Road in Tainan, there's a nameless fish congee stall. The boss, Li Zhenming, is 85 years old. He gets up at four thirty in the morning, lights the stove, first steeps fish bones for the broth, then cooks fish stomach, blanches fish skin, blanches clams, and works by the stove until about six ten before opening shop. The boss's wife, Li Cai Meiyun, says the broth is simmered fresh every day—over ten catties of fish bones need half an hour of simmering[^panorama].

The Zhenjia Cai cake at Sanshao Palace starts even earlier—or rather, never sleeps at all. The cake is soaked and boiled the night before, going into the pot at ten o'clock, and doesn't come out until four in the morning the next day. At five thirty in the morning, the second-generation Zhen Shihan, the boss's wife Wu Peiqiao, and the third-generation Zhen Peijing open shop as a family of three, unwrapping the cake leaves and drizzling on their special sauce[^panorama].

A breakfast worth dozens of dollars carries this unseen stretch of time hung out in front of it. The fire burned all night, and the shop still wasn't open.

![Tainan's milkfish congee](/article-images/food/taiwan-breakfast-tainan-milkfish-congee.webp)
_Tainan's milkfish congee. The fish bone broth needs half an hour of simmering before opening shop. Photo: Sin-siōng / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

The Weimei Breakfast Shop on Zhuangjing Road in Taipei is a different story. The boss's wife, Cai Xue-wen, took over her brother's shop, moved into the basement floor below the shop, and has been running it for over thirty years, building a reputation in Taipei with a single pan-fried golden-crusted egg pancake. When asked about this job, her answer is very direct.

```tw-quote
As long as the kids are taken care of, the business can be managed—surely that's easier than being an office worker
Cai Xue-wen | Boss of Weimei Breakfast Shop, Zhuangjing Road, Taipei, interviewed by CommonWealth Magazine, 2026
```

Two things are true in this sentence at the same time: she really did make both ends meet, and the prerequisite for her to do so is that her home and shop share the same address. Doing business upstairs and living downstairs means zero commute time, and the time for looking after children is squeezed out from the working hours[^cw-tsai].

The institutional side tells a completely different story. The Ministry of Labor's statistics only go as granular as "accommodation and food service industry"[^mol-vacancy]. There's no "breakfast shop" category in that same industry classification.

The severity of the labor shortage is measurable, though. The Ministry of Labor's vacancy survey shows that by the end of December 2025, the accommodation and food service industry had a vacancy rate of 4.3%, the highest among all major industries, with 25,000 open positions[^mol-vacancy]. 104 Job Bank's _2026 Service Industry Talent White Paper_ estimates an even larger gap: the accommodation and food service sector is short 273,000 workers per month, with 215,000 of those in food service roles[^104].

But the legal channels for filling these positions are narrow. The 2026 service industry migrant worker policy relaxation opens up four job categories in the hospitality sector: room service, cleaning, front desk, and food service[^bw-migrant]. Breakfast shops and street food vendors are not on the list. The general food service industry can currently only rely on the "migrant worker retention and extension program," which allows experienced migrant workers who have already been in Taiwan for a certain number of years to transition into mid-level technical roles as chefs[^eats365]. The path for hiring new entry-level foreign workers remains closed[^law-worker]. So the common explanation that "young people can't endure hardship" overlooks several institutional constraints that can be pointed to.

The list is compiled by the government; the numbers for the gap are calculated by the job bank. But how do the people kept outside this door feel? No one has ever asked them. Equally unasked are the wage-earning counter staff standing behind the griddle. The boss's wife's words have been recorded by the media, but the employees' working hours, wages, and protections have never been calculated in any dedicated statistics.

It's precisely these people, not caught by the system, who keep alive the breakfast that everyone can afford. The shop opens at 6:10 a.m., the rush hits at 7:30, and the line outside is made up of uniformed students and office workers rushing to catch the MRT. There's no slot for them in the classification tables, yet they've set aside a meal for everyone.

## Six to Eight Thirty — Speed Is a Skill

Tsai Hsueh-wen conducted fieldwork at a breakfast shop. He measured the peak rush falling between six and eight-thirty, three kinds of customers intersecting at the same griddle: those who grab-and-go finish within a minute, those who walk in for takeout within ten minutes, and those who sit in for about thirty minutes. When asked, the shopkeeper only said: "Before eight, it's all about speed."[^tnl]

The common saying goes that breakfast shop aunties remember your taste because of human warmth. But this explanation reverses cause and effect. Within those two and a half hours, memory itself is productivity. She remembers you don't want cucumber so she doesn't have to ask again, so she doesn't ask — that phrase "same as usual" saves ten seconds, the line moves forward. This is technique honed under speed pressure.

Forms of address are part of the same technique. "Handsome" and "beautiful" are the fastest universal calls — no need to remember names, no risk of calling the wrong person, and they carry across the oil smoke.

![Street-side breakfast shop with roll-up gate open and sign lit](/article-images/food/taiwan-breakfast-shop-street-2021.webp)
_Street-side breakfast shop. From six to eight-thirty, this doorway digests an entire street's morning. Photo: WC-QHS / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

In May 2026, a notice went viral on social media: "Dear customers over 40, please do not address the female staff working on site as 'auntie,'" suggesting instead "older sister," "younger sister," or "beautiful." The report didn't name the shop or location, but noted one thing: this note was printed by a customer for the shop, not posted by the shop itself.[^ayi]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> An entire system of address is being renegotiated, with the proposal coming from outside the counter. This is something quite special about the breakfast shop space. Its rules were never set by anyone — they're called out every day from six to eight-thirty by people standing on either side of the same griddle. The sign can be claimed by everyone; this whole way of calling out never belonged to anyone.

Heading further south, the content of this time slot completely changes. A Tainan morning is beef soup, fish roe congee, braised pork rice, and bean sprout noodles — no blood relation to the egg pancake and soy milk line. Most beef soup shops close on Mondays and Tuesdays because they don't slaughter cattle from Monday night to Tuesday morning. The shop opens around five in the morning, so it's also breakfast.[^tainan] A single shop's rest day is decided by the slaughterhouse calendar — an entirely independent logic, with Taipei's griddles running their own rhythms in the same time slot.

Beyond the griddle is another layer: the auntie remembers whose child is sick, whose family argued yesterday, which alley got new neighbors. That layer lives in [Breakfast Shop Auntie and the Community Intelligence Network](/en/society/breakfast-shops-and-community-intelligence-network). In the two meters before the griddle, there's only this: a "same as usual," a combo without mayonnaise, a cup of iced milk tea already sealed under film.

Recipes can't be held onto, signs can't be held onto, sleep can't be held onto. What can be held onto is "I remember you don't want cucumber" — and that happens to require no ownership at all.

## No One Remembers Who Invented Scallion Pancakes

The most ordinary dish in this meal, no one remembers where it came from. Food media Roomie's author might as well say it outright: "After steak left the menu, scallion pancakes and half-cooked eggs appeared in Taiwan's Western-style breakfast shops without a traceable origin."[^tieban]

Some might point to Sun Tung-po's first stall in 1976 as the answer, but that year was tied to the entire set of Taiwanese cheap steak and iron plate side dishes, not the noodle dish itself.[^sundongbao] Many items on the menu are like this—completely blank on their origins.

![Salted soy milk and egg pancake on the same table](/article-images/food/taiwan-breakfast-xian-doujiang-danbing.webp)
_Salted soy milk and egg pancake, both transplanted, both locally adapted. Photo: KQuhen / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0_

Those who can be named have one by one departed. On August 7, 2026, Lin Bing-sheng passed away in Taipei at the age of seventy.[^ebc-lin] He had nothing to do with the people selling soy milk at Zhongzheng Bridge in 1955; he was doing something else: registering a place name as a trademark, putting concentrated soy milk into machines, letting the four characters "Yonghe Soy Milk" travel from a Shilin storefront into breakfast shops, bakeries, schools, and supermarkets across Taiwan.

The shop at Zhongzheng Bridge is still open, still operating 24 hours. When interviewed, second-generation chairman Lee Ching-yu spoke about heat control: "Many people think the masters in the shop are lazy and don't stir the soy milk while it's cooking, which is why it develops a burnt flavor. But in fact, to drink soy milk this rich and with a slight burnt taste, it actually requires strict control of heat and temperature."[^verse]

The recipes weren't preserved, the names weren't preserved, and the time before the griddle was institutionalized. This meal's anonymity isn't because no one wanted to claim it. Lin Kun-yen, Lin Bing-sheng, Li Yun-zeng, and Wang Chun-jie have all put their names on the line—fighting lawsuits, paying damages, losing control—they too couldn't hold on.

What remains is someone still guarding the temperature of that pot of soy milk, that unspoken "same as usual" at 7:10. A breakfast with no author is rewritten every morning at dawn on twenty thousand griddles.
