> **30-Second Overview:** In the 1980s, heated braised dishes began to form a new way of eating in Taiwan. In the 1990s, brands like Beggar's Group (Gai Bang) pushed the single-pot broth model into chain operations. Braised food may look like simply tossing tofu, kelp, and meat slices into a dark broth, but it actually involves Chaoshan braising techniques, the chemical reactions of soy sauce and sugar, two distinct consumption habits (cold and hot), and the food logistics that moved from street carts to e-commerce. Its most "Taiwanese" feature is not a specific spice, but the act of letting a person stand before the stall and decide how to compose their dinner. [^1] [^3] [^4]

In the 1980s, heated braised dishes began to rise in Taiwan. In the 1990s, a bubble tea shop in Changhua shifted from cold braised dishes to heated ones, eventually developing into Beggar's Group (Gai Bang Luwei). In 2013, Chen Li-ting used braised food as the subject of her master's thesis research on characteristic snacks and sensory experiences. [^2] [^4]

Placing these three time points together reveals that braised food is no longer just a convenient option in night markets. On one hand, it possesses long-standing seasoning and preservation techniques; on the other, it is constantly reinvented by new store formats, distribution channels, and eating habits.

The premise that allows customers to freely select ingredients for Taiwanese braised food is that tofu products, meats, kelp, and eggs each possess different textures and require different braising times. _FoodNEXT_’s analysis of cold braised dishes notes that processing ingredients separately helps prevent flavors from mixing. [^1] [^7]

📝 **Curator’s Note:** What is most worth writing about braised food is not "what ingredients are there," but how it hands the power of choice to the person standing at the stall. You can take just one braised egg, or you can break your dinner down into tofu products, vegetables, meats, and noodles. The pot of broth thus becomes a small menu of life choices.

## Don't Rush to Find a Single Origin

The foundation of braised food is the braising liquid (lu shui). _FoodNEXT_’s analysis points out that braising liquid is related to the seasoning style of Guangdong’s Chaoshan region. For similar pre-seasoned sauce dishes, some scholars trace the history back to practices in Sichuan two thousand years ago, using well salt and Sichuan peppercorns. These descriptions refer to the flow of techniques and do not mean Taiwanese braised food can be simplified as the result of a single-line transmission from one place. [^1]

What Taiwan adopted was a logic that makes ingredients more durable and flavorful. Meat, tofu, bean curd sheets, kelp, braised eggs, chicken wings, and chicken feet can all be placed under the same cultural label, but different ingredients require different processing times and broth concentrations. _FoodNEXT_’s article also reminds us that meticulous cold braised dishes are braised separately by ingredient to avoid flavor mixing. [^1]

This technique aligns well with the rhythm of daily Taiwanese dining. It can be prepared in advance, cooled, and eaten; or reheated at the stall. It can be a side dish for drinking alcohol or a temporary meal after work. Braised food is neither simply a hot dish nor a cold platter; it preserves both needs.

Braised food is not a synonym for a single meat dish, nor is it a fixed ingredient list. It can unfold along different paths: braising meats, infusing tofu products, and harmonizing sauces, finally being combined by customers at the stall. This difference is precisely why Taiwanese braised food culture cannot be summarized by a single fixed menu. [^1] [^6]

## A Pot of Broth Is Actually a Ratio System

The color of braised food is not naturally grown. _FoodNEXT_’s explanation of soy sauce points out that the amino acids in soy sauce and the sugar in the broth undergo a Maillard reaction when heated, bringing aroma and browning. The sugar itself may undergo caramelization at high temperatures, forming a deeper color and viscosity. After leaving the stall, the food must still face the tasks of packaging, cooling, and delivery. [^3] [^5] [^6]

This is why the dark color of braised food cannot be directly equated with "heavy flavor." Color, aroma, saltiness, and the speed of ingredient absorption may come from different reactions and ratios. Too much sugar makes it bitter; too much soy sauce overpowers the ingredients. The true difficulty is not in boiling the pot black, but in ensuring every ingredient retains its own texture.

The common practice compiled by _FoodNEXT_ suggests maintaining a ratio of approximately 1:3 to 1:5 between soy sauce and water, but this is an operational range in cooking articles, not a standard answer that all shops or households must follow. Ingredients, pot size, heating time, and desired taste will all change the result. [^5]

Food critic Wang Rui-yao, when discussing family recipes for braised dishes, breaks down the "five fragrances" of braised food into the fragrance of raw materials, the fragrance of spices, the fragrance of dried materials, soy sauce fragrance, and wine fragrance. The value of this statement lies not in adding a fixed spice list to braised food, but in pulling "fragrance" back from being a single Chinese medicinal herb to a layer formed jointly by ingredients and heat control. [^7]

📝 **Curator’s Note:** A good pot of broth is not more powerful because it is more complex. It is more like a Taiwanese accent: you can hear the common base, but every family, every stall, and every city leaves its own habit in the final sound of a character.

## Cold and Hot Braised Dishes: Two Life Speeds

Cold braised dishes involve braising ingredients first, cooling them, and then eating. It is suitable for slowly gnawing on chicken feet or duck wings, and for taking items one by one while watching movies, drinking, or chatting. Hot braised dishes compress ingredient selection, heating, and eating into a single on-site process: whatever the customer picks, the shop cooks, and it can be taken away in a few minutes.

_FoodNEXT_’s interview records that after the rise of heated braised dishes in the 1980s, a new commercial combination formed between bubble tea shops and braised food. Wang Neng-hong, founder of Beggar's Group, recalled that in early bubble tea shops, braised food platters were important profit items. This indicates that hot braised dishes are not a natural continuation of ancient techniques, but a product that grew alongside store formats, consumption times, and demand for dining out. [^4]

Neither type of braised dish is more orthodox. Cold braised dishes make people notice preservation, cutting skills, and the settling of aromas; hot braised dishes make people notice speed, portion size, and the freedom of selection. Some treat it as a snack, some as a dinner, and some only think of it when traveling or watching movies.

This divergence itself is culture. When a food does not require everyone to eat it in the same way, it is hard to be represented by a single standard menu. The commonality of Taiwanese braised food is instead built on the fact that everyone can rewrite the content of that bowl.

## From Street Carts to Brands: The Broth Needs Explanation

Street-side braised food initially sold taste, recipes, and craftsmanship. As the number of shops increased, brands had to answer "why you." _FoodNEXT_’s analysis of the braised food market points out that the shift from street vendors to brands made stories, culture, concepts, and consumer experiences part of the differentiation. [^8]

Beggar's Group (Gai Bang Luwei) is an early example of this transition. _FoodNEXT_ records that in the 1990s, it established recognition through the naming "Beggar's Group" and experience design, even using martial arts characters to replace traditional numbering systems. These practices do not change the chemical reactions of the broth, but they change how customers remember a shop. [^8]

Later brands sought different target audiences. Some focus on online shopping and delivery; some go the vegetarian, healthy, or lifestyle route; others attract young customers with spicy flavors, stir-fried instant noodles, and in-store experiences. Braised food thus expanded from "what is in the pot" to "who this shop wants to eat and how." [^8]

This path is not all success stories. Brandization requires packaging, logistics, storefronts, and marketing costs. When experiences are easily replicated, brands must continue to prove their differentiation. The taste remembered by regular customers must, once entered into a chain, become a process that can be trained, preserved, and delivered.

## When Braised Food Leaves the Alley

In 2018, _FoodNEXT_ compiled food, cooking, and transportation issues with braised food as the theme. The article noted that after cold platter braised dishes entered the online and ready-to-eat market, the cold chain logistics' requirement for "open and eat, no reheating" became important. This turning point forces braised food to face an unromantic but unavoidable question: flavors can be passed down through memory, but microbial risks cannot be managed by memory. [^3]

Therefore, "old broth" should not be written as a myth of natural safety. The aroma of the broth, repeated heating, and ingredient infusion are culinary issues; how to cool, preserve, transport, and sell after cooking is a food hygiene issue. They may occur simultaneously in the same shop, but cannot be handled with the same reasoning.

The same article described the braised food market as an industry with a value of billions, but this figure comes from the industry data and practitioner statements compiled in the article, and cannot be directly taken as government statistics or a permanent, unchanging total output. More noteworthy here is that braised food is no longer just circulating in street carts and storefronts; it has entered convenience stores, hypermarkets, e-commerce, and delivery services. [^3]

📝 **Curator’s Note:** The modernization of braised food is not just swapping street carts for pretty signs. Its true test is whether it can turn a taste maintained by feel into a workflow that does not sacrifice food safety and does not erase local differences.

## Why People Still Remember That Pot

In 2013, a master's thesis used braised food as a characteristic snack to study sensory experiences in diet. The keywords listed on the thesis page include braised food, characteristic snacks, and sensory experience. This research entry is interesting: braised food is worth remembering not just because it has history, but because it simultaneously mobilizes color, aroma, texture, temperature, and the act of selection. [^2]

The _Taiwan Studies Newsletter_ (Taiwan Xue Tongxun) Issue 136, published by the National Taiwan Library in 2024, features a special issue on food culture. It places "Taiwanese taste" within the long-term changes of natural environment, ethnic migration, foreign trade, colonial rule, and the introduction of skills and knowledge. Looking back at braised food through this broader framework, it is not an isolated Taiwanese invention, but a daily form left after many foreign techniques, local ingredients, industrial conditions, and popular choices intersected in Taiwan. [^9]

Placing these materials back into Taiwan's dietary context, we can see that braised food is both a cooking technique and a daily work that can be learned, adjusted, and passed on. This article still uses Taiwan's academic bibliographies, government cultural publications, and Taiwanese food media as primary evidence.

Thus, the core of Taiwanese braised food is not a pot of old broth that never changes. It is more like a continuously adjustable container: some use it to preserve ingredients, some to save time, some to start a business, some to recall the alley after school, and some to stand before the stall to reassemble a meal when unsure what to eat.

## Braised Food Is Not One Single Flavor

When Taiwanese people say "eat braised food," they may actually be pointing to several different scenes. Cold braised dishes in markets are usually already cut; customers choose portion and combination. Heated braised dishes put fresh or semi-finished products into the pot to re-cook; customers wait for temperature and satiety. Brand delivery seals the braised food in packaging; when the customer receives it, the shop has already completed the selection, seasoning, and preservation for them.

These three forms share one name, but understand "freshness" differently. Cold braised dishes focus on the overall taste after the aroma settles; hot braised dishes focus on the temperature and broth upon entry; delivery must ensure the product remains safe and edible after leaving the kitchen. _FoodNEXT_’s reminder about cold platter braised dishes and the cold chain precisely explains that behind the same name lies different timelines. [^3]

Ingredients also change the character of a pot of broth. Tofu and kelp absorb broth differently; meats need time for fibers to soften; chicken feet and pork skin bring gelatinous texture into the broth. Wang Rui-yao, when discussing family recipes, mentioned that her home braised tofu and kelp separately, reasoning that they might make the broth sticky and sour. This is kitchen experience correcting the idea of "cooking everything together." [^7]

Therefore, the tongs in front of a braised food stall are not just a tool for picking up food. They translate ingredient differences into choices: Do you want a chicken foot that needs slow gnawing? Do you want a portion of broth-absorbing tofu? Do you use noodles to connect scattered ingredients into a complete meal? Culinary techniques here do not stay in the kitchen; they are handed to the customer to complete the final segment.

## The Labor of a Pot of Broth Has Not Disappeared

"Old broth" is easily imagined as a mysterious object, as if preserving it for years makes it naturally better than new broth. But from a culinary process perspective, the broth needs to be heated, filtered, replenished with water, and seasoned, and the sequence must be arranged according to different ingredients. What truly accumulates is not the years themselves, but the shopkeeper's judgment of temperature, saltiness, concentration, and ingredient state.

_FoodNEXT_’s compilation of braised food discusses cooking and transportation together, reminding us that the culinary techniques of the broth cannot replace the preservation standards of commercial food. The equipment, cooling, packaging, and delivery processes of Taiwanese shops on-site must still be confirmed individually; family cooking methods cannot be directly applied to commercial food production. [^3]

This distinction also makes us re-examine street vendors. Regular customers of a braised food stall only see a pair of tongs and a pot; the shopkeeper actually handles purchasing, cutting, pot separation, heating, cleaning, packaging, and closing. After brandization, these tasks are split into central kitchens, storefronts, warehouses, and delivery. The more convenient the braised food becomes, the less visible the labor behind it often is. [^8]

_FoodNEXT_’s compilation of the braised food industry places taste, cooking, transportation, and food safety in the same topic. This arrangement is important. Braised food is not just a score of "whether it tastes good." When it moves from the on-site table to the ready-to-eat market, where ingredients come from, how they are cooled, and how they are transported, all become part of whether the taste can be trusted. [^3]

## So-Called "Taiwanese Taste" Often Appears Only After Mixing

The food culture special issue of the National Taiwan Library places "Taiwanese taste" within the history of natural environment, ethnic migration, foreign trade, colonial rule, and the introduction of skills. This framework does not assign a single ancestor to braised food, but it avoids writing "Taiwanese taste" as something closed, pure, and unchanging. [^9]

The regional context of braising liquid, the brewing and blending of soy sauce, the imagination of "five fragrances," the ingredient selection methods of Taiwanese street vendors, and the packaging and logistics of modern chain industries are not mutually exclusive sources. They entered the same daily life at different times, finally making people feel "this is Taiwanese braised food."

This mixing also explains why braised food easily crosses class and age boundaries. It can appear in markets, near schools, business districts, night markets, bubble tea shops, and convenient delivery channels. Price, portion, and ingredients can be rearranged, allowing the same cooking technique to enter different people's lives. _FoodNEXT_’s analysis of brand cases also shows that shops redesign products, packaging, and consumer experiences according to target audiences. [^8]

But "crossing" does not mean "no difference." One person chooses chicken feet because they like gnawing; another avoids chicken feet because they want to finish quickly. Some pursue soy sauce aroma; some only want vegetables and tofu products. The public nature of braised food lies in accommodating different dinners within a common broth base.

## One Person Standing Before the Stall

Braised food stacks several aspects of Taiwanese snacks together: techniques left by Chaoshan and Chinese seasoning traditions, local adjustments by Taiwanese families and street vendors, heated consumption after the 1980s, brandization after the 1990s, and the still-changing cold chain and ready-to-eat markets today. Every layer is true, but no single layer can represent the whole.

This also explains why it is closer to daily Taiwanese life than an ingredient list. You can see it in the context of [Taiwanese Street Food](/en/food/taiwan-regional-street-food-map/), or understand it within the stall economy of [Night Market Culture](/en/food/night-market-culture/), but the moment that truly happens is usually just you picking up the tongs under the light and telling the boss whether to add chili, whether to add noodles, or whether to cut this in half.

Braised food looks the most ritual-less, yet it preserves the hardest-to-keep parts of food culture: the act of choice, the preferences of regular customers, the shopkeeper's feel, and a person's willingness to make temporary decisions for tonight's stomach. It has no single orthodox answer; it can stay alive precisely because every pot allows the next person to add a bit of their own flavor.

If you were to introduce braised food to someone coming to Taiwan for the first time, the easiest mistake is to only list a string of ingredient names for them. Tofu and kelp are just the entry; what truly needs explaining is how these ingredients are processed separately, how they move between cold and hot, and why customers can use tongs to design their own dinner. [^1] [^8]

Similarly, you cannot explain braised food only with "old-fashioned taste" (gaojiu wei). The heated store formats of the 1980s, the brand attempts of the 1990s, and later delivery and ready-to-eat logistics are all rewriting its appearance and rhythm. The tradition of braised food does not stop in the past; it is the layer left by every change in distribution channels. [^3] [^4] [^5]

It also does not need to be packaged as a perfect national snack. Braised food contains salt, sugar, and processed ingredients; cold platters involve cold-chain preservation. Therefore, appreciating its cultural position and paying attention to ingredient selection, preservation, and consumption frequency can coexist. Food safety is not an appendix that destroys the food story; it is part of the story. [^3]

Finally, braised food reminds us: Taiwanese taste does not start from an official recipe sheet. It often forms from the repeated adjustments of street vendors, families, immigrants, channels, and customers. Only when everyone becomes familiar with it does it look like a taste that has existed since ancient times. [^9]

So, next time you stand before a braised food stall, what is worth remembering may not be which one is the most orthodox, but that you are participating in a culture that has not yet clocked out. That brief process of picking, waiting, paying, and opening the paper bag is turning historical techniques into today's meal, and handing a pot of broth to the next person to continue deciding. This daily variability is precisely why it can move between markets, campuses, business districts, night markets, and online channels, yet still be recognized as the same Taiwanese life experience. It is precisely for this reason that braised food is not only worth eating, but also worth using as a window to understand how Taiwan adjusts, circulates, and remembers food.
