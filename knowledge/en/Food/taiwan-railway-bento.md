> **30-Second Overview:** The TRA bento was not first made as "old-fashioned" fare before being put on the rails; it was first a solution for long-distance travel, and later became a Taiwanese flavor through street vending at platforms, sales in carriages, preservation technology, and the memories of generations of travelers. In 1914, vendors were already selling railway bentos at stations; the high-speed trains without dining cars in 1956 gave birth to the prototype of rib rice; and the "fry then braise" method in 1961 fixed many recognizable flavors.[^1]

## I. The Bento Appeared on the Platform Before the Train Arrived

In 1914, a train journey from Taipei to Kaohsiung took about 10.5 hours. At that time, the train was not just a narrow space transporting people from north to south; it was a space where meals had to be solved en route. Lecture materials compiled by the National Taiwan Museum of History indicate that stores on trains were selling meals as early as 1906. Station sales in 1909 included cigarettes, alcohol, mandarins, and boiled eggs, and records of station bentos appeared by 1914.[^1]

Academic research places this origin in the demand for long-distance dining after the completion of the trunk railway line: platform vendors had to deliver a takeaway meal within the short stop time of the train. This beginning was not romantic, but precise—the journey was long, there were no reliable meal options on the train, and food had to be delivered quickly and last through an air-conditioned-less ride.[^12]

Therefore, the true protagonist of the railway bento is not the rib, but **time**. It transformed "how much longer do I have to travel" into "can this box last until the next station," shrinking the scale of the railway infrastructure into the steam smelled when someone opened a box.

📝 **Curator's Note:** If one only writes about the TRA bento as a list of ribs, braised eggs, and rice, its most important cultural significance is missed. It is food tailored to the transportation system; the flavor is merely an edible trace of that system.

## II. Brief Transactions on Platforms Became Part of TRA's Work

The railway bento introduced to Taiwan during the Japanese colonial period carried traces of Japanese _ekiben_ (station lunchbox) culture, but upon landing in Taiwan, it quickly mingled with local rail distances, speed, and commercial habits. Early bentos were not uniformly produced by a central kitchen but contracted by private merchants, completed through transactions on platforms, by train windows, and at market edges. This pattern continued after the war until TRA established its catering service in 1951, when the bento was officially incorporated into its services.[^1] [^2]

Exhibition materials from the National Railway Museum remind us that railway dining was never just a rice box: it encompassed dining cars, tea service, platform vending, station restaurants, and accommodation, all within one mobile service system. The bento was the easiest part to take away, making it the easiest element to condense the entire system into one object.[^3]

Stories compiled by StoryStudio point out that from 1949 onward, TRA restaurants in stations such as Kaohsiung, Tainan, Taichung, Taipei, and Songshan produced bentos for sale on trains. The establishment of the "Small Operations" department in 1960 further integrated bento sales.[^4]

📝 **Curator's Note:** Saying it was merely "introduced by Japan and improved by Taiwan" is still too simple. A more precise statement is: while the system, name, and some forms have Japanese origins, what truly made it part of Taiwanese daily life was its re-creation through post-war railway services, passenger consumption power, and local tastes working together.

### Small Operations Department: Turning Individual Bentos into a Supply Line

The "Small Operations" department established in 1960 was important not because the name sounded like a small restaurant, but because it shifted bentos from scattered sales to a manageable supply line. Restaurants first prepared ingredients, produced, and boxed them before sending the bentos to be sold at trains and station points. What passengers saw was a box; behind it was the synchronized result of kitchens, transportation, train operations, and station staff.[^4]

This also explains why railway bentos cannot be understood solely through "local specialties." While their flavor is influenced by local tastes, they must first adhere to the train schedule: being one minute late could mean missing a train. If given too much time, the ribs and rice would face preservation issues. The standardization of the bento was not about erasing regional differences, but ensuring that every box arrived on time.

## III. The High-Speed Train Without Dining Cars Gave Birth to Ribs

In 1956, TRA introduced high-speed trains to shorten long-distance travel time between the north and south. However, it could not accommodate dining cars. Research by Tseng Ling-yi, recorded in the Huayi Database, points out that this limitation prompted Tao De-lin, a manager at the Taichung Railway Restaurant, to launch Jiangzhe-style rib rice, which became the initial prototype of today's TRA rib bento.[^12]

It was not a signature dish suddenly invented by a famous chef, but an answer left behind after speed, carriages, and catering services compromised. It could not rely on on-site cooking, nor could it demand passengers wait; it could only compress a complete meal into one box.

Most train types at the time lacked air conditioning, so bentos tended to become "sour" if stored too long—a research abstract explains this in Tâi-gí as food oxidizing and decaying to emit sour smells. In 1961, Lin Huo-chai of the Taichung Railway Restaurant changed the original fried ribs to a "fry then braise" method and increased the salt content so that the food could better withstand the journey. This treatment simultaneously changed the texture: the exterior was not just sought for crispiness but also needed to absorb the braising sauce, allowing it to still taste good even when eaten off the line.[^12]

This is the counter-intuitive aspect of Taiwanese railway bentos: part of their most nostalgic flavor originates from necessities they had to face. No air conditioning, no dining cars, no instant reheating—these were not just background conditions, but co-authors of the dish itself.

## IV. Rice and Ribs Are Not Just Plain Rice or a Single Main Dish

The focus of early TRA rib rice was not simply placing a piece of meat on plain rice. Both research abstracts and historical records indicate that rib rice gradually took shape at the intersection of Jiangzhe flavors, preservation needs, and TRA catering services. It was not a single recipe suddenly descending, but a meal repeatedly modified by travel conditions.[^1] [^12]

English reports also point out that Taiwanese bentos often place protein, vegetables, pickles, and rice in the same box, which differs from the separated cold bento common in Japan. The meat in TRA rib bento is first marinated, coated with sweet potato starch, deep-fried, and then stewed for about 20 minutes, ensuring it remains moist even after cooling.[^11]

This method makes the bento more than just "ribs with rice." The rice absorbs the sauce; the braised eggs and pickles provide saltiness and contrast. Different foods within the box fulfill tasks of preservation and texture for each other. It is a miniature system that allows passengers not to search for soup, vegetables, and main dishes on the train.

## V. Four Transformations of the Box

An aluminum round TRA bento box collected by the National Taiwan Museum of History, catalog number 2010.019.1782, measures approximately 14.5 × 14.5 × 4.7 cm. Both the lid and body are stamped with the TRA emblem, and the lid is engraved with "Railway Property" and "Do Not Use Privately," reminding passengers to return it after eating.[^5]

These words, like cold traces left by administration, also preserve a specific scene: someone finishing their meal on the train, holding an empty box, having to decide whether to hand it back or take it as a travel souvenir. The lunchbox is not just the shell of the bento; it represents the tension between "finishing" and the system/the person.

Collection records document several changes in packaging: from 1949 to 1969, bentos were mainly sold in disposable wooden boxes. In 1970, aluminum meal boxes were fully adopted, but due to issues with passengers taking them away, difficulty cleaning, and material concerns, polystyrene was used in 1985. In 1997, paper and wood materials were re-adopted in line with the ban on plastic food containers.[^5]

_Figure: TRA Bento Shop No. 5, located on B1 of Taipei Station, modeled after an EMU1200 type electric multiple unit. Photography by Solomon203, CC BY-SA 4.0; [Wikimedia Commons image page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwan_Railway_Bento_No.5_20201017.jpg)._

The round stainless steel box was later re-released not just because it was durable. The round box, metal reflection, carrying bag, and chopsticks together form proof of "I once rode that train." Packaging transformed from a container into a collectible; food transformed from a meal into a piece of time one could take away.

📝 **Curator's Note:** The "old-fashioned flavor" of the TRA bento is not an unchanging recipe sheet. It includes replacements—wooden boxes, aluminum boxes, stainless steel boxes, polystyrene boxes—and the imagination of passengers regarding "that old box." Flavor and container have continuously manufactured memory together.

## VI. In 2000, A Defect Was Re-queued

In 2000, TRA launched the "Taiwan Railway Nostalgia Bento," putting rib rice back into a round stainless steel box in limited quantities of one thousand. It was officially sold the next morning and sold out within twenty minutes, with subsequent pre-orders bringing the total sales to about ninety thousand.[^14] Academic research views this "nostalgic innovation" as an important turning point where TRA transformed its cultural memory into brand resources.[^12]

The key to this success was not just the flavor. For many who grew up in times of material scarcity, the railway bento was food they could "smell but not afford." For others, it was a fixed scene associated with New Year's travel, returning home, studying, or their first long journey. When the round box reappeared, the act of purchasing felt like making up for a childhood that was never eaten.

Academic research places this turning point within the context of TRA bento reform: the 2000 nostalgia bento led TRA to begin treating its cultural memory as brand resources. In 2005, external expert Li Yu-chia managed the Taipei restaurant and incorporated Hakka flavors, forming a new direction for dishes.[^12]

The TRA bento was later featured in Taiwan-Japan Railway Culture exhibitions. The preliminary exhibition in 2024 had retired manager Li Yu-chia and conductor Huang Ming-chu sharing their production experiences, presenting the bento at the intersection of gustatory memory and railway stories to Japanese audiences.[^8] What it brought overseas was not an abstract Taiwan, but an experience that happened with hot rice, braising sauce, and movement.

📝 **Curator's Note:** The most noteworthy point is not "everyone loves old-fashioned flavors," but how a public service institution translates its operational memory into merchandise. TRA does not just sell a meal; it sells a shared experience of having "traveled together."

## VII. Ribs Are Not Just from Taipei

English reports state that official TRA bentos were made in places such as Taipei, Taichung, Kaohsiung, Hualien, and Qidu; the rib preparation at each station was similar, yet regular customers would still argue about which station's was best. In 2012, TRA even held a station competition, with Qidu Station winning partly because of the scallion marinade in the sauce.[^11]

The same report records that TRA sold over ten million bentos annually; in 2015, Taiwan also began hosting the Formosa Rail Bento Festival, allowing local flavors to be seen more clearly. These numbers and events elevated the bento from a railway ancillary service to a food culture representing localities and attracting dedicated tourists.[^11]

Chishang bentos put rice and paddy into wooden boxes; Fulong's bentos were linked to coastal travel and cycling routes; while Fenqihu placed its bentos in the mountain town setting of the Alishan Forest Railway. These local bentos borrowed the grammar of railway food but did not have to replicate the same piece of rib.[^11]

Thus, the railway bento can be both a standardized service of a state-owned railway and a geographical marker written by local businesses and travelers alike. For tourists, "where to buy" and "what to eat" are equally important. When the bento box is opened, the route is still inside.

## VIII. From Lunchbox to Cultural Field

The preliminary plan for the Fengshan Station Railway Bento Experience Museum, released in 2026 by the Taiwan Design Institute, plans to center on a century of bento culture, displaying ingredients, production processes, catering division of labor, and craftsmanship; before the official venue is complete, an experience store called "Bento All Right!" will serve as a precursor. This is a planned future, not a completed museum.[^6]

This direction carries risks: if the culture only remains in nostalgic packaging, the bento risks becoming a souvenir without labor. But if procurement, preparation, cooking, insulation, delivery, and platform sales are all explained clearly, what the audience sees is not just ribs, but a working system designed to get passengers their meal on time.

In 2020, the National Railway Museum prepared bentos by documenting the dishes developed by various TRA restaurants and train operations departments through imagery, using economical rib bentos and local flavor bentos as initial subjects to create models preserving railway dining culture.[^7]

The preliminary exhibition in 2024 and the "Japanese Style × Taiwanese Flavor" exchange exhibition in 2025 placed the bento within a narrative alongside literature, platform vending, dining cars, tea service, and railway hotels; the latter exhibited 188 items, with Taiwan providing 157 and Japan providing 31.[^3] [^8]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The best outcome of putting everyday food into an exhibition is not making it sacred, but making people see the work they usually overlook. What is worth preserving about the railway bento is not just the taste of ribs, but the technology that keeps a bento whole during travel.

## IX. Today's Bento Still Follows the Schedule

TRA currently offers a service where bentos can be pre-ordered online. Passengers can select them after successful ticketing, with a deadline of 17:00 the day before travel; bentos are only available for specified train services and delivery zones, delivered to the designated seat by on-board staff during the journey.[^9] The current catering department page also shows that bento sales remain part of TRA's catering service.[^10]

This system is much quieter than platform vending, yet it retains the original essence of the railway bento: it is not a restaurant at a fixed address, but a mobile service that must align with schedules, zones, seats, and time.

### Twelve Boxes in the Carriage: The Last Mile Delivery of Bento

A carriage photo taken in 2010 shows twelve boxes of TRA bentos stacked inside an EMU600 type electric multiple unit. Before passengers opened them, the boxes already showed another side of bento service: it was not just "bought" on the platform; it had to enter the carriage as a box and then be distributed by staff to the passengers.[^13]

This scene is not contradictory to early platform vending. The former relied on human shouts and hands reaching through windows for transactions; the latter relied on cardboard boxes, doors, seats, and delivery zones for fulfillment; the technology changed, but the railway bento remains a process of delivering food onto a moving vehicle.

English reports estimate that TRA bentos are priced in the NT$50 to NT$100 range; this range makes it both a lunch for commuters and a ritual passengers specifically wait for on the train. Price, delivery, and seating arrangement are all part of "how one box arrives on time during travel."[^11]

Japanese _ekiben_ is an important reference, but Taiwanese railway bentos did not stop at imitation. Japanese bentos often emphasize separation, cold food, and local specialties; Taiwanese bentos often place vegetables, meat, sauces, and rice in the same box, with the ribs undergoing a double process of frying and braising. It received external systems and technology but had to operate again within a humid climate, island distances, limited carriages, and different dietary habits.[^11]

## X. Slicing Long Journeys into Bites

To write the TRA bento as "national memory" does not mean everyone remembers the same flavor. Some remember the steam rising from the box on the train; some remember platform vending; some only get a piece of ribs when family returns home; and some remember that the lunchbox must be returned, never taking it home. The commonality of memory is often not in the content being identical, but in everyone having waited for a meal during travel.

The history of railway bentos is not just the official version. Archives from the Provincial Assembly of Taiwan in 1994 still show cases where citizens petitioned to continue selling bentos at the Fenglin Station platform on the Hualien–Taitung line; this reminds us that after TRA became institutionalized, private sales and local stations did not disappear immediately.[^12]

Therefore, the bento can be a state-run service, a local business, a passenger memory, and a craftsman's technique. It was changed for speed, it was made saltier due to preservation issues, it returned to the round box out of nostalgia, and it developed different flavors because of locality.

The TRA bento remains not because it is always the same, but because each generation can re-eat it as their own in transit. The train shakes, the rice is still hot, the rib sauce stains the chopsticks, and the city outside the window has changed stations; a meal tailored for the transportation system ultimately preserved the signal of "I am going home" for many people.
