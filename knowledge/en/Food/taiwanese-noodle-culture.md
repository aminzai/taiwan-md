# Taiwanese Noodle Culture: A Seventy-Year Saga from US Aid Flour to National Delicacy

## Setting the Scene: The First Bowl of Noodles in front of Waterlily Palace, Tainan

In 1895, in front of Waterlily Palace, Tainan. Fishermen like Hong Yutou sold bowls of hot noodles while carrying _bianyong_ (a type of food/wares), under the faint glow of paper lanterns. This was not the first time noodles appeared in Taiwan, but it marked the beginning of the legend of "Duo Xiaoyue." At that time, Taiwan was an agrarian society; cattle were farming partners rather than ingredients, and noodles were mostly a makeshift meal near fishing ports.

Today, half a century later, when _Asia Weekly_ names Taipei Beef Noodle Soup as "One of Asia's Best Foods," and when travelers from Japan and Hong Kong insist on tasting Taiwanese beef noodles before leaving, we might ask: **How did Taiwan’s most famous noodles—which are less than a hundred years old—transform from memories of foreign lands into an island identity in just a few decades?**

## The Counterintuitive Core: "Sichuan-flavored" Beef Noodle Soup Was Actually Invented in Taiwan

When discussing Taiwanese noodles, most people think of long and storied traditions. However, the truth is: the representative Sichuan-flavored braised beef noodle soup of Taiwan did not originate from Sichuan; it was an **authentic Taiwanese invention**.

This must begin with the post-war era. In 1949, the Kuomintang government relocated to Taiwan, bringing military and civilian populations from various provinces. The Sichuanese chefs stationed at the Air Force Base in Okayama, Kaohsiung, faced homesickness and real difficulties: they longed for beef dishes from their hometowns but lacked authentic ingredients; they had US aid flour but lacked familiar seasonings. Thus, a great culinary experiment began.

They used local chili bean paste (especially "Earth Brand") from Okayama to simulate the flavor of Sichuan bean paste; they used canned US military beef to recreate memories of braised beef; and they paired it with noodles made from US aid flour. The resulting "Sichuan-flavored braised beef noodle soup" was, in fact, **an innovation combining the memories of the military family villages with local Taiwanese ingredients.**

Food writer Luo Yaodong once said, "The Sichuan-flavored braised beef noodle soup was invented in the Okayama Air Force military family village, using bean paste to stir-fry the broth, and only then did it become popular in Taipei and spread throughout Taiwan." This is not genuine Sichuan cuisine, but rather a unique "military family village cuisine"—a reconstruction of homeland flavors in a foreign land.

## Curator's Note 1: Noodles as Witnesses to History

**If rice carries the memory of agrarian Taiwan, noodles record the process of modern Taiwan’s birth.**

From the Danzai Noodles of "Duo Xiaoyue," we can see the seasonal fluctuations of early Taiwanese economy. "Xiaoyue" refers to the typhoon season, a low season when maritime operations decrease and fishermen's income drops sharply. Hong Yutou setting up his stall in front of Waterlily Palace was precisely to survive this difficult period. A small bowl of Danzai Noodles, not large in portion but rich in flavor layers, perfectly reflects the wisdom of the Taiwanese people to "make every bit count."

The post-war military family village noodles witnessed a larger historical turning point. When culinary cultures from Sichuan, Shandong, Henan, and Shaanxi converged in Taiwan, noodles became the medium for "dispersion" and "recomposition." **Every strand of noodle carries nostalgia; every mouthful of broth redefines the taste of 'home.'**

## From South to North: The Geography of Taiwanese Noodles

### Tainan: The Noodle Origin of Maritime Civilization

Tainan's noodle culture originates from maritime trade. Besides Danzai Noodles, there are also specialties like _yianmian_ (egg noodles) and eel noodles. Tainan egg noodles originated in saltwater, with eggs added to the flour, resulting in a yellowish hue, which is called "Yuruyi." These noodles are relatively thick and suitable for pairing with rich broths or stir-frying.

**The essence of Danzai Noodles** lies in being "small yet refined." A small portion, yet featuring multiple layers of side dishes like shrimp, chives, meat sauce, and garlic paste, combined with a sweet broth simmered from shrimp heads and pig bones—every bite is concentrated satisfaction. This philosophy of "meticulous craftsmanship" later influenced the development of noodles in other parts of Taiwan.

### The North: A Melting Pot of Military Family Village Memories

The areas around Yongkang Street and Jianuo South Road in Taipei were once centers for military family village cuisine. Here, there was not only Sichuan-flavored braised beef noodle soup but also Shandong's scallion pancakes, Henan's mixed noodles, and Shaanxi's _zaozi mian_ (minced meat noodles). **Every alleyway is a province; every noodle stall is a segment of migration history.**

The characteristic of northern noodles is "rich diversity." Even within beef noodles, there are various flavors such as braised, clear-broth, mala (spicy), and tomato. This diversity reflects the historical background of Taipei as a political center that gathered immigrants from various provinces.

### Central and South: Localized Innovation Experiments

Although _rouyuan_ (meat balls) in Changhua is not noodles, it also uses starch for its skin and belongs to the broad category of noodle culture. The garlic-flavored meat soup noodles of Yilan and the _bianshi_ (wontons) of Hualien all demonstrate different regions' localized transformations of foreign noodles.

**The specialty of Taichung** is "rouzao rice" (meat sauce rice), which combines the concept of northern meat sauce with central Taiwanese taste preferences. Meanwhile, Yunlin and Chiayi developed braised beef noodle soup that uses Chinese medicinal herbs instead of bean paste, demonstrating the influence of Traditional Chinese Medicine dietetics on noodles.

## Curator's Note 2: The Democratic Spirit of Commoner Noodles

**Yangchun noodles, wonton noodles, and qiezai noodles—these "commoner noodles" may not have the fame of beef noodles, but they better represent the democratic spirit of Taiwanese noodle culture.**

Yangchun noodles are made from low-gluten flour, water, and salt; the body is smooth and chewy, and the ingredients are simple yet meticulous. A bowl of Yangchun noodles late at night often soothes the soul of travelers; this quality of "simple yet warm" is precisely the core of Taiwanese food culture.

Wonton noodles have different interpretations across Taiwan. The north prefers small, delicate wontons, while the south likes large, plump _bianshi_. **This regional difference is not a division but an expression of richness**—the same food growing in different forms on different lands.

Qiezai noodles embody the "flexible wisdom" of the Taiwanese people. They are cooked and sold fresh according to customer demand; they can be dry or wet, and can be paired with various side dishes. This concept of "customization" later influenced Taiwan's service industry culture.

## The Instant Noodle Revolution: An Industrial Miracle of Taiwanese Flavor

In 1973, Weilife Food launched _zha jiang mian_ (savory sauce noodles), pioneering the fast-food dry noodle system of "separate wet and dry." This instant noodle, with its unique savory bean paste packet, is still one of the "Four Heavenly Kings of Taiwanese Instant Noodles" (alongside Uni's rouzao noodles, Waiyang's original beef noodles, and Wei Dan's pork chop chicken noodles).

**The success of Taiwanese instant noodles is not just an industrial achievement but a manifestation of cultural innovation.** In 1983, Uni introduced _Manhan Grand Feast Scallion Braised Beef Noodles_, inviting master chef Fu Peimei to develop the recipe, introducing the concept of high-end cuisine into fast food. This "refinement" mindset later became a characteristic of Taiwan's food industry.

More importantly, Taiwanese instant noodle manufacturers realized the importance of "localization" early on. They did not simply imitate Japanese ramen; they developed flavors with Taiwanese characteristics such as meat sauce, beef, and pork chop chicken. These flavors conquered the domestic market and successfully expanded internationally, allowing the world to experience instant noodles with a "Taiwanese flavor."

## Dry Noodle Culture: A Grand Transformation from Tradition to Fashion

The dry noodle system in Taiwan has deep traditional roots. Oil noodles (alkaline noodles), which are golden yellow due to the addition of alkaline water, are chewy and robust, serving as the main character in Taiwanese stir-fried noodles. After being introduced to Taiwan, knife-cut noodles were improved into a wavy shape that is thick in the middle and thinner on the sides, increasing the surface area for sauce adhesion.

Entering the 21st century, **the rise of dry mixed noodles injected new vitality into Taiwanese noodle culture.** From _zengban mian_ (mixed noodles) to Manhan Grand Feast, various brands of dry mixed noodles appeared like spring sprouts, even successfully entering international markets. This type of noodle, which does not contain broth, mixes the seasoning and noodles directly, creating a rich texture while meeting the fast-paced lifestyle needs of modern people.

**The success of dry mixed noodles demonstrates the innovative capacity of Taiwan's food industry.** It retains the essence of traditional noodles while incorporating modern packaging and marketing concepts. Weilife _zha jiang mian_ can be eaten dry or made into soup; there are endless creative ways to eat it, with some even adding cream and cheese shreds to create a new style called "creamy mixed noodles."

## International Influence: The Cultural Export of Taiwanese Noodles

**While Japanese ramen culture swept through Taiwan, Taiwanese noodles were also exporting their cultural influence to the world.**

Taiwanese beef noodles have become a must-try "Taiwanese flavor" for international tourists. Many foreign visitors come to Taiwan and specifically ask for beef noodles as their first meal. This is not just a taste experience; it is a cultural exchange. Through a bowl of beef noodles, foreigners can feel the historical layers, cultural inclusiveness, and innovative spirit of Taiwan.

The international success of Taiwanese instant noodles is also worth noting. Brands like Uni and Weilife do not only supply the domestic market but also extend their reach overseas. These companies promote instant noodles with Taiwanese flavors to international markets, allowing consumers around the world to taste a "Taiwanese flavor."

**More importantly, it is the export of ideology:** values such as "inclusive innovation," "meticulous craftsmanship," and "democratic diversity" embodied by Taiwanese noodle culture are also influencing the development of global food culture.

## Curator's Note 3: Rouzao Rice vs. Beef Noodle Soup—The Dual Identity of National Delicacies

**In Taiwanese food culture, there is an interesting phenomenon of "dual identity": both _rouzao rice_ and beef noodles are considered "national delicacies," but they represent different cultural genes.**

_Rouzao rice_ embodies the spirit of "deep local roots." It is cheap and widespread, found from roadside stalls to high-end restaurants, truly achieving the commoner ideal of "delicious and affordable." Furthermore, there are regional differences: northern _rouzao rice_ uses minced pork (_rouzao_), while southern _rouzao rice_ uses three layers of meat chunks (_kongrou_). This regional difference is not a conflict but an expression of richness.

Beef noodles represent the spirit of "integrated innovation." They originate from overseas military family villages but are re-created on Taiwanese soil. From Sichuan flavors to clear broth, and from tomato to mala, the diverse development of beef noodles reflects the inclusiveness of Taiwanese culture.

**The coexistence of these two national delicacies precisely symbolizes the dual characteristics of Taiwanese culture: possessing deep local roots while also having an open spirit of innovation.** They are not in opposition but complementary, jointly forming a complete picture of Taiwanese food culture.

## Future Outlook: The Eternal Cycle from Tradition to Innovation

Facing the challenges of globalization, Taiwanese noodle culture is also seeking new directions for development. **Healthification, refinement, and internationalization** are current important trends.

In terms of healthification, products like whole wheat noodles, vegetable noodles, and low-sodium broths are gaining increasing attention. In terms of refinement, high-end beef noodles, boutique ramen, and creative Italian noodles are elevating traditional foods to higher levels. In terms of internationalization, Taiwanese cuisine is flourishing around the world; one can see traces of Taiwanese food culture from Taiwanese ramen shops in New York to beef noodle restaurants in London.

**No matter how it changes, the core value of Taiwanese noodle culture remains unchanged: inclusiveness, innovation, refinement, and commonness.** These characteristics make Taiwanese noodles not just food but a carrier of culture, a bond of emotion, and a symbol of identity.

From the first bowl of Danzai Noodles in front of Waterlily Palace in 1895 to the Taiwanese beef noodles that are popular globally today, Taiwanese noodle culture has gone through more than a century of development. **This is not just an evolution of recipes; it is an epic of how a migrant society rebuilt its home and created an identity on new land.**

In today's globalized world, when we taste a bowl of Taiwanese noodles in any corner of the world, what we are tasting is not just deliciousness, but the cultural density of an island, the collective memory of a group of people, and a ceaseless creativity. **The noodles may break, but the spirit of creation that the Taiwanese people embody will never be severed.**
