> **30-Second Overview:** The Alishan Forest Railway is not merely a tourist train for viewing sunrises. It began operations in 1906 to transport high-mountain timber down the slopes and was initially completed to Erwanping in 1912, later gradually shifting toward passenger service and tourism. The Morakot typhoon halted it again in 2009 for 15 years. Today, this 71.6-kilometer route connects the Chiayi plains, mountain settlements, and high-mountain forests. As a railway born from forest development becomes a cultural asset, what are we preserving: the tracks, or the entire history of human coexistence with the mountains? [^1] [^2] [^3]

## What the Train First Crossed Was Not Scenery, But a Difference in Elevation

In December 1912, the Alishan Railway from Chiayi to Erwanping was officially completed and opened. The starting altitude was approximately 30 meters, while Alishan Station is at 2,216 meters. [^1] [^2] Traveling uphill from the plain, the train first passed through farmlands before entering bamboo forests, tea plantations, and forests of varying altitudes. Passengers witnessed layers of changing scenery, but engineers faced the challenge of keeping the track continuous across steep slopes, fault lines, and rainwater runoff.

The operational length of this route is about 71.6 kilometers, and its cultural landscape potential spans Chiayi City, Chiayi County, and Nantou County, encompassing the railway, settlements, forest remnants, and natural environment. [^2] Therefore, it is not an isolated transportation facility but a linear cultural landscape connecting plain cities, mountain communities, and ecological zones.

![Alishan Forest Railway DL 36 Locomotive at Beimen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fd/Taiwan%2C_Alishan_Forest_Railway_%27DL_36%27_at_Beimen.jpg)

_Image: DL 36 locomotive stopping at Beimen. Author Industrial Wales. CC BY-SA 2.0. See the image page and licensing information on [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwan,_Alishan_Forest_Railway_%27DL*36%27_at_Beimen.jpg).[^10]*

Thus, the "terminus" of the Alishan Forest Railway is actually several places. For travelers, the terminus is Alishan Station. For early logging operations, the terminus was when the timber reached Chiayi and entered factories and markets. For the cultural landscape, the terminus cannot just stop at the station platform; it must extend to how the settlements along the line continue to live. This multiplicity of termini means a train journey is not just moving from low to high, but also traveling between different eras of Taiwan.

What makes the Alishan Forest Railway special is not just its "ascent." It possesses characteristics of a forest railway, mountain railway, and high-mountain railway, using 762 mm narrow gauge, leaving behind spirals, reversals, and large curves along the route due to topography. [^2] The spiral line at Duling Mountain allows the train to climb around the mountain mass. When space is limited, Z-shaped reversals allow the train to gain altitude by going up and down repeatedly.

| Place You Pass Through | Historical Clues It Leaves Behind                                                                               |
| ---------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Beimen Station         | A wooden station on the plain, connected to the wood texture of Chiayi. [^2]                                    |
| Duling Mountain        | Uses a spiral route to handle steep terrain and gain altitude. [^2]                                             |
| Fenqihu                | An intermediate stop where logging work meets mountain communities. [^2]                                        |
| Zhaoping, Zhushan      | From the forest line to the high-mountain railway, gradually shifting toward tourism and sunrise journeys. [^2] |

This table is not a travel itinerary but a way of reading the railway: each station name points simultaneously to a type of terrain, a period of industrial history, and a way in which people stayed in the mountains.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** What is most memorable about the Alishan Railway is not that it is "beautiful," but that it transformed elevation difference into a visible engineering language. As the forests change along the window, the route beneath the wheels answers the same question: the mountain will not yield to the railway; the railway must learn to go around.

The slowness of the train allows this relationship to be seen. It does not compress the mountain scenery into a photograph from a highway; instead, it lets passengers pass through different forest types, stations, and tunnels. This speed also preserves the original scale of an industrial railway. This is not romanticizing slow travel, but acknowledging that in mountainous areas, safety, maintenance, and viewing all require time.

## It Was Originally a Road to Turn Forests into Commodities

In 1903, the Taiwan Governor-General's Office began planning the development of Alishan forests. Construction started by Fujita in 1906, later taken over by government operations due to financial issues. The line from Chiayi to Erwanping was completed and opened in 1912, with a total length of 66.6 kilometers. As forest development expanded, the route was later extended to Alishan, forming the current scale of about 71.6 kilometers. [^1]

This history cannot be whitewashed by the four words "century railway." The Alishan Forest Railway was built to transport timber; logging operations officially began after its extension to Zhaoping in 1914. From the Japanese colonial period until the end of the war in 1945, the Alishan forest operated for about 30 years, and the original cypress forests were heavily logged as a result. [^2]

![View of Beimen Station platform from an Alishan Forest Railway carriage](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/0f/Alishan_Forest_Railway%2C_Beimen_Station%2C_Chiayi_CIty_%28Taiwan%29.jpg)

_Image: View of Beimen Station platform from an Alishan Forest Railway carriage. Author Malcolm Koo. CC BY-SA 4.0. See the image page and licensing information on [Wikimedia Commons](<https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Alishan_Forest_Railway,_Beimen_Station,_Chiayi_CIty_(Taiwan).jpg>).[^11]\_

The changes brought by the railway were not limited to the mountain. Sawmills, timber wholesale and processing, employee dormitories, businesses, and schools clustered along the transport system, leading Chiayi to gradually form an urban texture centered on the timber industry, known as the "City of Wood." [^2] Thus, one industrial line performed two tasks: transporting forest resources out of the mountain and bringing urban life into the mountains.

This is the first contrast of the Alishan Forest Railway. Today it is viewed as slow travel close to nature, but its original mission was to utilize the forests more efficiently. If it is only written as a romantic high-mountain train, readers miss the Taiwanese history this railway truly carries: modernization, forestry, urban growth, and the process of recalculating natural resources.

Academic research discusses the Alishan Forest Railway at the intersection of "railway cultural heritage preservation" and "cultural tourism," rather than treating it as a single scenic spot. [^7] This perspective is important because it shows us that conservation faces two types of time simultaneously: the material age of the railway, stations, and locomotives; and the social age of industrial decline, rising tourism, and changing local life. If only the former is restored, the latter will disappear from the story.

## From Logging Line to Cultural Landscape: Transformation Is Not Deleting the Past

After the logging industry retired, the railway did not disappear. Its function gradually shifted to passenger service, tourism, and cultural education; stations, settlements, tunnels, bridges, and forest remnants along the line transformed from production equipment into historical sites that are preserved, interpreted, and used. The Forestry and Natural Conservation Agency summarizes this change as a transformation of settlement texture and human-land interaction from logging industry to tourism. [^1]

Beimen Station is an excellent entry point for this transition. Formed around 1910–1912, it was a Japanese wooden station built with Alishan red cypress, serving as the first space where the plain understood the forest railway. [^2] When travelers walk from Chiayi City into Beimen Station, they see not the mountain scenery but how timber is received, processed, and distributed in the city. Here, "forest" and "city" are placed on the same platform.

In 2018, the Alishan Forest Railway and Cultural Heritage Management Office was established to more centrally promote research, education, and cultural heritage management. In 2019, the "Alishan Forest Railway and Tramway" was registered as Taiwan's first national-level important cultural landscape, with its value organized into five aspects: natural ecology, forestry development, railway technology, settlement life, and human memory. [^1]

The significance of this registration is that it does not just protect a station or a stretch of track. The subject of the cultural landscape is the railway and the work, living, transport, and natural environment surrounding it. In other words, preservation is not locking an old carriage in a glass case; it is ensuring that a set of relationships that once operated can still be understood.

Alishan has also entered discussions as a potential World Heritage site. Official documents describe it as covering 32,900 hectares, including the surrounding settlements and ecological environment of the railway, from Chiayi, Beimen, Zhuxi, Zhangnaoliao, Fenqihu all the way to Zhaoping. [^2] This scale reminds us that Alishan is not a "scenic spot," but a system spanning cities, mountainous areas, and industrial memory.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** What is hardest to preserve in cultural heritage is often not the wood and steel rails, but how they were once used by people. If the Alishan Forest Railway only remains as a beautiful station, it is just an outer shell. What truly needs preservation is how one line changed the fate of Chiayi, mountain villages, and forests.

## 2009: When the Mountain Exposed the Railway's Vulnerability

After the Morakot typhoon in 2009, 421 disasters occurred along the Alishan Forest Railway, including landslides, road collapses, and bridge interruptions. Official reviews indicated that this railway was halted for 15 years as a result. The section from Chiayi to Fenqihu resumed service in 2014, but Typhoon Dujuan delayed the repair plans for the remaining sections again in 2015. [^3] [^4]

This was not an engineering project of "fixing and returning to normal." The mountain's foundation is washed away by water; tunnels encounter rockfalls; and the original route may be located in a geological environment no longer suitable for carrying trains. Interview records from National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University mention that repair teams evaluated building a new tunnel between Pingshan and Erwanping, and dealt with the collapse of Tunnel No. 42 between Shuzilu and Pingshan. [^5]

In 2021, the management unit constructed a new tunnel of about 1.1 kilometers between Zhuxi and Zhaoping, costing about 420 million NTD. Construction was affected by factors such as flooding, rockfalls, labor shortages, and explosive material supply. [^4] These figures are not just the scale of engineering but also indicate that "full line operation" is not simply rejoining old tracks, but re-evaluating where to preserve and where a detour must be made.

On July 6, 2024, the entire line from Chiayi to Alishan resumed service. [^3] An English article on tourism by the Ministry of Agriculture described the main line as the 71.6-kilometer route from Chiayi Station to Alishan Station, with Chiayi Station at an altitude of about 30 meters and Alishan Station at 2,216 meters. The full resumption was thus not just a news item for tourism but also represented the reconnection of a long-disrupted mountain public transport system due to disasters. [^6]

This date of resumption has changed the meaning of the "Alishan Little Train." A photo essay in _The New York Times_ places this journey at the intersection of Taiwanese cultural heritage and international travelers. [^8] But what truly gives it substance is not whether it can be a beautiful postcard, but whether travelers know why the line was interrupted, who repaired it, and what challenges must be faced after the repair.

## The End of Repair Is Not Reopening, But the Next Rainstorm

After resuming service in 2024, the safety work for the Forest Railway is no longer solely reliant on personnel patrols. Repair projects have incorporated track and slope abnormality detection systems. Between Tunnels No. 29 and No. 30, the system issues warnings when rockfalls or trees obstruct the tracks, allowing station staff to notify the driver. [^4]

Interviews with National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University also record the repair team's emphasis on long-term monitoring, including using LiDAR to track topographical changes and identify potential risks early. The interview mentioned that after Typhoon Kaimi in 2024, the Forest Railway briefly stopped to clear debris and fallen trees before resuming operations. [^5] For a mountain railway, "recovery" does not mean never stopping again; it means knowing how to judge the next step faster and safer after every interruption.

In its annual review of reopening in 2025, the official body divided the repair content into tracks, roads, locomotive maintenance, digitized along-line communication, and site creation. [^3] This list appears technical, but it precisely shows that cultural preservation cannot rely solely on nostalgia: for the old railway to remain alive, it must possess historical credibility, modern safety standards, and a system capable of continuous maintenance.

As the railway becomes a tourist route again, new pressures emerge. Official World Heritage potential data has long pointed out that Alishan requires long-term management for human impact from tourists and vehicles, disaster reconstruction, and environmental maintenance. [^2] Tourism can help more people understand the cultural landscape, but it can also subject the mountains to greater traffic, garbage, noise, and maintenance burdens. "Letting the world see Alishan" and "not consuming Alishan" must both be true.

## The Question Left by One Railway

The most moving aspect of the Alishan Forest Railway is not that it finally conquered the mountain. It never conquered the mountain: the 1906 construction had to navigate the terrain; the heavy rains in 2009 could wash away the foundation; the collapse in 2015 could delay the nearly completed resumption again; and every typhoon since 2024 continues to test it. [^3] [^4] [^5]

What it truly achieved is making human engineering gradually acknowledge the mountain's conditions. The early railway treated forests as resources that could be transported, while the later cultural landscape forces us to place the railway, settlements, ecology, and disaster on the same map. This is not rewriting the past history of logging into an ecological story, but acknowledging that Taiwan once developed its mountains using this line, and must now face the consequences of that development with new governance methods.

So, when riding the Alishan Forest Railway, remember two directions. Traveling up shows you the changes from tropical forest to temperate forest to high-mountain forest. Traveling back shows you how timber, workers, cities, indigenous traditional territories, typhoons, and repair projects are layered on one line. [^2]

You can also remember a third direction: looking sideways. The story of Alishan is not just in the straight path forward from the carriage; it is also in the preserved side lines, the old corridors converted into walking paths, the shops in the settlements, and the surrounding forests and the long-term living spaces of the Tsou indigenous people. Official English materials introduce the example of the Shishan Line's old corridor being turned into a trail, demonstrating that closure does not necessarily mean disappearance. A line can continue to be used in another way. [^6]

The Alishan Forest Railway has not taken Taiwan away from nature. It is more like a long reminder: we entered the forest with tracks, and only after learning to respect the speed, slope, and uncertainty of the forest can we possibly remain there.
