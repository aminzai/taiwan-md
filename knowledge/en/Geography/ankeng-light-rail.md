> **30-second overview:** On February 10, 2023, the Ankeng LRT in New Taipei City's Xindian District began operations; the full 7.5-kilometer line with 9 stations runs from Ankeng across the Xindian River to connect with the circular line at Xintianzhuang. Originally intended to provide a fixed outbound route for Ankeng's growing population and narrow roads, the line carried about 2.03 million riders in 2025, with a 2026 public review citing a ridership achievement rate of 65.38%. The real story of the Ankeng LRT is not just about opening day, but how a stretch of track finds its place in daily life among hillside communities, existing buses, transfer networks, and resident habits. Data sources: [New Taipei City Bureau of Metro Engineering](https://www.dorts.ntpc.gov.tw/about/route/jW0OdPGn2nbJ), [New Taipei Metro](https://www.ntmetro.com.tw/basic/?mode=detail&node=843), and [United Daily News audit report](https://udn.com/news/story/7323/9655456).

## Ankang Road Has Only One Track: Transit Demand Grows Out of Traffic Jams

In May 2015, the National Development Council deliberated the Ankeng LRT system and surrounding land development comprehensive plan. The official documentation did not begin with a beautiful train, but with a very practical dilemma: Ankeng in Xindian's population continued to grow, while outbound roads failed to keep pace with development and widening, making traffic congestion a recurring issue raised by residents and local representatives. [^1]

Later, the Central News Agency summarized Ankeng's traffic background by narrowing it down to a single road: in the 1980s, Ankeng's population surged, but outbound travel still relied mainly on Ankang Road, which lacked sufficient road width, had heavy bus traffic, and was prone to congestion. This also explains why residents were waiting not for an isolated station, but for a transit option that could maintain predictability during peak hours. [^2]

NDRC records show the Ankeng line feasibility study was approved in principle by the Executive Yuan on July 1, 2013, before entering comprehensive planning. Early planning envisioned a corridor of about 7.67 kilometers, 9 stations, and 1 depot, connecting Ankeng to the Xintianzhuang circular line. Current operational data describes the line as 7.5 kilometers connecting to K9 Xintianzhuang Station. These two sets of figures belong to the planning stage and the completed route respectively, and should not be conflated into a single version. [^1] [^3]

The planning documents recorded a self-recovery rate of 55.18%, total project cost of NT$1.6632 billion, with NT$396.8 million in central government subsidies. The New Taipei City Bureau of Metro Engineering later supplemented that the forward-looking subsidy was NT$343.9 million, with New Taipei City bearing NT$1.2664 billion, roughly 76% of the total cost. Placing these figures back into Ankeng's road context, the issue is not merely "how much the LRT cost," but whether the city should use fixed-track infrastructure to claim space for daily transit when the population has already grown beyond what roads can expand. [^1] [^4]

> **📝 Curator's note:** The Ankeng LRT is most easily written as "a new line to a new town." But it is actually the reverse: residents lived for decades in areas with inadequate roads, and the tracks were slowly pulled into existing settlements. Understanding this sequence explains why later transfer and connection issues cannot be dismissed with "insufficient capacity" alone.

## From Ankeng All the Way to the Network at Xintianzhuang

Today's Ankeng line is about 7.5 kilometers long with 9 stations. The route starts from the depot at the intersection of Antai Road and Anyi Road, heading north along Anyi Road, then turning into Anhe Road via the Anhe branch line. From K6 to K8, the line proceeds via elevated structures, crossing over National Highway 3 and the Xindian River before arriving at K9 in the Xintianzhuang area, where it connects with the circular line's Y8 Xintianzhuang Station. [^3]

The line has 5 elevated stations and 4 at-grade stations, with a full journey taking about 21 minutes. The first section (K1 to K5) is close to Anyi Road and existing communities, while the latter section runs along Anhe Road, the Xindian River, and the Xintianzhuang area. This is a short line that must constantly change posture between residential areas, roads, bridges, and rivers. [^2] [^5]

Several key milestones show how the Ankeng LRT evolved from policy documents into a daily operating public facility:

The MOT completed the inspection in January 2023, approved operations on February 2, and officially opened on February 10. Before opening, officials estimated that commuting time to Taipei City could be reduced by up to 15 to 20 minutes, with about 53,000 entries and exits per day during the first year of operation. These were estimates made during planning and before opening, not actual operational results. [^2] [^3]

The trial run on February 10, 2023 was also recorded by the English media Focus Taiwan: passengers could ride for free until March 12. For a local track that had waited nearly 30 years, free trial rides were like a public test, allowing residents to first convert "will it be convenient in the future" into an actual round trip. [^9]

## An Anxin Bridge That Sends Ankeng to Xintianzhuang

The most recognizable feature of the Ankeng LRT may not be a station, but the Anxin Bridge spanning the Xindian River. According to the Central News Agency, the bridge adopts a single-tower asymmetric cable-stayed design, spanning about 400 meters of waterway, with a total bridge length of 502 meters, maximum span of 225 meters, and tower height of 130 meters. [^10]

The constraints under the bridge are very specific. The Xindian River area already has circular line bridges, Zhong'an Bridge, and Xiulung Bridge. To avoid increasing piers and causing water backflow, the Anxin Bridge could only have 2 piers. The construction team therefore adopted a three-span continuous cable-stayed design and installed fixed tower cranes on the riverbed. The bridge had to grow outward section by section, rather than first building a temporary trestle and dismantling it after completion. [^10]

The same route also had to cross over the环河路 and National Highway 3. The construction team could only use the time after the last bus departed until the morning traffic returned to complete traffic maintenance, entry, construction, and removal. Reports also recorded inconsistencies between pile positions and existing utility maps for sewage, gas, and water pipes, with construction once breaking a sewage pipe. [^10]

These details make "crossing the Xindian River" more than just a route description. The Ankeng LRT does not pass through a blank map, but through a city that already has bridges, buses, pipelines, and residents' daily routines. Every newly added section of track must first secure space to coexist with existing life.

## Butterflies, Fireflies, and a Train Car Full of Mountains

The Ankeng LRT also brings the "mountain city" into view for passengers. According to the Central News Agency, the train's exterior design is inspired by Ankeng's mountains and forests and early settlers' reliance on the hills, with carriages featuring large side windows. The 9 stations and depot follow the design concept of "an exhibition hall of light and green, a guardian station of home and mountains." [^11]

The "Flying Ankeng" at Xintianzhuang Station places 80 colorful butterflies inside the station. The "Fireflies Find Their Way Home" on the platform consists of 1,000 micro wind turbines that glow when the wind blows. These works do not simply make the stations beautiful, but place Ankeng's natural narrative along the path that commuters pass through every day. [^11]

The New Taipei City Bureau of Metro Engineering's 2025 award announcement further elaborated on this design language: station bodies respond to the surrounding landscape through "seasonal light corridors," natural lighting, and perforated designs, using imagery such as "listening to the wind tea pavilions" and "green tunnels" to handle different station areas. This is the official explanation of engineering and design, which can explain how stations were planned, but cannot directly prove that passengers are more willing to ride because of it. [^7]

The Anxin Bridge thus gained another layer of meaning. The design imagines white birds spreading their wings on the Xindian River, with the bridge tower, trains, rivers, and circular line bridges all appearing in the line of sight. The tracks here do more than just send people to the next stop—they reconnect the riverbank scenery that would otherwise be cut apart by expressways. This is the viewing perspective provided by the design report, which cannot replace the measurement of transportation effectiveness, but can explain why a bridge becomes a local memory. [^11]

> **📝 Curator's note:** The public art of the Ankeng LRT does not bring nature into the stations, but reminds passengers: this line has always had to pass through mountains, rivers, roads, and communities. When the green outside the train window is not a background but a living space that the route must carefully navigate, the stations finally become localized.

## A Train Carries Not Only People, but Also Connects Five Bus Lines

The transportation capacity of the Ankeng LRT is not determined solely by the trains. The New Taipei Tourism Network's ride data records that the Ankeng LRT trains consist of 5 cars, with a maximum capacity of 265 people, are 100% low-floor trains, and can reach a maximum speed of 70 kilometers per hour. Passengers use electronic fare cards or monthly ticket machines, with their own usage rules for entering and exiting stations and train doors. [^5]

More importantly, the same page lists five shuttle bus lines coordinated with the Ankeng LRT: Ankeng Line 1 runs from Erzhu Zhu Plantation to Taipei Xiaocheng, Ankeng Line 2 runs from Liming Qingjing to Meiflower China Town, Ankeng Line 3 runs from Linyu Xiango to Anzhong Road, Ankeng Line 5 runs from Wucheng Community to Kengshin Ankang Area, and Ankeng Line 6 runs from Antai Road to the LRT Shuangcheng Station. These shuttle buses are included in the NT$1,280 public transportation monthly pass, with YouBike stations at each stop. [^5]

This system arrangement reveals an often-overlooked fact: the Ankeng LRT does not aim to replace all buses, but to separate "entering communities" and "fixed cross-district" into two layers. Buses are better at navigating into hillside communities, while the LRT provides a main line unaffected by traffic flow. If the schedules of the two do not align, the waiting time for passengers will consume the speed advantage of the tracks.

A year after opening, the operational adjustments announced by the New Taipei City Bureau of Metro Engineering explained this point. Starting February 26, 2024, the peak hours from 6:30 AM to 8:30 AM switched to full-line operation with intervals shortened to 6 to 8 minutes. The afternoon peak from 5:00 PM to 8:00 PM also switched to full-line operation with intervals compressed to 8 to 10 minutes. Other time periods and holidays maintained an average interval of about 15 minutes, coordinating with the circular line's bidirectional transfers. [^6]

This is not simply "adding more trips." It represents that the operations team must observe which sections have people, which sections need connections, and which transfer directions will bring in passengers, then redistribute capacity through intervals. Once the tracks are built, the transportation service begins to learn the residents' schedules.

## Saving Twenty Minutes, First You Have to Find the Connection

The pre-opening estimate of "saving up to 20 minutes" was attractive, but it did not answer the first question every resident encounters: how long does it take from home to the station? Before opening, the Central News Agency interviewed Ankeng residents and recorded that some stations and densely populated communities were still far apart. Some residents walked 10 to 20 minutes uphill from Meiflower China Town Station back to their communities, while others felt that going to Taipei City required first taking a shuttle, then the LRT, then transferring, which was not necessarily more convenient than the original direct bus. [^12]

These interviews are not a statistical survey of all residents, but they make the "last mile" visible: a stretch of uphill path, a shuttle bus, and a decision about whether to transfer once more. For residents with mobility challenges, the LRT's low-floor design and fixed route may be safer than buses. For those accustomed to riding scooters or taking direct buses, one more transfer may negate the speed advantage of the tracks. [^5] [^12]

The New Taipei City Bureau of Metro Engineering also listed several changes that were difficult to anticipate during planning: the Ankeng LRT opened during the post-pandemic adjustment of commuting behavior, with public transportation usage declining and scooter ownership increasing, declining birth rates reducing the number of students along the line below predictions, and development along the line and at Erzhu Zhu Plantation still underway. The government listed these factors as background for the lower-than-expected capacity, and proposed improvements in shuttle buses, pedestrian environments, scenic spot guides, and surrounding development. [^4]

Here it is necessary to distinguish two things: the government's explanation can account for why planning assumptions were rewritten by reality, but it cannot treat "will improve in the future" as "already convenient now." The arrival of tracks in Ankeng only represents the completion of the main line. Whether residents can avoid detours, wait less, and walk safely is the answer to whether they will board every day.

## 2.03 Million Riders in 2025: Convenience Begins, Habits Are Still Forming

New Taipei Metro announced that the Ankeng LRT achieved a stable annual ridership of 2.03 million in 2025. The same announcement also mentioned that the Ankeng LRT is mainly driven by commuting and transfer needs, and continuously cultivated the "young professional card" through commuting-oriented strategies. [^13]

The July 2026 public report further quoted the New Taipei City Control Yuan's review, stating that the Ankeng LRT achieved 65.38% of its 2025 passenger capacity target, with fare revenue reaching 70.02% of the budget. New Taipei Metro responded that the combined ridership of the circular line, Danshui LRT, and Ankeng LRT reached about 30.11 million in 2025, achieving about 93.5% of the annual budget target, and will continue to adjust connections, fare discounts, and surrounding development. These two sets of information cannot offset each other: the overall network can reach new highs, while the Ankeng line can still have its own operational pressure. [^8]

Placing the 2025 figures back into the planning data, the gap becomes apparent, but the time scales must first be aligned:

| Figure                             | Time scale                                                                               | What it can explain                                          | What it cannot be directly called                                                       |
| ---------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| About 53,000 per day               | Pre-opening estimate of first-year daily entries and exits                               | Demand forecast at the time                                  | Not the actual value for 2025. [^2]                                                     |
| About 69,000 per day               | Comprehensive planning target year 130-year forecast                                     | Long-term planning assumptions                               | Not the standard to be achieved immediately after opening. [^4]                         |
| About 2.03 million per year        | Actual annual ridership in 2025                                                          | Equivalent to an annual average of about 5,562 per day       | Cannot be directly compared to the target year's daily figures on the same scale. [^13] |
| 65.38%                             | 2026 report citing the Control Yuan's review of 2025 passenger capacity achievement rate | Shows the gap between budget or targets and actual ridership | Not the "utilization rate" of the Ankeng LRT or all passengers' satisfaction. [^8]      |
| Average 96,000 per day by May 2026 | Latest public figure for the combined three New Taipei Metro lines                       | The overall network is growing                               | Not the single-line ridership of the Ankeng LRT. [^8]                                   |

Putting "2.03 million" and "69,000 per day" together easily becomes a sensationalized failure verdict. But a more accurate way of writing is: the actual usage of the Ankeng LRT is still below planning expectations, and the expectations themselves are divided into first-year, target year, annual budget, and single-line/network scales. Mixing these scales only creates disputes, not helping us understand why capacity fails to grow.

The next section of the Ankeng line may not be a new track, but the pedestrian environment from K7 to Yangshan Sports Park, the frequency of shuttle buses, the clarity of transfers at K9 Xintianzhuang, and whether the area around the stations can form daily destinations worth staying in. The speed of the trains is calculated by engineering; the willingness to use them must be slowly filled in by the city.

## A Silver Medal Station and a Ridership Chart Still Not Filled

In 2025, the Global Excellence in Construction Awards awarded the Ankeng LRT a silver medal in the "Public Sector Infrastructure / Environmental Adaptation Engineering" category. The New Taipei City Bureau of Metro Engineering regarded this as international recognition of design, engineering, and sustainability concepts, and pointed out that the Ankeng LRT is the second LRT line in Taiwan to win the same international award after the Danshui LRT. [^7]

The award and ridership appear to be two report cards that conflict with each other: one praises bridges, stations, landscapes, and urban aesthetics, while the other reminds us that passenger volume and fare revenue have not yet met expectations. In fact, they measure different things. The construction award asks how the project was designed, constructed, and integrated into the environment. Operational performance asks how many people use this line as their own road every day.

The common saying is that as long as the stations are beautiful, the trains are comfortable, and the route connects to the circular line, ridership will eventually follow. But this causal logic is too simple. Ankeng residents' problem is not entirely the absence of tracks, but the slope between stations and homes, the cost of connections and transfers. The problem with planning data is not the lack of vision, but that the pandemic, scooter ownership, declining birth rates, and the progress of surrounding development have changed the original assumptions. [^4] [^12]

A more realistic understanding is: the design award proves that the Ankeng LRT has learned how to integrate mountains, rivers, and urban engineering into a single line. The ridership review reminds us that it has not yet learned how to connect every resident's way of leaving home to this line. The former is the completion of hardware and environment; the latter is the daily attachment of public transportation.

## After the Tracks Are Built, the City Begins to Fill in the Last Mile

The Ankeng LRT entered the planning process after the Executive Yuan's principal approval in 2013, and officially opened in 2023, spanning ten years. Looking further back at the history of local residents waiting for the metro, the Central News Agency even described the residents' expectations as "nearly 30 years." [^1] [^12] What remains from this period is not only 7.5 kilometers of tracks and 9 stations, but also the process of how a mountain city struggles to be seen by the network.

The contradiction is clear: Ankeng needs tracks, but cannot rely solely on tracks to solve traffic problems. Trains can cross the Xindian River, but cannot climb every hill for each resident. A NT$1.6632 billion public project can connect the city, but still has to face stubborn issues such as bus routes, walking safety, transfer times, and resident habits. [^1] [^4] [^10]

Therefore, the most memorable scene of the Ankeng LRT may not be the ribbon-cutting ceremony on opening day, nor just the 130-meter-high white tower of the Anxin Bridge, but whether someone can smoothly find the next leg of their journey after getting off at K9. When the tracks are no longer just seen as a line from Ankeng to Xintianzhuang, but as a continuous action of walking, waiting, transferring, and returning home within the community, the mountain city LRT finally begins to reach the people it is meant to serve.
