# Bannan Line: The 26.5 Kilometers Stitching Two Cities Together Underground

> **30-second overview:** The Bannan Line is not merely a blue route ferrying passengers across Taipei City. It began as an early concept of "Banqiao to Songshan," underwent route adjustments, undergrounding negotiations, and phased openings, ultimately becoming the 23-station underground high-capacity system from Dingpu to Nangang Exhibition Center. What it shortened was not just a train ride, but a metropolitan area's imagination of "what counts as the same place."

On December 24, 1999, the section from Ximen Station to City Hall Station, plus the Banqiao Line segment from Ximen Station to Longshan Temple Station, began revenue service on the same day.[^1] At that time, the Bannan Line was not yet in its complete form. It first stitched Ximen, Taipei Main Station, Zhongxiao East Road, and the Xinyi Special District into an east-west underground backbone, then pushed the operational terminus to Kunyang the following year.[^1]

On July 6, 2015, the section from Dingpu Station to Yongning Station opened, extending the Bannan Line's western end further into Tucheng.[^2] Today's BL Bannan Line runs from Dingpu Station to Nangang Exhibition Center Station, spanning 26.5 kilometers with 23 stations, all underground.[^3]

What makes this line most worth writing about is how it connected three originally quite different rhythms of life onto a single timetable: the residential commute of Banqiao and Tucheng, the commercial and transfer hub of central Taipei City, and the railway and industrial nodes of Nangang. Station counts are merely the result; what truly changed the city was these rhythms beginning to interlock on the same day.

## Not One Line, But a Repeatedly Revised Urban Choice

The Bannan Line's predecessor was not a fixed blue line map from start to finish. According to the Taipei City Government Department of Rapid Transit Systems (DORTS), the early Blue Line concept ran from Banqiao to Songshan, later evaluated for extension to Nangang, with reasons including serving commuter trips to Songshan, Nangang, and Xizhi, and guiding balanced development of the Taipei metropolitan area.[^1]

This change reveals the side of metro planning most easily overlooked: routes are determined jointly by distance, commuting demand, and urban direction. It simultaneously answers three questions: "who needs to commute," "where needs to be connected," and "where does the city hope to grow." By crossing east past Zhongxiao East Road, the Bannan Line also rewrote Nangang from the city's edge back into the central narrative of metropolitan transportation.

Construction at the time did not occur on blank land. The Nangang Line ran along Zhonghua Road, Zhongxiao West Road, and Zhongxiao East Road, passing through densely populated commercial districts, adjacent to Taipei Main Station, the Executive Yuan, and the Control Yuan, and crossing beneath the North Gate. Officials therefore described this segment as a route with high construction difficulty.[^1]

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The Bannan Line's first critical turning point was not a station opening, but engineers having to find a new path through the interstices of an existing city. The metro does not raze and rebuild the city; it learns to pass underneath when the city refuses to yield.

## Why the Nangang Segment Changed from Elevated to Underground

The Nangang segment most clearly shows how local life and engineering planning pull against each other. Official data indicate the revised Nangang Line was originally planned to transition to elevated at the Dongxin Street tunnel portal, crossing over the Taiwan Railways (TR) main line before proceeding to Nangang. From the late 1970s to early 1990s, this plan changed again due to local proposals and multiple rounds of coordination. The Taipei City Council once proposed undergrounding the approximately 1.75 km section from Xiangyang Road to Nangang Road; subsequently, the Executive Yuan Public Construction Commission ruled that the section east of Houshanpi to Kunyang Station, including Kunyang Station, would be undergrounded.[^1]

Undergrounding looks like a simple aesthetic choice, but actually contains exchanges of land, transportation, and local development. Elevated structures can form visible transport corridors more quickly; undergrounding keeps roads and streetscapes at ground level but increases engineering difficulty and cost. The Nangang segment ultimately being tucked underground means the city chose a less conspicuous way to gain ground-level spatial continuity.

This also explains why the Bannan Line is often hard to see at ground level. On Zhongxiao East Road you see shops, arcades, and traffic, yet can hardly read from the streetscape that a high-capacity railway runs beneath your feet. Its urban effect is an invisible corridor that does not interrupt ground-level life, allowing people to continuously cross administrative districts and commercial zones.

## Two Openings: First Connecting the City Center, Then Pushing the Eastern End to Nangang

The Bannan Line's birth adopted phased opening. On December 24, 1999, the City Hall to Ximen and Ximen to Longshan Temple segments began revenue service first. On December 30, 2000, the City Hall to Kunyang segment followed.[^1] This sequence is interesting because it let the densest east-west axis of the city center start operating first, then pushed the terminus toward Nangang, rather than waiting for the entire line to finish before letting passengers use it.

Phased opening made the Bannan Line a piece of infrastructure citizens learned segment by segment. Passengers first familiarized themselves with movement between Ximen, Taipei Main Station, Zhongxiao Fuxing, and City Hall, only later incorporating Kunyang, Nangang, and the more western Banqiao and Tucheng into the same daily routine. Network effects did not wait for completion day to appear. Each terminus shift added a little more to "places you can conveniently reach en route."

In 2015, the Dingpu to Yongning segment opened, adding 1.9 km to operational length. Taipei Rapid Transit Corporation listed this in its major milestones.[^2] The extension from Banqiao to Tucheng turned the Bannan Line from merely an east-west axis within Taipei City into a long-distance commuting corridor spanning Taipei City and New Taipei City.

## Convenience Does Not Mean Everyone Profits More

The Bannan Line certainly brought passenger flows, but between "increased foot traffic" and "increased commercial district revenue" there is no automatically established equation. Taking the Longshan Temple commercial district as an example, a National Taiwan University master's thesis studying interviewed merchants and consumers found that while respondents generally agreed the metro made transportation more convenient, this convenience did not necessarily convert directly into merchant revenue. The research also noted that walking connections between metro exits and street blocks remain insufficient.[^4]

This gap is important. The metro brings people to a station, but does not mean people will walk into every alley. If clear and comfortable walking paths are lacking between station bodies, exits, temples, old shops, arcades, and markets, travelers may simply transfer underground without truly entering the ground-level commercial district.

The Bannan Line's change to localities truly occurs after flows are redistributed. The question becomes who can catch the crowds, and who can only watch them pass by. The Longshan Temple study reminds us that the metro must work together with commercial district marketing, historic and religious activities, sanitation and safety, and ground-level environmental planning.[^4] A line can bring people, but cannot substitute for a locality deciding how to welcome them.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The metro is most easily misunderstood as a machine pouring crowds into commercial districts. In fact it is more like an amplifier. Whatever alley flows, shop natures, and local memories originally were, they all get amplified after opening.

## Land Beside Stations Begins Learning to Price Itself by the Metro

The Bannan Line also changed how people imagine housing. A study focusing on Bannan Line stations from Longshan Temple to City Hall analyzed passenger entry/exit volumes at each station, surrounding announced land values, and district housing price indices, finding that after the metro appeared, commercial real estate responded more positively than residential properties. The research also noted that station areas may shift toward commercial use due to increased foot traffic, while residential moves toward station peripheries.[^5]

This is not the simple story of "metro opens, housing prices definitely rise." Another policy analysis noted that metro-adjacent real estate price changes often concentrate around opening periods; as the network gradually completes and convenience becomes universal, the original scarcity premium also declines. Metro-adjacent housing is not a guaranteed perpetual appreciation.[^6]

Newer TOD research places this within urban institutions. The research points out that Taipei had already seen land-use regulations equivalent to TOD as early as 1983, with development within 500 meters of major public transit stations eligible for floor area ratio bonuses, and land value increment recapture viewed as a policy tool supporting public transit.[^7]

In other words, the Bannan Line is not just a transportation construction project. It also made land around stations become a venue where public transit, urban renewal, and private development jointly negotiate. Below the station body are platforms; above or beside it may be malls, offices, residences, and walking spaces. The true scope of a subway has never been only inside tunnels.

## A Cultural Facility Disappearing into the Streetscape

The Taipei City DORTS English introduction particularly emphasizes that metro space includes not only stations and trains, but also underground streets, plazas, pedestrian spaces above station bodies, and linear parks under elevated sections. It describes the metro as part of urban life, not merely a transportation tool.[^8]

In the Nangang Line case, official data mention that the Zhongxiao Dunhua Station ventilation shaft and multi-functional underground pedestrian street were transformed into the public art "Tree River." Nangang Line station designs also minimize impact on the urban landscape through simplicity, then create new urban landmarks through public art.[^8]

This design yields a counterintuitive result: a fully underground route continuously changes the visual and walking experience above ground. It does not announce its existence via viaducts, but translates the underground system into ground-level urban life through exits, underground streets, ventilation shafts, signage, artworks, and passenger flows.

During Nangang Line construction, the engineering team also handled historic preservation. The official English page notes that during construction, stone and wooden pillars from the foundations of the old Taipei City Wall were discovered; some components were excavated, restored, and displayed at the Zhongxiao West Road underground street location.[^8] This makes the metro's underground engineering not just about burying a new system, but also bringing material evidence of the old city into new public spaces.

## Nangang Is Not a Terminus, But Another Urban Rhythm

The significance of the Bannan Line heading east cannot be summarized merely as "faster to Nangang." DORTS notes that the Nangang Depot is located near Xiangyang Road and Dongxin Street, providing train stabling, maintenance, and washing functions, with the depot roof reserved for development. The Nangang Line also planned transfer functions with the TR Western Trunk Line at Songshan and Nangang.[^1]

The Nangang East Extension itself was not a one-time completed terminus. The approximately 2.5 km extension from Kunyang to Nangang Station used shield tunneling, with Nangang Station and Nangang Exhibition Center Station, of which approximately 725 meters lie within the TR right-of-way, forming a three-rail co-location with HSR and TR. DORTS, responding to construction schedule and Nangang traffic diversion needs, first opened Kunyang to Nangang Station on December 25, 2008, then opened Nangang to Nangang Exhibition Center Station on February 27, 2011.[^15]

This Nangang, and the commercial streetscape of Zhongxiao Dunhua, are not the same urban rhythm. It connects to railways, depots, exhibitions, and industrial spaces. The Bannan Line puts these different rhythms into a single route passengers can understand, making "going to Nangang" no longer feel like heading to the city's periphery, but more like going to another workplace scene within the same metropolitan area.

_Image: Robert S. Donovan/Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0. Taken at Taipei City Hall Station.[^13]_

The significance of Nangang Exhibition Center Station is not just pushing the Blue Line further east. DORTS lists Bannan Line station architectural designs separately for Nangang Station and Nangang Exhibition Center Station, and places Nangang Exhibition Center and Nangang Software Park in the same urban design context. The station body thus becomes an entrance where industry, exhibitions, and transfers mutually recognize each other, rather than just an underground box responsible only for boarding and alighting.[^16]

> **📝 Curator's Note:** What most deserves attention in the Nangang segment is not "how many stations the terminus extended," but how the opening sequence connected traffic diversion, rail co-location, and exhibition industry to the network at different times. The city's eastern end is therefore not a fixed terminus, but an interface gradually brought into use.

A route becomes a life-circle backbone not because its terminus is bustling, but because its intermediate transfers and stops are stably used. Taipei Main Station stacks railways, another metro line, and the Airport Line at the same underground node; Zhongxiao Fuxing intersects Bannan Line and Wenhu Line commuting directions; Nangang connects metro with TR, exhibition events, and industrial spaces. These nodes give the Bannan Line effects exceeding its own station spacing: passengers may not alight at every station, but can use transfers to fit more distant cities into the same day's schedule.

Taipei Rapid Transit Corporation's ridership statistics include the Bannan Line in high-capacity system statistics. Official data simultaneously provide annual and monthly totals for high-capacity lines, but do not break out Bannan Line single-line figures; therefore this article does not mistakenly write high-capacity totals as Bannan Line single-line ridership.[^9]

This statistical limitation also reminds us that the Bannan Line's importance is not only in single-line ridership rankings. Its role is more like a network backbone, connecting different lines, stations, and life circles through transfers. Calculating how many passengers a line has is certainly important. Understanding how many people a line lets start putting different places into the same day may be closer to its urban value.

> **✦** "What was truly shortened is not just the distance between two stations, but the boundary in people's minds of 'that's not my living range.'"

## Questions the Blue Line Leaves Behind

The Bannan Line's success cannot be written as an urban fairy tale without costs. Research has already pointed out that station-area commercialization may push residences outward, and transportation convenience may also cause land and housing prices to re-stratify.[^5] Commercial districts gain foot traffic, but may also bear pressures of exit circulation, touristification, and repackaging of original local life.[^4]

TOD research further reminds that if public transit and land development lack walking environment, mixed use, and public interest supporting measures, they may become merely "development near transit," not a complete transit-oriented city.[^7] The Bannan Line brought people closer; the city still must answer whether people can safely, comfortably, and with reason walk the last 500 meters.

So the Bannan Line's core question is not "did it change Taipei," but "to whom did it hand the changes." DORTS decided routes and construction methods; the metro corporation maintains headways and station bodies; local commercial districts absorb passenger flows; developers revalue land; passengers vote with daily movement. Everyone holds only a small segment, but the entire blue line is the result these small segments jointly produce.

## The Last 500 Meters Is Where the City Truly Receives People

The Bannan Line delivers people to station entrances; the road after that is still completed by the city itself. Whether exits face markets, whether underground streets clearly connect to arcades, whether the elderly, people with strollers, and those with mobility impairments can avoid an extra detour — these determine whether the metro's convenience truly reaches daily life. The Longshan Temple study already noted that insufficient walking connections between exits and street blocks is precisely one reason transportation convenience did not automatically become commercial district revenue.[^4]

Therefore, the Bannan Line's ground-level engineering deserves to be viewed in the same article as its tunnel engineering. Station-area canopies, pedestrian signals, arcades, public art, underground street connections, and commercial district signage are all the grammar translating "arriving at the station" into "arriving at the place." They do not appear in the colored lines of route maps, yet decide whether a passenger ultimately remembers a neighborhood with character, or just a node suitable for transferring.

Here also lies the Bannan Line's homework for the future. TOD should not be measured only by floor area ratio bonuses, but must also ask whether station areas have diverse uses, continuous walking environments, and public spaces that do not exclude original residents.[^7] The metro brought the city closer; the city still must decide what to keep after proximity.

## Conclusion: The Underground Line, the Shared Daily Life Above Ground

On December 24, 1999, the Bannan Line first connected Ximen, Longshan Temple, and City Hall. In 2000, it reached Kunyang. In 2015, it extended further to Dingpu.[^1] [^2] Each opening was a moment a terminus entered daily life.

Today, passengers on platforms see blue roundels, train doors, and next-station announcements. Fewer people think that this line was once re-debated between elevated and underground, once had to pass beneath the North Gate, and was once expected to balance Taipei metropolitan area development.[^1]

The Bannan Line's counterintuitive nature lies here: the more successful it is, the more it seems not to exist. When Banqiao people can go to Nangang for work in the same day, and Nangang people can go to Longshan Temple for a meal, the city no longer treats this as an engineering miracle, but as a matter of course.

This matter-of-course did not form naturally. The Bannan Line is a line that was planned, fought for, excavated, preserved, and then repeatedly used into existence by daily riders — an underground suture. The Bannan Line did not eliminate differences between Taipei and New Taipei; it merely added a returnable path between differences.

The blue line on the route map has only one color; the life map has many layers. Some alight at Longshan Temple to buy breakfast, some transfer at Zhongxiao Fuxing toward offices, some change to buses at Kunyang, some finish a day's work at Nangang Exhibition Center. The Bannan Line puts these different purposes into the same direction, and the city thereby becomes larger than administrative boundaries in daily back-and-forth. This is the line's quietest yet deepest achievement: it let originally specially arranged cross-city movements gradually become daily routines needing no explanation, and let a metropolitan area's boundaries re-expand between footsteps and trains into a shared experience verifiable every day.
