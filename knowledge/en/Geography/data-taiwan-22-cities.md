# A Data Look at Taiwan's 22 Municipalities: Density Varies by 151 Times, Oldest and Youngest Differ by a Generation

Driving from Xinyi District in Taipei toward the south and east, eventually reaching Taitung City, the navigation shows over three hundred kilometers, less than a day's drive. But if you focus on another metric—how many people live per square kilometer outside your window—that journey feels like crossing two countries. In Taipei City, where Xinyi District is located, there are 8,975 people per square kilometer. In Taitung County, where Taitung City is located, there are only 59 people per square kilometer. On the same island, with the same passport, the density difference is a full 151 times.

This number is not an extreme value trick; it reflects the real disparities within Taiwan. We tend to discuss "Taiwan" as a whole—its economy, its elections, its low birth rate—but when you lay out the complete set of municipal data from the Ministry of the Interior's Civil Affairs Department at the end of 2025, you find there is no homogeneous Taiwan. Some people are queuing for red lights in concrete jungles; some drive for ten minutes without seeing a second car. Some counties are still growing; most municipalities are shrinking. The youngest corners and the oldest corners differ by nearly a generation.


This article aims to do one thing: use official data to paint a verifiable portrait of the entire island. After painting it, you will see a face that is highly differentiated and rapidly aging.[^1]

> **30-Second Summary:** At the end of 2025, Taiwan's total population was 23,299,132 (approx. 23.3 million), having decreased for 23 consecutive months, with newborns falling below 110,000 for the first time. In the same year, Taiwan officially entered a "super-aged society," where one in five people is over 65. However, the national average masks vast internal disparities: the difference between the most and least dense municipalities is 151 times, the population size gap is 297 times, and the aging level differs by nearly a generation. People concentrate in the six metropolitan areas; the front line of aging is not in the cities but in the East, outlying islands, and agricultural counties, with Hsinchu County being the youngest area supported by the Hsinchu Science Park. This is a differentiated Taiwan that is aging together.[^2]

## 70% of People, 30% of Land

First, let's look at a fact that is often overlooked: Taiwanese people are actually very concentrated.

At the end of 2025, the six metropolitan areas (Taipei, New Taipei, Taoyuan, Taichung, Tainan, Kaohsiung) collectively housed 16,278,931 people, accounting for 69.87% of the total population. In other words, nearly seven out of every ten Taiwanese live in these six metropolitan areas. Yet, the land area of these six metropolitan areas only accounts for 30.12% of Taiwan's total area. Seventy percent of the people are crammed into thirty percent of the land. The remaining thirty percent of the people are scattered across the other seventy percent of the land. This is the first structural pattern of Taiwan's population distribution.

```tw-stat
23.3 million | Total Population | Decreasing for 23 consecutive months as of end of 2025
Over 20.06% | Share of population aged 65 and over | Officially crossed the super-aged threshold in 2025
69.87% | Proportion of six metropolitan areas' population to national total | Yet residing on only 30% of the land
```

This 7:3 pattern was not formed naturally; it has a clear institutional starting point. On December 25, 2010, Taiwan rewrote its administrative map: Taipei County was upgraded to New Taipei City; Taichung and Tainan counties merged; Kaohsiung county merged; and the original Taipei City was added, resulting in five metropolitan areas simultaneously. Four years later, on December 25, 2014, Taoyuan was upgraded to the sixth metropolitan area. In just four years, Taiwan changed from having two metropolitan areas to six, causing the balance of resources, budgets, and infrastructure development to tilt accordingly.[^4]

Upgrading was never just about changing a name. The centralized allocation funds, personnel structure, and independent financial resources received by metropolitan areas are far greater than those of ordinary counties. Where construction goes, where job opportunities grow, and where young people move—that is the direction things flow. Institutions drew a line first, and the population followed that line. The concentration in the six metropolitan areas we see today is, to some extent, the result of over a decade following the 2010 reorganization.

The following chart breaks down "where Taiwanese live" even more clearly. New Taipei City alone houses 17.4% of the national population, Taichung at 12.3%, Kaohsiung at 11.7%, Taipei at 10.5%, Taoyuan at 10.1%, and Tainan at 7.9%—the six metropolitan areas account for nearly 70%. The remaining 16 counties, scattered across Taiwan from the north to the outlying islands, only account for 30.1%.


Stare at this waffle chart for a moment, and you will ask one question: what kind of life do the people in that 30% scattered across 70% of the land lead? The answer is hidden in the density.

## From Most Dense to Most Sparse: A Difference of 151 Times

Population concentration addresses "how many people," while density addresses "how crowded." The latter better conveys the distance between two Taiwans.

At 8,975 people per square kilometer, Taipei City is the most densely populated place in Taiwan. Congestion in Taipei is daily: subways are packed during peak hours, waiting for a red light takes two rounds, and houses are built tall and narrow. Immediately following is Hsinchu City at 4,376 people per square kilometer, less than half of Taipei's density. Then comes Keelung City with 2,710 people. Just among these three most dense cities, the density already shows a precipitous decline.

```tw-bars
Taipei City | 8,975 people/km²
Hsinchu City | 4,376 people/km²
Keelung City | 2,710 people/km²
Changhua County | 1,126 people/km²
Hualien County | 68 people/km²
Taitung County | 59 people/km² | The sparsest in Taiwan
Source: Ministry of the Interior's Civil Affairs Department, end of 2025
```

Look to the other end of this bar chart, and the disparity truly becomes apparent. Changhua County at 1,126 people per square kilometer is relatively dense for an agricultural county. Reaching Hualien County, the number drops to 68. At the very bottom, Taitung County has only 59 people per square kilometer. The number of people that can fit in one square kilometer of Taipei is equivalent to what would fill 151 square kilometers of Taitung. This is the true appearance of a 151-fold difference—you drive from Xinyi District toward Taitung and witness the thinning population outside your window firsthand.

A geographical reality supports this. Hualien County, which has the largest area among Taiwan's counties, spans 4,628 square kilometers, almost an entire eastern side of the Central Mountain Range. With a large area, few people, and many mountains, the density in Hualien and Taitung is naturally diluted to the lowest level in Taiwan. The smallest county, Lianchiang County, only has 28.8 square kilometers. Density is half determined by human choice and half by inherent topography.

Common discourse often treats "high population density" as progress and "low density" as backwardness, but this correlation reverses cause and effect. Taitung's low density is not because it cannot develop; it is due to its geographical conditions of vast land and sparse population, backed by mountains and facing the Pacific Ocean. Hualien's "emptiness" holds Taroko Gorge, the Coast Mountain Range, and Taiwan's most complete hinterland scenery. The density number only tells you how crowded people are; it does not tell you the value of that land. When you line up all 22 municipalities in Taiwan on a spectrum from dense to sparse, what you see is different ways of life growing on an island with varied topography.

## Giants and Dust Motes

If density measures "how crowded," population size measures "how large." The disparity in this dimension is even more exaggerated than the density.

The most populous county in Taiwan is New Taipei City, with 4,044,831 people—one city equivalent to many countries. The least populous is Lianchiang County, which is Mazu, with only 13,621 people. The population of one city in New Taipei is 297 times that of Lianchiang. If you moved all the residents of Lianchiang county into New Taipei, you still wouldn't fill even a sliver. Sharing the same Republic of China passport, an entire county to one county is merely a dust mote in the eyes of another.

This scale disparity directly becomes a governance challenge. New Taipei must manage traffic, housing, long-term care, and garbage for four million people; Lianchiang must contemplate how to prevent its 13,000 residents from continuing to leave, how to maintain one hospital, and how to keep the last ferry running. The same laws, the same central policies, manifest completely differently when applied across a 297-fold population gap. When we talk about "local government," our minds often presuppose the scale of an urban area, but Taiwan has quite a few counties operating a complete governmental machine on a dust mote scale.

However, here is an intuitive trap to be careful of: small size does not mean young people. You might think that outlying islands and small counties are sparse because young people migrate out, leaving behind the elderly; or you might think large cities are large and young because they have abundant resources and opportunities. The real data refutes both assumptions. Lianchiang County, with the fewest residents, has an aging rate of 17.14%, which is lower than several larger counties; while Taipei City, which ranks fourth in population and is incredibly prosperous, has the highest aging rate in Taiwan at 24.18%. There is no clean correlation between size and aging. To understand how Taiwan is aging, you must change the map.

## The Front Line of Aging Is Not in the Cities

In 2025, Taiwan crossed a threshold: the national proportion of people aged 65 and over reached 20.06%, officially becoming a "super-aged society" as defined by the World Health Organization. This means that 4.67 million Taiwanese are over 65, with one in five being seniors. Furthermore, 14 out of Taiwan's 22 municipalities have an aging rate exceeding 20%. Aging has become a common backdrop across the island.

But this national average of 20.06% smooths over the dramatic internal differences. The following table lines up the aging rates and aging indices of the 22 municipalities, which is the most crucial chart to study in this article. First, let's explain how to read the "aging index": it equals (population aged 65 and over / population aged 0 to 14) \* 100. A score of 100 means seniors and children are equal; a higher number means the area is more "top-heavy" with more elderly people at the top and fewer children at the bottom.

When reading this table, pay special attention to the Aging Index column. The highest in Taiwan is Chiayi County at 291.69, meaning that in Chiayi County, every child corresponds to nearly three seniors. Close behind are Kinmen County at 255.57 and Keelung City at 240.21. These three places share a common characteristic: they are all agricultural counties, outlying islands, or old ports where industry has moved away; none of them make the list of Taiwan's most prosperous cities. The deepest front line of aging is located in these corners that have long faced out-migration of young people. When young people leave for work or study, and those who remain slowly age while newborns fail to compensate, the aging index shoots up.

So, where is the youngest corner in Taiwan? The answer might surprise many: not in some secluded paradise, but next to a technology park. Hsinchu County has an aging rate of only 15.08%, the lowest in Taiwan; its aging index of 101.88 is almost balanced between seniors and children, standing out amidst the general aging trend in Taiwan. Hsinchu City at 16.16% and Taoyuan City at 16.72% follow closely. The youthfulness of these three places is largely attributed to one reason: the Science Park (Zhuke). The semiconductor and technology industries attract a large number of engineers and technical staff in their prime reproductive years, who settle down and have children here, forcibly pulling the population structure of these counties toward youth. What the Science Park supports is not just Taiwan's export figures, but also Taiwan's youngest demographic map.

Putting the youngest and oldest extremes together makes the disparity more striking.

```tw-versus
Hsinchu County (Youngest in Taiwan)| Chiayi County (Oldest in Taiwan)
65+ Share 15.08% | 65+ Share 24.11%
Aging Index 101.88 | Aging Index 291.69
Population Still Growing | Population Continues to Decline

```

Hsinchu County's 15.08% compared to Chiayi County's 24.11% shows a 9 percentage point difference in aging rate; and an aging index of 101.88 versus 291.69 is nearly three times the difference. One county is still growing, with people flowing in; another is continuously declining, with people flowing out. These two counties on the same island have a generation's difference in aging levels. In a park in Hsinchu, you might see young parents pushing strollers; in the countryside of Chiayi, an entire street might be filled with seniors walking slowly. Both these types of Taiwan are calculated by the Civil Affairs Department's data.

Here we must dispel a popular misconception: many people think aging is exclusive to rural areas and outlying islands, and that large cities, due to more young people and opportunities, should be immune to aging. The data says this is not true. Taipei City's aging rate of 24.18% is the highest in Taiwan; its aging index of 202.06 is also the highest among the six metropolitan areas. This most prosperous and resource-concentrated city in Taiwan is also one of the oldest among the six metropolitan areas. The reason is not hard to understand: housing prices in Taipei are high, forcing young families to settle in New Taipei or Taoyuan to start families; those who remain in the urban core are generations who settled early and are now gradually aging. Urban prosperity cannot stop aging; it simply ages in another way. Aging is not a disease of certain counties; it is a common condition of the entire island, just with different speeds.[^5]

## One Island, Aging Together

Zooming out from counties to the nation, Taiwan's story of aging has another dimension that warrants caution: speed.

Taiwan did not start aging recently. In 1993, the national proportion of people aged 65 and over exceeded 7%, entering a "aging society" according to international definitions. In 2018, this figure reached 14.05%, entering an "aged society." In 2025, it surpassed 20%, becoming a "super-aged society." These three milestones look like just three years, but what is hidden within them is a downward slope that accelerates.

```tw-timeline
1993 | Aging Society | 65+ exceeds 7%; Taiwan begins to age
2018 | Aged Society | 65+ reaches 14%; took 25 years from the previous stage
2025 | Super-Aged Society | 65+ surpasses 20%; this stage only took 7 years
```

Look at the two intervals on this timeline. It took Taiwan 25 years to go from 7% to 14%; but it only took 7 years to go from 14% to 20%. The latter segment took less than a third of the time of the former. Taiwan is aging, and it is doing so faster and faster. This is considered rapid in many countries; other nations have decades to gradually adjust their long-term care, pension, and medical systems, but Taiwan was compressed into just seven years, forced to build its entire elderly care system within a short window.

```tw-line
Rise of the National Proportion of People Aged 65+ (%)
Year | 65+ Share
2000 | 8.6
2010 | 10.7
2020 | 16.1
2025 | 20.06
Source: Ministry of the Interior and National Development Council
```

This rising line makes the acceleration clearer. In 2000, the national proportion aged 65 and over was 8.6%; in 2010, it was 10.7%, an increase of just over two percentage points in ten years; but from 2010 to 2020, the number jumped from 10.7% to 16.1%, and by 2025, it reached 20.06%. The tail of the line is clearly steeper than the head. Taiwan's aging curve is curving upward.

On the other side of aging is the collapse of births. In 2025, the number of newborns in Taiwan fell below 110,000 for the first time, at only 107,812. Low birth rates and aging are two sides of the same coin: more seniors accumulate at the top while fewer children compensate at the bottom, causing the entire population structure to become increasingly top-heavy. This is why the aging indices in those counties mentioned earlier were so high: more seniors on top, too few children below.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> We easily understand "population decline" as "young people moving from rural areas to cities, causing rural areas to shrink and urban areas to grow," treating it like an internal relocation within the island. But the 2025 data reveals a more fundamental truth: the "natural increase" in every county across Taiwan is negative. This means that in every municipality in Taiwan, regardless of whether it is urban or rural, large or small, the number of deaths exceeds the number of births. This is no longer an issue of where people move; it is the entire island suffering from a state of decline. In-migration and out-migration are merely redistributing a population that was already shrinking; they do not create any new people.

This fact demands careful consideration. In 2025, only four counties in Taiwan were still growing: Taoyuan, Hsinchu County, Taichung, and Hsinchu City. And their growth is entirely due to "social increase," meaning the influx of people from outside—relying on drawing in residents from other counties, not on having children themselves. Apart from these four, the population of the remaining 18 counties has all decreased. The counties with the largest decreases are Kinmen County, Lianchiang County, and Taipei City; note that even the most prosperous Taipei City is on this list of decline. When births in every corner of the island cannot compensate for deaths, those four growing counties are merely temporarily standing on the decline of others.

This is why the total population has been decreasing for 23 consecutive months, falling to 23,299,132 people. It is a synchronous contraction of the entire island; every county is shrinking. The only difference is that some places manage to sustain themselves temporarily through in-migration, while others do not even have this buffer.

## A Portrait Drawn by Data

Returning to the initial journey. From Xinyi District in Taipei to Taitung, the population per square kilometer drops from 8,975 to 59, like crossing two countries. Now you know that it is not just a density gap. Along that road, the scale of the population changes from giant to dust mote; aging shifts from the youth supported by the Science Park to the front line of aging in agricultural counties. The only constant is that no matter which county you stop at, local deaths have exceeded births.

This is the portrait of Taiwan in 2025: a highly differentiated and collectively aging face. Its internal disparities are astonishing—a density gap of 151 times, a scale gap of 297 times, and an aging difference of nearly a generation; yet its fundamental predicament is remarkably consistent: the entire island suffers from decline, the total population has shrunk for 23 consecutive months, and it rushed from an aging society to a super-aged society in seven years. The National Development Council estimates that, according to their projections, Taiwan's total population will be only 14.97 million by 2070, with the proportion aged 65 and over reaching 46.5%; and the demographic dividend will end in 2028. This portrait will not improve on its own.[^3]

Admitting this internal heterogeneity is necessary to see the island's true challenges: a place with such vast internal differences must implement policies that simultaneously care for the four million people crammed into the basin and the thirteen thousand people guarding the outlying islands, while also dealing with the youngest Hsinchu and the oldest Chiayi. It is differentiated, but it must act as a whole; it is aging, but it must find solutions together. The data has painted this face for us; how to respond to this face is a question that the island must answer together.
