## 30-Second Overview

Before gold mining, Jiufen was not an empty town "waiting to be discovered." Local records from the Ministry of Transportation and Communications' Tourism Administration indicate that it was originally engaged in camphor processing; the name "Jiufen" is related to a division method for camphor furnaces. After gold veins were discovered around 1890, population and capital rapidly concentrated on the hillside, forming a new settlement scale.[^10]

Jiufen’s second turning point occurred after mining ceased. In 1971, gold mining officially ended in Jiufen, and the Shengping Theater closed amidst pressures from television, video tapes, population outflow, and operational losses. The release of _A City of Sadness_ (悲情城市) in 1989 brought Jiufen's staircases, mist, and teahouses into the view of film audiences. Subsequent preservation efforts did not restore it as a mining town but rather narrated together the theater, mining archives, teahouse culture, and consumer tourism.[^1] [^2] [^6] [^11]

## A Timeline That Only Leads to Gold

It is best to read Jiufen’s history through its industrial changes, rather than by looking at the shop signs on the old street today. The timeline below juxtaposes "gold mining," "screening," and "preservation," showing that Jiufen was not transformed once, but rather taken over by different forces multiple times.

| Period       | Local Change                                                                             | Remaining Space or Data                                                                   |
| :----------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Approx. 1890 | Gold veins discovered; gold-mining population rapidly gathered                           | Fushan Temple Land Shrine, mining memories in the Xiaojin Gua area[^10]                   |
| 1893–1896    | Jiufen and Jinguashi acquired mining rights; industrialization of mining                 | Mining rights of Jiufen, mining rights of Jinguashi, and different extraction methods[^2] |
| 1931         | Shengping Theater completed, accommodating 543 spectators                                | Two-story theater, theatrical and film screening functions[^11]                           |
| 1957–1971    | Jiufen mines decline; official cessation in 1971                                         | Population outflow, breakdown of commercial networks[^1] [^2]                             |
| 1989         | _A City of Sadness_ released; Jiufen imagery enters international film circulation       | Shuziq Road, Shengping Theater, teahouses, and mining scenery[^6]                         |
| 2009–2010    | Shengping Theater achieves preservation turning point, designated as a memorial building | Old street cultural facilities and restoration plans[^11]                                 |
| 2010–2020    | Shengping Theater transforms into an exhibition, display, and local cultural space       | Records of local figures, old theater exhibitions, participatory proposals[^11]           |

This table also reminds us that Jiufen is not as simple as "mining ends, film takes over." The theater was built before the mining decline, and the theater closed after the gold mining peak. Before film captured Jiufen as a historical setting, the Shengping Theater itself had already undergone changes from an entertainment venue to a cinema and a space of community memory.[^11]

## Before Gold Mining: Camphor Furnaces and Place Names

The official scenic spot page on the Ministry of Transportation and Communications' Tourism Administration states that before the gold boom, Jiufen was engaged in camphor processing. There were ninety camphor furnaces locally, with ten forming one unit, leading to the naming convention "Jiufen." This is a narrative of local history from the tourism page and cannot be taken as the sole etymological conclusion, but it at least corrects the simplified view that "Jiufen only appeared in 1890."[^10]

Around 1890, residents discovered gold veins near Fushan Temple Land Shrine in Xiaojin Gua, leading to a rapid gathering of gold-mining populations. Official data estimates that the village once housed three or four thousand mining households. This figure describes a rapidly expanding mining settlement over a short period, not equivalent to the long-term population today.[^10]

The history of mining in the National Taiwan University Digital Repository places this turning point within a longer regional context: alluvial gold was found in Keelung River in 1890, gold veins were discovered in Jiufen in 1893, and mineral deposits were found in Jinguashi Main Mountain in 1894. Gold miners did not suddenly build a city in the mountains but gradually moved along rivers, mineral veins, and transportation lines.[^2]

In 1896, mining regulations for Taiwan were promulgated during Japanese rule. The Jiufen mining rights were acquired by Fujita Zenzaburō, while Jinguashi was acquired by Tanaka Chōbei. These two adjacent areas began to be influenced by different management and investment methods from this time onward.[^2]

The gold veins in Jiufen are mostly found in relatively brittle sandstone, with gold appearing as leaf-like or branch-like structures within quartz veins. The gold in Jinguashi is often generated in hard intrusive andesite; the latter adopted large-scale direct mining, while Jiufen developed a system of zoned contracting for extraction. It is convenient to call both places "Jinjiu" (Gold Nine), but a convenient route name should not replace two different industrial histories.[^2] [^3]

## The Slope Is Not a Backdrop; It Is a Labor Route

Miners, contractors, shopkeepers, theater troupes, and mining families all occupied the limited hillside, forcing houses to be stacked according to the terrain. Staircases connected mine entrances, residences, shops, and theaters; the streets were first routes of work and life, later becoming lines of sight for tourists taking photos. Data from NTU considers the narrow land, dense population, slope-based construction, and concentration of mining populations as key factors in forming the Jiufen mountain town settlement.[^2]

This type of settlement cannot be represented by a single building. Teahouses, theaters, general stores, mine shafts, and dormitories each corresponded to different living needs. If only the facade of the most easily filmed teahouse is preserved without addressing the tunnels, hospitals, schools, transportation, and residents' memories, what is saved is an exterior, not a settlement.

The differences between Jiufen and Jinguashi are also evident here. When the National Development Council Archives organized data on Jinguashi, they retained not only gold production but also tunnel maps, hospital medical records, student dormitory applications, railway timetables, and company articles of association. These documents reconstruct mining as a system for organizing work, seeking medical care, attending school, and commuting.[^1]

## Shengping Theater: From 543 Seats to Local Cultural Space

The Shengping Theater was not granted cultural value only after _A City of Sadness_ was filmed. Articles from the Ministry of Culture state that its predecessor, "Shengping Hall," was completed in 1931 as a two-story building combining theater and film screening; the ground floor is brick, and the second floor is mainly wood, accommodating 543 spectators. It was first an entertainment facility for the mining settlement, later becoming a preserved historical building.[^11]

The period from the 1950s to the 1960s was the theater's heyday. The decline of gold mining, population outflow, and changes in the investment environment made operations gradually difficult. As the film industry matured in the 1970s, the theater temporarily transformed into a cinema before eventually closing due to the influence of television, video tapes, and new media losses. This thread is more concrete than "the old theater witnessing prosperity and decline" because it points out that audiences, media, and industry changed simultaneously.[^11]

After the theater closed, the building was nearly demolished. Articles from the Ministry of Culture state that in 2009, local preservation efforts began; in 2010, Shengping Theater was designated as a memorial building, and surrounding spatial creation plans were proposed. It has since been managed by the New Taipei City Gold Museum, gradually transforming into a cultural exhibition and performance space.[^11]

Between 2010 and 2020, the Gold Museum interviewed Jiufen residents through "the excavation and recording of mining stories," placing local figures' stories in the theater, and providing participatory budget plans for locals to propose uses for the space. This approach changed the viewing relationship with the theater: tourists not only see an old building but also see who provides the story and who determines the exhibition content.[^11]

Today, walking into the Shengping Theater, one can see antique projectors, film promotional tricycles, posters, ticket windows, and replica general stores. These objects are not a complete picture of the mining era, but evidence selected by later curators. Exhibitions help people understand history, but they also arrange the order of history; therefore, the exhibits must still be cross-referenced with residents' memories and mining archives.[^11]

## Gold Exits, Film Enters

Jiufen began to decline after 1957, with gold mining officially ending in 1971. The mining in Jinguashi continued until the cessation of the Taiwan Mining Company in 1987; the exit times for the two areas were different.[^1] [^2]

After mining ended, what disappeared first was not the houses, but the people who served them. Miners no longer came and went daily; the theater lost regular audiences; shops lost steady customers; contractors and families gradually left. Jiufen's later "decline" was not a sudden uglification of the scenery, but the breakdown of the working network that supported the landscape.

In the 1980s, New Taiwanese Cinema began using Jiufen and Jinguashi as story settings. Wu Nian-zhen once encouraged friends to film in Jiufen; films such as _A Day at the Beach_, _A City of Sadness_, and _Duosang_ brought the mountain town's slopes, mining hospitals, old houses, and theaters onto the screen.[^6] [^7]

_A City of Sadness_, released in 1989, set against the backdrop of regime change and the 228 Incident, captured the mist, staircases, and houses of Jiufen with long takes by Hou Hsiao-hsien, winning the Golden Lion at the Venice Film Festival. The film did not fully document Jiufen's industrial history; it chose the street corners capable of carrying a story and brought those corners to audiences far from Ruifang.[^6]

In an interview in _Taiwan Guanghua_, Wu Nian-zhen said that Jiufen, after its decline, was still beautiful, "like a gray-haired auntie." He also compared later overdevelopment to applying "powder and rouge" to this auntie. Looking at these two metaphors together, the discussion is not about rejecting tourism, but whether the content of life remains visible after the local appearance has become brighter.[^7]

## The Rumor of _Spirited Away_ and Local Imagery

The connection between Jiufen and _Spirited Away_ is one of the most common misplacements in tourist narratives. Many travel writings treat the red lantern street scenes and bathhouses as evidence of filming locations, but _City Studies_ points out that Studio Ghibli and Hayao Miyazaki have both denied that Jiufen was a filming location.[^6]

This rumor deserves to be included not because it is more interesting than mining history, but because it demonstrates how a place can be reassembled by images. Real Jiufen has its own slopes, theaters, and mining history; yet visitors may arrive with the imagery of another film. When error becomes travel expectation, the locality must exert effort to separate "similarity" from "filming."

## Teahouses and Food: Tourism Is Not Just Selling an Old Street Photo

The teahouse culture of Jiufen did not appear after the films, nor can it be simplified into the three characters "ancient flavor." Han Xiu-li's 2012 study analyzed Jiufen’s history, food culture, and tourism environment through in-depth interviews and literature review, pointing out that local cultural industry is composed of historical landscapes, life memories, and traditional cultural products, with teahouse culture being integrated with Jiufen's living scenery and rhythm.[^12]

This study also pointed out that the shops on Jiufen Old Street formed a commercial association, gradually stabilizing the business structure. This indicates that the tourism transformation was not solely caused by external visitors; local businesses were also establishing a shared commercial space. The question is whether individual residents and non-commercial memories still have a place to be seen when the common brand of the commercial area becomes clearer.[^12]

Therefore, Jiufen's food is worth writing as an industrial relationship, not just a legend about snack origins. Teahouses, desserts, taro balls (yuanyuan), _caozai guo_ (a type of rice cake), and souvenirs can all be part of the local economy, but without reliable shop history or research sources, one should not create a "passed down since ancient times" story for a single food item.

## Concrete Conflicts Between Preservation and Tourism

The Gold Museum, which opened in November 2004, preserves the Jinguashi mining heritage under the concept of an ecological museum. Records from the Ministry of Culture state that the core area is about 4.5 hectares, restoring seven types of sites—Japanese dormitories, mining offices, canteens, and Taizi Hotel—and one mine shaft, while continuously conducting oral interviews, cultural asset research, and educational promotion.[^4]

The Ministry of Culture's "Recreating Historical Sites" project directly lists the challenges of Shui-Jin-Jiu: mining pollution persists, land owners and residents have differing visions for development, and preservation and revitalization cannot rely solely on repairing buildings. The project proposes advancing from directions such as local needs, community participation, historical space reuse, and enhancing mining value.[^5]

A 2024 study in the _International Journal of Heritage Studies_ interviewed 18 residents and 16 tourists, compiling four interconnected themes: memory, collective identity, sense of belonging, and mass tourism. The study also found that residents and tourists have inconsistent understandings of the best cultural landscape. This result shows that "Jiufen is very commercialized" is still too coarse; the real difference lies in who frames which part of life as scenery, and who bears the daily costs brought by tourism.[^8]

## Today's Problem in Jiufen Is Not Whether It Should Be Seen

Jiufen has been seen many times: gold miners saw veins, miners saw work, theater audiences saw the screen, film audiences saw the historical atmosphere, tourists saw teahouses and mountains/sea, and residents see transportation, rent, garbage, noise, and livelihood. None of these perspectives can represent Jiufen alone.

What truly needs to be maintained is the gap between the data. The discovery of gold veins in 1890, the Shengping Hall in 1931, the cessation in 1971, _A City of Sadness_ in 1989, the designation as a memorial building in 2010, and today's teahouses and commercial areas cannot be compressed into a smooth story from "mine to scenic spot." Each turning point changed who could use this hillside and what would be preserved.

If Jiufen’s preservation only restores the most camera-friendly street scenes, it loses the mining system, labor, pollution, population outflow, and local organizations. If residents' lives are only treated as tourist material, the inhabitants also become objects of observation again. The experience of preserving the Shengping Theater shows that local figure interviews, performances, artifacts, and visitor viewing can coexist, provided that the exhibition content explains its source and allows locals to participate in how the space is used.[^5] [^11]
