# Kaohsiung: In 1979, the Year It Became a Special Municipality, Eight Kaohsiung Natives Were Locked Up in Taipei

> **30-Second Overview:** On July 1, 1979, Kaohsiung became Taiwan's second special municipality, twelve years after Taipei's upgrade in 1967. That same year, on December 10, International Human Rights Day, a rally organized by Formosa Magazine at Dagangpu Roundabout turned into a clash under tear gas. On April 18, 1980, a military tribunal handed down its verdict: Shih Ming-te received a life sentence; Huang Hsin-chieh, fourteen years; and Yao Chia-wen, Chang Chun-hung, Lin Yi-hsiung, Lu Hsiu-lien, Chen Chu, and Lin Hung-hsuan each received twelve years. In the early morning of February 28, 1980, at the Lin family home at No. 16, Lane 31, Section 3, Xinyi Road, Taipei, Lin Yi-hsiung's mother and twin daughters were murdered. All three wounds trace back to Kaohsiung, the special municipality that had just been upgraded. 2.71 million people are spread across the administrative coordinates of one special municipality's 38 districts — sandbars, Bunun villages, steel-mill smokestacks, and aluminum-alloy roofs, all sharing the same address. What this article wants to say is this: Kaohsiung is both a wound in Taiwan's democracy and the physical mass of this island's industrialization.

## 4:30 AM, Qianzhen Fishing Port

If you ask someone from Kaohsiung when the city is at its most captivating, they won't tell you about the Liuhe Night Market (that one's for tourists). They might tell you about Qianzhen Fishing Port at 4:30 in the morning, or the Gushan ferry at five.

Qianzhen Fishing Port sits in Qianzhen District, the largest deep-sea fishing base in Taiwan. Longline tuna boats set out from here for the Indian Ocean, the Atlantic, the South Pacific, and don't return for six months to a year. Most of the crew are Indonesian or Filipino, their speech a mix of Hokkien, English, and their own mother tongues. During the days when catches are unloaded, the port fills with frozen tuna, fish bigger than a person, carried one by one onto the auction floor. The tuna a Taipei sushi restaurant hand-presses tonight may have been hooked off the coast of Madagascar two weeks earlier.

The Gushan ferry runs every three minutes, Gushan to Qijin, a five-minute crossing, NT$40 a ticket. The first boat leaves at six, but commuters are already lining up before dawn. Qijin's 28,000 residents have no bridge to the main island (the Kaohsiung Harbor Tunnel is for vehicle traffic only) — the ferry is this sandbar's only link to downtown Kaohsiung.

This is the most contemporary proof that Kaohsiung is a port city. The historical glory of the 1979 municipality upgrade, the industrial glory of China Steel's founding in 1971, the shipping glory of seven straight years, 1993 to 1999, as the world's third-busiest container port — all of that is in the past now. But **while the capital is still asleep, Kaohsiung has already started moving goods for the entire world**.

## Takau, Takao, Kaohsiung: The 1920 Renaming

Four hundred years ago, at the foot of Takau Mountain (today's Mount Shou), there was a Makatao settlement ringed by bamboo groves. In a Hokkien approximation, the people called it "Takau" (打狗), meaning "bamboo grove"[^1]. Han settlers arrived in growing numbers after the Qing court established Fengshan County in 1684 — first a fishing village, later a port.

After the 1858 Treaty of Tianjin, Takau opened as a treaty port in 1863. The British consulate was set up at Shaochuantou (today's Gushan), and alongside Xiamen and Fuzhou it became one of the ports the Qing court opened to the foreign powers. In 1899 (Meiji 32), Gotō Shinpei, the fourth Director-General of Civil Affairs under Japanese rule, toured southern Taiwan and decided to develop Takau Harbor. Funding fell short, and the first phase of harbor construction didn't formally begin until 1908[^2].

In October 1920, Den Kenjiro, the eighth Governor-General under Japanese rule, restructured Taiwan's local administration — abolishing the old ting (廳) divisions for shū (州) prefectures — and renamed "Takau Street" to "**Kaohsiung Street**." The characters "高雄" were chosen to approximate the Japanese reading "takao"; whether this has any connection to Mount Takao near Kyoto is still debated among scholars[^1]. But from that moment on, the city was no longer called Takau.

In 1924 (Taisho 13), Kaohsiung Street was upgraded to "Kaohsiung City," with its municipal office at what is now the site of Daitian Temple on Gubo Street (Yancheng District). The population at the city's founding exceeded thirty-five thousand, growing to 160,000 by 1940 — Taiwan's second-largest city, after Taipei[^1]. Modernization under Japanese rule was concentrated in Hamasen (a Taiwanese Hokkien transliteration of the Japanese "hama-sen," or "shoreline"); Yancheng, Gushan, Zuoying, and Lingya were developed one after another, with the municipal office, banks, and trading companies clustered in Hamasen and Yancheng.

> **📝 Curator's note:** Most people in Kaohsiung no longer use the name "Takau" today, but it is buried in the texture of place names and food. "Mount Shou," the modern name for Takau Mountain, was adopted after Crown Prince Hirohito's visit in 1923, during the Japanese colonial period; "Mount Chai," the name commonly used today for the mountain's macaques, is just another folk name for the same peak. The sound "Takau" survives in early historical records from the Tainan-to-Kaohsiung region, in the oral memory of the Makatao people from before their sinicization, and in a wave of cultural workers since the 2000s who have adopted "Takau" as a brand name (the Takau Historical and Cultural Association, the Takau Kaohsiung History and Culture Network) — a pushback against the Taisho-era renaming. **It took a century to rename a city once. Renaming it back may take another century.**

## The Army Came Down From Mount Shou in March 1947

After the events of February 28, 1947 in Taipei broke out, the unrest spread to Kaohsiung in early March. The Kaohsiung Fortress Commander at the time was Peng Meng-chi (1908–1997), a native of Wuchang, Hubei, and an Army lieutenant general stationed on Mount Shou.

"As early as March 4, Peng Meng-chi, commander of the Kaohsiung Fortress Headquarters, had already decided to carry out military suppression"[^3]. On the morning of March 6, seven negotiators — including Kaohsiung Mayor Huang Chung-tu and City Council Speaker Peng Ching-kao (father of surgeon Peng Ming-min) — went up to Mount Shou as agreed, to negotiate a peaceful resolution with Peng Meng-chi. "No sooner had the group entered the meeting room than Tu Kuang-ming, Fan Tsang-jung, and Tseng Feng-ming were arrested"[^3].

"Having seized the negotiators, Peng Meng-chi decided to move up the suppression planned for the 7th, sending troops down the mountain that same afternoon at two o'clock"[^3].

What happened after two o'clock that afternoon was recorded in these passages:

"Troops surrounded the Kaohsiung Railway Station from Sankuaicuo; the crowds and travelers in front of the station scattered in panic, and the troops also opened fire on travelers hiding in the underpass, causing countless casualties"[^3].

"The troops attacking City Hall threw grenades first and killed anyone in sight. Representatives from all walks of life and residents who were waiting in front of City Hall for word on the negotiations… suffered heavy casualties, with roughly fifty or sixty people killed"[^3].

The defense of Kaohsiung Senior High School was another face of this massacre. "On March 4, students formed a self-defense corps (the Kaohsiung Senior High Self-Defense Corps), led by Taiwan Provincial Kaohsiung First Senior High School (today's Kaohsiung Senior High)… its purpose was to maintain order near the school, and to use the school as a temporary shelter to protect mainlanders whose lives were under threat"[^3]. A group of high school students sheltered their mainlander neighbors who had come under attack, using their textbooks as shields against the troops.

The three negotiators, Tu Kuang-ming, Fan Tsang-jung, and Tseng Feng-ming, were executed by firing squad at the Kaohsiung Fortress on March 9. Peng Meng-chi was later promoted and put to greater use by Chiang Kai-shek, rising to Commander-in-Chief of the Army in 1957 and to the rank of General of the Army in 1966. His nickname in 1947 Kaohsiung was "the Butcher of Kaohsiung." The very same man kept climbing through Taiwan's military and political hierarchy afterward.

This was a wound Kaohsiung already carried, long before the 1979 Formosa Incident.

## 1968 Export Processing Zone, 1971 China Steel, 1973 Linyuan Petrochemicals

Twenty years passed, and by the late 1960s, Kaohsiung had become a different city altogether.

"In December 1966, Taiwan passed the Statute for the Establishment of Export Processing Zones, designating Qianzhen District in the Port of Kaohsiung area as the site of the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone"[^4]. This was Taiwan's first, and one of the world's earliest, export processing zones, combining the functions of a free trade zone and an industrial park: tax incentives, a single window for approvals, fast customs clearance. The first factory moved in in 1968, employing mostly young female workers from Tainan, Pingtung, and Chiayi, at a monthly wage of just over NT$1,000, housed four or eight to a dormitory room. That generation later came to be called the "EPZ aunties" — today their grandchildren work at TSMC.

The oil refinery came even earlier. CPC's Kaohsiung Refinery sits at the foot of Banping Mountain in Nanzi District, covering 262 hectares; it was originally the "Kaohsiung Plant of the Imperial Japanese Navy's Sixth Fuel Factory" during World War II, taken over, repaired, and put back into operation by CPC in 1947. "The first naphtha cracker (No. 1 Naphtha Cracker) came online in 1968 and closed in 1990; the second (No. 2 Naphtha Cracker) came online in 1975 and closed in 1994; the fifth (No. 5 Naphtha Cracker) came online in 1994 and closed in 2015"[^5]. Over time the refinery "housed more than forty large-scale plants, including the No. 1, No. 2, and No. 5 crackers, and became officially recognized as the most polluted area in the country"[^5].

On December 3, 1971, China Steel Corporation was formally established[^6]. The 1973 oil crisis sent the global economy into a tailspin, and Chiang Ching-kuo responded by proposing the Ten Major Construction Projects, folding China Steel into that plan. The first phase of construction was completed in 1977, and the plant began producing steel. China Steel is still in Xiaogang today, producing roughly ten million metric tons of crude steel a year, with steel plate, bar and rod, hot-rolled, and cold-rolled products as its main lines[^6]. The accompanying Linyuan Petrochemical Zone was built in 1973; together with the Nanzi refinery and China Steel in Xiaogang, these were the three heaviest gears of postwar Taiwan's heavy industry.

In 1965, the government issued regulations to encourage the import of old ships for scrapping, and shipbreaking began in the Qianzhen and Caoya area. "About 20,000 workers a day dismantled ships at the docks, breaking down as much as 10,000 tons of steel plate daily on average, earning Taiwan the title of the world's number-one 'shipbreaking kingdom'"[^7]. In 1973, Kaohsiung set the world record for shipbreaking volume. In 1986, a tanker called the Kanari exploded in the Darenkong shipbreaking industrial zone due to residual oil in its hold, killing 16 people and injuring more than a hundred. "In 1989 the Kaohsiung Harbor Bureau reclaimed the Darenkong shipbreaking zone and converted it into the Fifth Container Center. In 1990, the shipbreaking industry was formally forced to exit"[^7]. An industry that had led the world simply vanished within twenty years.

> **✦** "**Over time it housed more than forty large-scale plants, including the No. 1, No. 2, and No. 5 naphtha crackers, and became officially recognized as the most polluted area in the country.**" (The Reporter, _The Undying Petrochemical Zombie_[^5])

Kaohsiung people's memory of this period is deeply split. From 1971 through the 1980s, heavy industry was Kaohsiung's pride, and its paycheck. But during that same stretch, residents of Houjing began noticing an unusually thick layer of ash settling on their roofs, and Dalinpu fishermen began noticing fewer fish in their nets. Then came 1987.

## The Houjing Vote: 61 to 39

"In June 1987, CPC's plan for the 'Fifth Naphtha Cracker' was formally announced in the media"[^8]. When Houjing residents learned the government intended to build a fifth cracker on top of the existing No. 1 and No. 2 plants, they organized immediately.

"On August 5, 1987, a village assembly was convened, establishing the 'Self-Help Committee Against the Fifth Naphtha Cracker'"[^8]. The long siege of the plant began that August, with residents taking shifts guarding CPC's gate to block construction vehicles from entering. This movement coincided with that year's lifting of martial law, and was postwar Taiwan's first large-scale environmental protest.

"On May 6, 1990, Taiwan held its first referendum on the construction of a naphtha cracker. Result: turnout was 66%, and those firmly opposed to the fifth cracker won with 4,499 votes (61%) against 2,900 votes for those willing to negotiate"[^8].

The referendum result did not stop the fifth cracker.

"On September 14, 1990, Premier Hau Pei-tsun promised that CPC would relocate the plant after 25 years, and gave Houjing residents a NT$1.5 billion compensation fund — a record at the time for anti-pollution compensation in Taiwan"[^8]. Eight days later, "on September 22, 1990, Minister of Economic Affairs Hsiao Wan-chang announced that construction on the fifth cracker would begin"[^8].

Twenty-five years after Hau Pei-tsun's promise was exactly 2015. **The fifth cracker really did shut down on November 1, 2015** — the promise was kept — but an entire generation of Houjing residents grew up breathing the fifth cracker's air. From the siege beginning in 1987 to the shutdown in 2015, this movement is known as "the 28-year anti-fifth-cracker movement"[^8].

> **📝 Curator's note:** The conventional narrative of environmental movements tends to focus on partisan framing like "green governance." But the story of Houjing's fight against the fifth cracker looks more like a different structure: **these 28 years are the story of a small village trading 25 years of patience for one factory's closure**. There was no single moment of victory in between — every year was a year of losing. The fifth cracker still got built, the emissions still got released, residents' health still suffered. Only in the 25th year of losing did the plant finally close. What most distinguishes Taiwan's environmental movements from its other social movements is their time scale — measured in generations, not terms of office. After the Houjing case, Dalinpu spent more than 30 years fighting for resettlement[^9], before Premier Lin Chuan finally issued a public apology there in 2016. **Winning was never about one movement winning once — it's about the next generation not having to breathe the same air.**

## Tear Gas at Dagangpu Roundabout, on International Human Rights Day

Back to 1979.

On July 1, "Kaohsiung City, formerly a provincial city under Taiwan Province, absorbed Xiaogang Township from Kaohsiung County and was simultaneously upgraded to a special municipality, becoming Taiwan's second special municipality"[^10]. The first special municipality was Taipei City, upgraded in 1967. Kaohsiung was the second, twelve years later. At the time of the upgrade it comprised 11 districts (including the newly created Xiaogang District), with a population of roughly 1.3 million — the largest industrial center in southern Taiwan at the time.

In that same year, 1979: in January, the United States severed diplomatic relations with the Republic of China. On July 1, Kaohsiung was upgraded. In August, _Formosa Magazine_ was launched, with Huang Hsin-chieh as publisher, Hsu Hsin-liang as president, and Shih Ming-te as general manager. The magazine became the flagship of the tangwai (outside-the-party) movement. In early December, tangwai activists, under the name of the "Human Rights Day Commemoration Committee," applied to hold a rally and march in Kaohsiung on December 10 — United Nations International Human Rights Day — demanding an end to martial law and the release of political prisoners. The authorities did not approve the application.

On the evening of December 10, "because the originally planned venue, Rotary Park, had already been sealed off, the tangwai activists decided to head instead for the large roundabout at the junction of Zhongshan and Zhongzheng Roads"[^11]. The rally first gathered near the Xinxing Roundabout on Zhongshan 1st Road in Xinxing District, then moved to Dagangpu Roundabout, at the time one of downtown Kaohsiung's most important traffic circles.

"The crowd taking part in the march was surrounded near Dagangpu Roundabout by thousands of riot police; under intense searchlights, tear gas, and advancing shields, a clash between the two sides finally broke out"[^12].

The clash continued deep into the night. Three days later, at six in the morning on December 13, the Taiwan Garrison Command launched island-wide mass arrests. Chen Chu, Lin Yi-hsiung, Lu Hsiu-lien, and Chang Chun-hung were arrested one after another that morning. Huang Hsin-chieh was arrested at the Legislative Yuan on the morning of December 14. Shih Ming-te evaded capture for 26 days and was arrested at a private residence on January 8, 1980. A total of 152 people were implicated.

> **✦** "**On April 18, the military tribunal handed down its final verdict: all 8 defendants were found guilty. Shih Ming-te was sentenced to life imprisonment; Huang Hsin-chieh to fourteen years; and Yao Chia-wen, Chang Chun-hung, Lin Yi-hsiung, Lu Hsiu-lien, Chen Chu, and Lin Hung-hsuan each to twelve years.**" (Wikipedia, "Formosa Incident" entry[^12])

The military tribunal proceeded over nine days starting March 18, 1980. The defendants retained 15 defense lawyers, including Chiang Peng-chien, Hsieh Chang-ting, Yu Ching, Chen Shui-bian, Su Tseng-chang, and Chang Chun-hsiung. Chen Shui-bian was responsible for defending Huang Hsin-chieh. **Every one of these lawyers went on to become a major figure in the tangwai movement**: Chen Shui-bian was later elected President; Hsieh Chang-ting, Su Tseng-chang, and Chang Chun-hsiung each served as Premier; Yu Ching served as Taipei County Magistrate; and Chiang Peng-chien became the first chairman of the Democratic Progressive Party.

"**The calls to lift martial law, legalize opposition parties, and hold full elections for the central representative bodies were instead spread even further by the military tribunal**"[^13]. The Democratic Progressive Party was founded on September 28, 1986, and martial law was lifted on July 15, 1987. From the tear gas at Dagangpu Roundabout in 1979 to the lifting of martial law in 1987 — a full seven and a half years — was the most pivotal stretch of Taiwan's democratic transition. **The starting point of that transition was not in Taipei. It was in Kaohsiung.**

### February 28, 1980, Xinyi Road, Taipei

Before the military tribunal had even begun, things had already fallen to a darker place.

On February 28, 1980 — the anniversary of the 228 Incident — a bloody attack occurred at the home of Lin Yi-hsiung, who was being held at the Jingmei Military Detention Center awaiting trial. The location was **No. 16, Lane 31, Section 3, Xinyi Road, Taipei City** — Lin Yi-hsiung's home in Taipei. "Lin Yi-hsiung's mother, sixty-some-year-old You A-mei, and his seven-year-old twin daughters, Lin Liang-chun and Lin Ting-chun, were stabbed to death"[^14]. His nine-year-old eldest daughter, Lin Huan-chun, was stabbed six times but survived because her schoolbag shielded her back.

**The Lin family murders happened in Taipei — not in Kaohsiung, and not in Yilan.** Lin Yi-hsiung was from Wujie, Yilan; after being arrested in 1979 over the Formosa Incident, he was held at the Jingmei Military Detention Center in Taipei, and on the day of the attack he himself was in Jingmei, far from the scene. "The case remains unsolved to this day. And because the maximum 25-year statute of limitations for criminal prosecution has now passed, even if the true culprit were found, they could no longer be convicted"[^14].

This case's connection to the Kaohsiung Formosa Incident lies in its timeline; geographically, the location is 350 kilometers from Kaohsiung. The Lin family murders occurred 19 days before the military tribunal opened, and stood as the cruelest page of the White Terror era at the time, as well as a wound shared in common memory by everyone in Taiwan's democracy movement. **The tear gas at Dagangpu Roundabout in 1979, the blood on Xinyi Road in Taipei on February 28, 1980, and the military tribunal's verdict on April 18, 1980** — three wounds, all connected to Kaohsiung, the special municipality that had just been upgraded.

The red ribbons from the upgrade ceremony hadn't even come down yet, and this city's political history had already been cut open.

## Formosa Boulevard Station, the Dome of Light

Twenty-nine years passed.

On September 14, 2008, the Kaohsiung MRT Orange Line opened, and "Formosa Boulevard Station" began service the same day[^15]. The Kaohsiung city government deliberately chose this station name, to turn the site of the old Dagangpu Roundabout into the name of an MRT station. Around one corner, "The Dome of Light" sits on the R10/O5 concourse level.

![The Dome of Light at Formosa Boulevard Station, 2024. Hand-painted by master glass artist Narcissus Quagliata, its 4,500 panes of colored glass are divided into four zones — water, earth, light, and fire — spanning 30 meters in diameter and 660 square meters in area. Directly beneath the MRT station lies the historic site of the December 10, 1979 rally at Dagangpu Roundabout.](/article-images/geography/kaohsiung-02.webp)
_The Dome of Light at Formosa Boulevard Station, September 19, 2024. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dome_of_Light_at_Formosa_Boulevard_Station_20240919.jpg)._

The Dome of Light was "hand-painted by the internationally renowned artist Narcissus Quagliata, taking four and a half years to complete, with a diameter of 30 meters and an area of 660 square meters"[^15]. Narcissus Quagliata (born in Rome in 1942) is a leading authority in contemporary glass art. The dome is made up of 4,500 panes of colored glass, divided into four zones: Water (birth), Earth (growth), Light (glory), and Fire (destruction and rebirth). In 2012, the American travel website BootsnAll ranked Formosa Boulevard Station second among the world's 15 most beautiful metro stations.

The turn from Dagangpu Roundabout to Formosa Boulevard Station took 29 years. **On December 10, 1979, people were prosecuted here for holding a rally; on September 14, 2008, this spot became home to a world-class art installation.** The physical location of the roundabout never changed — its meaning has been rewritten twice by the people of Kaohsiung.

> **📝 Curator's note:** Turning the site of that old confrontation into an art station is Kaohsiung's answer to 1979. It is a form of remembering through rewriting. Taipei's 228 Peace Memorial Park and the Jingmei Human Rights Park remember through commemoration — monuments, memorial statues, memorial exhibitions, all telling visitors "something happened here." Kaohsiung's Formosa Boulevard Station does something different: it is ordinary daily life. More than thirty thousand people pass through this station every day. Whether or not they look up at the Dome of Light doesn't matter — what matters is that this name has been embedded into the skeleton of the city's metro network. **How a city handles its own historical wounds shows in whether it turns that site into a place everyone passes through every day.**

## 38 Districts: From the Qijin Sandbar to the Bunun of Namasia

On December 25, 2010, another upgrade.

"On December 25, 2010, Kaohsiung City's original land area of 153 square kilometers absorbed Kaohsiung County, and its total area jumped to 2,951 square kilometers; the former city's population of 1.52 million was combined with the county's, bringing the total population to 2,774,470"[^16].

"The former Kaohsiung City's 11 districts and the former Kaohsiung County's 27 townships and county-administered cities merged into 38 districts (including 3 mountain indigenous districts of the special municipality)"[^17].

From that day on, "Kaohsiung City" became a municipality stretching **all the way from the Qijin sandbar to the foot of Yushan**. The original 11 districts of Kaohsiung City formed the urban core: Yancheng, Gushan, Lingya, Xinxing, Qianjin, Qianzhen, Sanmin, Nanzi, Zuoying, Qijin, and Xiaogang. The former Kaohsiung County's 27 townships, towns, and cities were scattered around the periphery: Fengshan, Daliao, Niaosong, Renwu, Dashe, Gangshan, Luzhu, Hunei, Yong'an, and Qieding; further north, Qiaotou, Yanchao, Dashu, Dashe, Tianliao, Alian, Mituo, and Ziguan; further east, Meinong, Qishan, Neimen, Shanlin, Liugui, and Jiaxian; and in the deepest reaches, the three mountain indigenous districts of Maolin, Taoyuan, and Namasia.

Every district's story is different. Qijin District, population 28,000, is an 11.3-kilometer-long sandbar where the ferry is the main way in and out. Maolin District, population 1,867, is Kaohsiung's least populous district, home mainly to the Rukai people, at elevations rising from 200 meters to over 1,000 meters. Fengshan District, population 355,183, is Kaohsiung's most populous district — formerly the seat of Kaohsiung County's government, it became the second-most populous single district in the country after the 2010 merger[^18]. The three districts differ in population by a factor of 190, yet administratively they are all simply "Kaohsiung City, [Name] District."

### Three Mountain Districts: Rukai, Bunun, Kanakanavu, Hla'alua

**Maolin District**, in Kaohsiung's southeastern mountains, is home mainly to the Rukai people's Lower Three Villages group, split into the Maolin, Duona, and Wanshan communities. Each community's Rukai speech has its own dialect differences, and the Duona community has the most intact cluster of slate houses[^19].

**Taoyuan District** lies along the upper reaches of the Laonong River. Its main ethnic group is the Bunun, and it is also the traditional territory of the Hla'alua people. The Hla'alua were long misclassified as a subgroup of the southern Tsou, until "the Republic of China government, on June 26, 2014, recognized the Hla'alua as Taiwan's 15th officially recognized Indigenous people, and the Kanakanavu as the 16th"[^20]. The Hla'alua today number roughly 400, concentrated mainly in Gaozhong and Taoyuan Villages in Taoyuan District.

**Namasia District** lies along both banks of the Nanzihsian Creek, where the Bunun and the Kanakanavu live together. The Kanakanavu today number roughly 350, living mainly in Dakanuwa and Maya Villages in Namasia District. The name "Namasia" comes from a legend: "several hundred years ago, a young man named 'Namasia' discovered a giant eel blocking the creek and endangering the village; the people named the Nanzihsian Creek after him, 'Namasia,' in his memory"[^21]. In 2008, Sanmin Township was renamed Namasia Township, and in 2010 it was upgraded again to Namasia District.

The names "Hla'alua" and "Kanakanavu" only returned to official ethnic classification on June 26, 2014. Before that, their ancestors had been recorded for more than a century as a subgroup of the "Tsou," which scholars called the "Southern Tsou." **Before the name restoration, they were represented by others. After it, they speak for themselves.**

### Meinong: The Right Tuai of the Liudui

Following the Nanzihsian Creek downstream from Namasia, you'll reach Meinong. Meinong District is Kaohsiung's only classic Hakka settlement — administratively part of Kaohsiung City, but culturally part of the "**Right Tuai**" of the "**Liudui**."

The Liudui (Six Corps) is the collective name for southern Taiwan's Hakka self-defense militia settlements, divided into the Vanguard Tuai, Front Tuai, Central Tuai, Rear Tuai, Left Tuai, and Right Tuai. The "Right Tuai" covers all or most of Meinong, Liugui, and Shanlin Districts, a small part of Jiaxian District, and parts of Qishan District. During the 1721 rebellion of Zhu Yigui, Hakka villages in the Xiadanshui River basin jointly organized a self-defense militia; "they together elected Li Chih-san as commander-in-chief and split into six groups, the Liudui, to defend their homeland"[^22]. This organization later became the cultural identity of the Hakka settlements.

But this story isn't only about the 1721 militia. Meinong has another, more recent history.

"At the end of 1992, the first 'public hearing on building the Meinong Reservoir,' convened by the Meinong Township Office and local civic groups, set off what became known as the 'town against a nation' — the Meinong Anti-Dam Movement"[^23]. The government had planned to build a reservoir on the upper Meinong River to supply water to the Kaohsiung area, but the dam site sat on a fault line and would have damaged the ecology of Yellow Butterfly Valley's tropical seed-tree forest, as well as impacting traditional Hakka culture in Meinong.

The movement ran for eight years. "On March 18, 2000, Chen Shui-bian was elected President, and subsequently announced that the Meinong Reservoir would not be built during his term"[^23]. The Meinong Association for Community Development became a model for the sustainable development of Taiwan's Hakka communities, and it also helped spark an entire generation's movement of young Hakka people returning home. Today's Meinong paper umbrellas, _bantiao_ rice noodles, Hakka cuisine, and tobacco-curing barns beneath Yueguang Mountain are choices this township made for itself — not decisions made for it by someone else.

## The August That Buried Namasia and Taoyuan

From name restoration and the anti-dam movement, back to a more recent wound.

On August 8, 2009, Typhoon Morakot struck Taiwan. That day, "Jiaxian Township's 72-hour cumulative rainfall reached as high as 1,856 millimeters"[^24] — a figure nearly equal to seventy percent of Taiwan's average annual rainfall. Starting on the morning of the 8th, warnings of landslides began coming out of the mountains, but communications were cut off, and the outside world could not immediately grasp the true scale of the disaster.

The worst-hit was Xiaolin Village. "The most severe case was the destruction of the Xiaolin community in Xiaolin Village, Jiaxian Township, Kaohsiung County, which buried 474 people alive"[^25]. Xiaolin Village's settlement, built along the mountainside, was entirely covered by a landslide: "During the 2009 August 8 flood, as Typhoon Morakot struck, more than 600 households in Xiaolin Village, Jiaxian Township, Kaohsiung County were destroyed by a landslide." Survivors from Xiaolin were later resettled in three groups — to the Da'ai Community in Shanlin, and to Xiaolin Village 1 and Xiaolin Village 2 in Wulipu — but the community's original geographic context could never be recovered.

Namasia, Taoyuan, and Maolin — the three mountain indigenous districts — were hit equally hard. Access roads were cut, bridges collapsed, and villages were isolated from the outside world for days. Namasia's newly built permanent housing was in Shanlin District; some members of the original communities chose to return to the mountains to rebuild, while others stayed below. **From that year on, each community effectively split into two: one in its ancestral homeland, one in the permanent-housing settlement.**

> **📝 Curator's note:** The conventional disaster narrative tends to focus on "death toll," "reconstruction progress," and "government efficiency." But the deeper impact of Typhoon Morakot on Kaohsiung's mountain indigenous communities was that **the geographic connections binding each community together were severed**. For the Bunun, Kanakanavu, Hla'alua, and Rukai peoples, ritual practice, land-based knowledge, and language transmission are all tied to specific locations: which creek's headwaters hold the ancestral spirits, which mountain carries a taboo, which hunting ground belongs to which family. Once a village is resettled, the foundation carrying that geographic knowledge breaks. Seventeen years after Morakot, the population of Kaohsiung's three mountain districts continues to drift downhill; a new generation of indigenous children attends school in Shanlin and Qishan, no longer waking to mornings in slate houses. **Rebuilding a disaster zone is a physical matter — how long it takes for cultural loss to even be recognized is another question altogether.**

## The Two Faces of the Love River

Back from the mountains to the city, to talk about a river.

The Love River rises from Baguailiao Pond in Renwu District, flows through downtown Kaohsiung, and empties into the sea in Qianzhen District, a total length of 16.4 kilometers[^26]. Under Japanese rule it was called the "Takau River" or "Kaohsiung River"; after the war it was renamed the "Love River." After the 1960s, as Kaohsiung industrialized rapidly, sewer systems failed to keep pace, and industrial, agricultural, and domestic wastewater was discharged directly into the river. The Love River became one of Taiwan's most polluted urban rivers — its water a dark gray, slicked with oil, with a sharp chemical smell noticeable up close.

But around the time martial law was lifted in 1987, Taiwan's attitude toward urban rivers began to change. Media coverage began drawing a contrast between "the Love River's successful cleanup and the still-filthy, foul-smelling Tamsui River"[^27], even though the "success" at that point amounted to only preliminary progress. Real remediation accelerated after Hsieh Chang-ting was elected Mayor of Kaohsiung in 1998: "the sewer connection rate rose from 6% at the start of his term to 25%," alongside a landscape overhaul around the river — waterfront walkways were built, a cinema and library were renovated, and the Love Boat river cruise was introduced.

In 2001, the Taiwan Lantern Festival was held in Kaohsiung for the first time (its first time outside Taipei), and the lantern festival along the Love River became an annual highlight. "After the 2000s, the Love River successfully transformed into a major Kaohsiung tourist attraction, gradually shedding its longstanding stereotype as an 'industrial powerhouse, cultural desert'"[^28].

But the industry never left. China Steel is still in Xiaogang, the refinery in Nanzi, the petrochemical plants in Linyuan, the coal-fired power plant in Dalinpu. The Love River's successful cleanup is only one face of this city's transformation; the other face is Dalinpu, still waiting for resettlement.

"The Coastal Industrial Zone is home to China Steel, CPC, Taipower, and many related downstream industries — hundreds of companies, a forest of smokestacks — surrounding settlements like Dalinpu on three sides"[^9]. After residents fought "more than 30 years of protest," Premier Lin Chuan traveled to Dalinpu in 2016 to issue a public apology, acknowledging the environmental pollution and launching a resettlement study. But as of 2026, the environmental-impact-assessment disputes over the resettlement plan still haven't been resolved.

## Weiwuying: 4,500 Panels Under an Aluminum-Alloy Roof

On October 13, 2018, the National Kaohsiung Center for the Arts (Weiwuying) opened[^29]. The site, in Fengshan District, was originally the "Army New Recruit Training Center" (which had been relocating out since 1979); during the martial law era, what the words "Weiwuying" meant to people in Kaohsiung was "the place my son went for military service." Dutch architect Francine Houben drew on Weiwuying's old banyan trees for design inspiration, using a flowing aluminum-alloy roof to link the complex's four performance halls.

![Exterior of the National Kaohsiung Center for the Arts (Weiwuying). Dutch architect Francine Houben designed the flowing aluminum-alloy roof after Weiwuying's old banyan trees — 35,000 square meters, 4,500 aluminum-alloy panels, the largest single-roof performing arts complex in the world.](/article-images/geography/kaohsiung-05.webp)
_The National Kaohsiung Center for the Arts (Weiwuying). Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wei-Wu-Ying_Center_for_the_Arts_01.jpg)._

"The site covers 9.9 hectares, with 3.3 hectares of built floor area; the roof is a 35,000-square-meter curved surface assembled from 4,500 aluminum-alloy panels, making it the largest single-roof performing arts complex in the world"[^29]. The opera house seats 2,236, the concert hall 1,981, the playhouse 1,209, and the recital hall 434. In 2019, _Time_ magazine included Weiwuying on its list of the "World's Greatest Places."

From new-recruit training center to world-class performing arts center, this plot of land's story is the same kind of story as Formosa Boulevard Station: the people of Kaohsiung took the military memory of the martial law era and rewrote it as artistic memory. **A city's martial law history doesn't disappear, but it can be put to new use.**

The year Weiwuying opened happened to mark the 39th anniversary of both the 1979 municipality upgrade and the Formosa Incident. From Dagangpu Roundabout to Formosa Boulevard Station, from the Weiwuying military barracks to the Weiwuying theater complex, it took this city nearly 40 years to stitch its own wound into its own shape.

## 2.71 Million People, Across 38 Districts

As of April 2026, Kaohsiung City's population stands at 2.71 million, making it Taiwan's third-largest special municipality (after New Taipei and Taipei)[^30]. Fengshan District has the most residents, at 355,000; Maolin District has the fewest, at 1,867. That 2.71 million is scattered across 38 districts spanning 2,951 square kilometers, from the Qijin sandbar to the Bunun villages of Namasia, at elevations from 0 meters to over 3,000 meters.

Population makeup: Hoklo people form the majority (descendants of Han settlers who arrived after the Qing court established Fengshan County in 1684); Hakka people are concentrated in Meinong (the Right Tuai of the Liudui); the Makatao, originally Pingpu (Plains Indigenous) people of the "Eight Fengshan Communities," lived on the plains for a thousand years before assimilating into the Hoklo population following Qing-era sinicization. Mainlanders are concentrated in Zuoying's military dependents' villages (a postwar Navy garrison site and the largest cluster of such villages in Taiwan) and the Guomao Community (the largest military-dependents'-village-turned-public-housing complex in southern Taiwan). Mountain indigenous peoples are concentrated in Maolin (Rukai), Taoyuan (Bunun, Hla'alua), and Namasia (Bunun, Kanakanavu).

Compared with Keelung's 360,000, Kaohsiung has more than seven times the population; compared with Taipei's 2.4 million, Kaohsiung has slightly more. But what makes this city distinctive isn't its population count — it's its range: **Sizihwan Beach, the villages of Namasia, the Qijin ferry, and Meinong's paper umbrellas all coexist within the same administrative coordinates.** It is the only special municipality in Taiwan that simultaneously contains an urban core, a heavy-industry belt, a Hakka enclave, and three distinct mountain indigenous peoples.

> **✦** "**The former Kaohsiung City's 11 districts and the former Kaohsiung County's 27 townships and county-administered cities merged into 38 districts (including 3 mountain indigenous districts of the special municipality).**" (Wikipedia, "Administrative Divisions of Kaohsiung" entry[^17])

## 2018's "Han Wave," 2020's Recall

Thirty-nine years after the municipality upgrade, this city's political history gained another chapter.

"On November 24, 2018, Han Kuo-yu defeated Chen Chi-mai in the nine-in-one local elections with 892,545 votes, a 53.87% vote share, to be elected the third-term Mayor of Kaohsiung, taking office on December 25 of that year"[^31]. Han Kuo-yu's victory reversed the DPP's 20-year hold on power in Kaohsiung. The term "Han wave" swept the island from south to north that year; his speaking style, his slogan ("Goods go out, people come in, Kaohsiung strikes it rich"), and his image were nothing like any previous Kaohsiung mayor's.

But the reversal didn't last long. In May 2019, Han Kuo-yu announced his candidacy for the 2020 presidential election, drawing criticism for "neglecting city governance." After he lost the January 2020 presidential race to Tsai Ing-wen, people in Kaohsiung began collecting signatures for a recall.

"The recall vote was held on June 6, 2020; the final count was 939,090 votes in favor and 25,051 against, with a turnout of 42.14%"[^32].

"**Han Kuo-yu became the first special-municipality mayor in the history of the Republic of China to be recalled**"[^32].

The 939,090 votes in favor were nearly 50,000 more than the 892,545 votes that had elected him in 2018. The same group of Kaohsiung voters used the ballot box to elect the same man and then remove him, within two years. The Reporter's headline put it plainly: "**Taiwan's first local head of government to be recalled**"[^33].

The recall itself isn't the point of this article. The point is that this recall on June 6, 2020 belongs to the same political throughline as Dagangpu Roundabout on December 10, 1979, and Formosa Boulevard Station on September 14, 2008 — the same city's same political lineage. **The people of Kaohsiung don't put much faith in "gods."** In 1979, they stood up against martial law. In 2020, they stood up again to recall a sitting mayor. This city translates public opinion into physical action more directly than any other city in Taiwan.

## 4:30 AM, Black Kites Behind the China Steel Smokestacks

Back to the opening scene.

At 4:30 in the morning, longline tuna boats unload their catch at Qianzhen Fishing Port; at five, the Gushan ferry carries the first wave of Qijin commuters across the harbor; at six, Formosa Boulevard Station sits spotless beneath the Dome of Light, before the morning commuter crowds arrive; at seven, the first high-speed rail train out of Zuoying Station carries a group of people north to work in Taipei.

Two kilometers away, white smoke rises from the smokestacks of China Steel's Xiaogang plant. A little further on, the waters of Houjing Creek flow slowly toward Kaohsiung Harbor. From the 1987 siege to the fifth cracker's 2015 shutdown, the smell of that water has changed for Houjing's residents. A little further still, Dalinpu has yet to be resettled.

Farther out, at elevations above 1,000 meters, the mornings of the Rukai, Bunun, Kanakanavu, and Hla'alua peoples in Maolin, Taoyuan, and Namasia are just beginning. Residents of the post-Morakot permanent housing wake up in Shanlin District; the community members who stayed behind in their ancestral villages wake up in the mountains.

So many things happen simultaneously in this city's mornings because the 2010 county-city merger placed places that once belonged to entirely different geographic units into the same administrative frame. **The 38 districts, from the Qijin sandbar to the villages of Namasia, are the most concrete physical expression of Taiwan's administrative upgrades.**

Seen from Taipei, Kaohsiung is a heavy-industrial port city plus a chapter of democratic history plus a Weiwuying. Seen from the ocean, Kaohsiung is the granddaughter of a 1968 export-processing-zone factory girl now working at TSMC; it's the son of someone from the 1990 Houjing referendum now a professor; it's one of the lawyers from the 1979 Dagangpu tear gas who later became President. Seen from Qianzhen Fishing Port at 4:30 in the morning, Kaohsiung is a port city where **the whole world is still asleep, and this city has already gone to work**.

Next time you visit Kaohsiung, don't just go to Liuhe Night Market and the 85 Sky Tower. Try taking the Gushan ferry to Qijin at five in the morning to watch the fishing boats come in. Or take the Kaohsiung MRT Red Line to R8 Sanduo Shopping District, transfer to the Orange Line, and get off at R10/O5 Formosa Boulevard Station to look up at the Dome of Light. Or go further still: rent a car and drive from Meinong to Namasia, following the Nanzihsian Creek into the mountains. Then you'll remember one thing: **the wound this city carried from December 10, 1979; the glint off Weiwuying's aluminum-alloy roof in 2018; the 2014 name restoration of the 15th and 16th recognized peoples; the roughly 930,000 votes of the 2020 recall — all of it is still circulating through this city of 2.71 million people.**

Kaohsiung is not Taiwan's lower body. Kaohsiung is the city where three layers of Taiwan's postwar eras — industrialization, democratization, and cultural transformation — physically coexist at once.
