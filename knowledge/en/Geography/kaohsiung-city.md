# Kaohsiung: The Year It Was Upgraded to a Special Municipality, and Eight People from Kaohsiung Were Imprisoned in Taipei

> **30-Second Summary:** On July 1, 1979, Kaohsiung was upgraded to Taiwan's second special municipality, following Taipei in 1967. On December 10 of the same year—World Human Rights Day—a speech by _Beautiful Island_ magazine at Daguangpu Roundabout turned into a conflict under tear gas. On April 18, 1980, military court verdicts sentenced Shih Ming-te to life imprisonment, Huang Hsin-kai to fourteen years, and Yao Chia-wen, Chang Chun-hsing, Lin Yi-hsiung, Lü Hsu-lien, Chen Chu, and Lin Hong-hsien to twelve years each. In the early morning of February 28, 1980, at Lin Residence, No. 16, Lane 3, Section 3, Xinyi Road, Taipei City, Lin Yi-hsiung's mother and twin daughters were murdered. The wounds on Section 3 are all related to this newly upgraded special municipality of Kaohsiung. With 2.71 million people spread across 38 districts, the administrative coordinates of a special municipality containing Rukai and Bunun tribal communities, steel smokestacks, and aluminum roofs. This article aims to say that Kaohsiung is a wound in Taiwanese democracy, and it is also the physical mass of this island's industrialization.

## 4:30 AM at Qianzhen Fishing Port

If you ask a person from Kaohsiung "When is Kaohsiung most charming?", they won't tell you about Liuhe Night Market (which is for tourists). They might tell you about the Qianzhen Fishing Port at 4:30 AM, or the Cijin Ferry at 5 AM.

The Qianzhen Fishing Port, located in Qianzhen District, is Taiwan's largest deep-sea fishing base. Tuna longliners leave from here, traveling to the Indian Ocean, Atlantic Ocean, and South Pacific, returning after six months to a year. The fishermen are mostly Indonesian or Filipino, speaking a mix of Tâi-gí, English, and their native languages. In the days of unloading, the port is piled high with frozen tuna; the fish are bigger than people and are moved one by one onto the auction floor. The tuna that Taipei sushi restaurants will prepare tonight might have been caught off the coast of Madagascar two weeks ago.

The Cijin Ferry runs every three minutes, traveling from Cijin to Qixing in five minutes, for a fare of 40 NTD. The morning service starts at six AM, but office workers are already queuing before dawn. With 28,000 people on Qixing Island, there is no bridge connecting it to the mainland (the tunnel crossing is for vehicles); the ferry is the only link between this sandbar and the Kaohsiung urban area.

This is modern evidence of Kaohsiung as a port city. The historical glory of being upgraded to a special municipality in 1979, the industrial glory of China Steel Corporation building its plant in 1971, and the shipping glory of being one of the world's top three container ports for seven consecutive years from 1993 to 1999—all belong to the past. But **while the capital is still asleep, Kaohsiung has already started moving things for the whole world**.

## Takau, Taku, Kaohsiung: The Name Change in 1920

Four hundred years ago, there was a Makadai tribe settlement at the foot of Takau Mountain (now Shoushan), surrounded by bamboo groves. The tribe called it "Takau" using a local pronunciation of Minnan, meaning "bamboo forest" [^1]. Han people gradually moved in after the Qing court established Fengshan County in 1684; initially as a fishing village, it later became a port.

After the Treaty of Tianjin in 1858, Takau opened as a port in 1863. The British consulate was established in Chaoshuantou (now Gushan), making it one of the treaty ports opened to foreign powers by the Qing court, alongside Xiamen and Fuzhou. In 1899 (Meiji 32), Goto Shinpei, the fourth Resident-General during Japanese rule, toured Southern Taiwan and decided to develop Takau Port. Due to insufficient funds, the first phase of port construction was not officially started until 1908 [^2].

In October 1920, Governor-General Kenjiro Daike, the eighth governor during Japanese rule, abolished the township system and renamed "Takau Street" to "**Kaohsiung Street**." The characters "Kaohsiung" are derived from the Japanese pronunciation _takao_. Whether this has any connection to Mt. Kaohsiung in Kyoto is still a subject of academic debate [^1]. However, from that moment on, the city was no longer called Takau.

In 1924 (Taisho 13), Kaohsiung Street was upgraded to "Kaohsiung City." The city hall was located at the site of the Dai Tian Temple in Guabo Street (Yancheng District). When it became a city, the population exceeded 35,000 people; by 1940, it had grown to 160,000, making it Taiwan's second-largest city, surpassed only by Taipei [^1]. Modernization in Kaohsiung during Japanese rule was concentrated in Hamaxi (a Taiwanese transliteration of the Japanese _hama-sen_), with Yancheng, Gushan, Zuoying, and Lingya developing sequentially. The city hall, banks, and trading companies were clustered in Hamaxi and Yancheng.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Most residents of Kaohsiung today do not use the name "Takau," but it is embedded in the texture of local place names and food. The "Shoushan" in Takau Mountain (Shoushan) was changed after Emperor Hirohito visited in 1923 during Japanese rule; "Shaishan" (Pine Mountain), another local saying for Shoushan, refers to the macaques there. The sound "Takau" remains in early documents between Tainan and Kaohsiung, in the oral memory of the Makadai tribe before their Sinicization, and is preserved by a group of cultural workers from the 2000s who use "Takau" as a project name (such as the Takau History and Culture Association or the Takau Kaohsiung History and Culture Network) to resist the resurgence of the Taisho-era naming. **It takes a hundred years for a city to change its name once; it might take another hundred years to bring that name back.**

## The Army Descended from Shoushan in March 1947

Following the events of February 28, 1947, in Taipei, the situation spread to Kaohsiung in early March. At that time, the commander of the Kaohsiung Fortress was Peng Meng-chi (1908–1997), a general in the Army from Wuchang, Hubei Province, stationed on Shoushan.

"As early as March 4, Commander Peng Meng-chi of the Kaohsiung Fortress decided to use military suppression methods,"[^3]. On the morning of March 6, seven negotiation representatives—including Mayor Huang Chung-tu and City Council Chairman Peng Qingkao (the father of surgeon Peng Ming-min)—went to Shoushan as agreed to negotiate a peaceful resolution with Peng Meng-chi. "As soon as they entered the conference room, Tu Guangming, Fan Cangrong, and Tseng Fengming were arrested,"[^3].

"After arresting the negotiation representatives, Peng Meng-chi decided to bring forward the suppression action scheduled for the 7th, ordering the army to descend the mountain that afternoon at two o'clock,"[^3].

What happened after two in the afternoon is recorded in these segments:

"The army surrounded Kaohsiung Train Station from Sanquai, and the crowds of citizens and travelers fled in haste. The military also shelled passengers hiding in underground tunnels, resulting in countless casualties,"[^3].

"The troops attacking the city hall first threw grenades and killed anyone they saw. Representatives and citizens waiting for news of negotiations in front of the city hall suffered heavy casualties; about fifty or sixty people died,"[^3].

The defense of Xiongzhong was another aspect of this massacre. "On March 4, students formed a self-defense force (Xiongzhong Self-Defense Force), led by Kaohsiung First Middle School (now Xiongzhong) in Taiwan Province... The purpose was to maintain security near the school and use Xiongzhong as a temporary shelter for foreign residents whose lives were threatened,"[^3]. A group of high school students sheltered attacked neighbors in the school while using books as shields against the army.

The three representatives, Tu Guangming, Fan Cangrong, and Tseng Fengming, were executed by firing squad at the Kaohsiung Fortress on March 9. Peng Meng-chi was later promoted and highly valued by Chiang Chung-cheng, becoming the Commander-in-Chief of the Army in 1957 and a General of the Army in 1966. His title during the 1947 events in Kaohsiung was "The Butcher of Kaohsiung." The same individual later advanced through the military and political system of Taiwan.

This was one of the wounds that Kaohsiung had before the Beautiful Island Incident in 1979.

## 1968 Export Processing Zone, 1971 China Steel, 1973 Lin-Yuan Petrochemical

Two decades passed, and by the late 1960s, Kaohsiung had become another city.

"In December 1966, Taiwan passed the 'Act on Establishing Export Processing Zones,' designating the Qianzhen District of Kaohsiung Port as the Kaohsiung Export Processing Zone" [^4]. This was one of Taiwan's first and among the world's earliest export processing zones, combining free trade zone and industrial park functions with tax incentives, a single window, and fast customs clearance. The first factory moved in in 1968, employing many young female workers from Tainan, Pingtung, and Chiayi, who earned over one thousand dollars per month and lived in four- or eight-person dormitories. These workers were later nicknamed the "Processing Zone Aunts," and their grandchildren now work at TSMC.

Refineries were even earlier. The CPC Kaohsiung Refinery, located at the foothills of Banping Mountain in Nanzi District, covers an area of 262 hectares, and its predecessor was the "Dai Nippon Teikoku Navy Sixth Fuel Plant Kaohsiung Site" during World War II, which was received and repaired by CPC in 1947. "The first light crude cracking plant (No. 1) was put into operation in 1968 and closed in 1990; the second light crude cracking plant (No. 2) was put into operation in 1975 and closed in 1994; the fifth light crude cracking plant (No. 5) was put into operation in 1994 and closed in 2015" [^5]. The refinery "sequentially housed dozens of large factories, including No. 1, No. 2, and No. 5, and became the officially recognized area with the greatest pollution" [^5].

On December 3, 1971, China Steel Corporation was formally established [^6]. The first oil crisis in 1973 caused a global economic downturn, leading Chiang Ching-kuo to propose the "Ten Great Construction Projects," which included China Steel. Phase one of the project was completed in 1977, and steel production began. Today, China Steel is still located in Xiaogang, with an annual crude steel output of about ten million tons, primarily producing steel plates, wire rods, hot-rolled, and cold-rolled products [^6]. The supporting Lin-Yuan Petrochemical area was established in 1973, and together with the Nanzi refinery and Xiaogang China Steel, it formed the three heaviest gears of Taiwan's post-war heavy industry.

In 1965, the government issued the "Regulations for Promoting the Recycling of Old Ships," leading to ship dismantling in Qianzhen and Caoya areas. "Around 20,000 workers dismantled ships at the docks daily, achieving a dismantling rate of up to ten thousand tons of steel plates per day, earning Taiwan the reputation of the world's 'Shipbreaking Kingdom'" [^7]. In 1973, Kaohsiung City set the world record for shipbreaking volume. In 1986, an oil tanker exploded in the Daren Palace Shipbreaking Industrial Zone due to residual oil in its bilge, killing 16 people and injuring hundreds. "The Kaohsiung Port Authority reclaimed the Daren Shipbreaking Specialty Area in 1989, converting it into the Fifth Container Terminal. In 1990, the shipbreaking industry was officially forced out" [^7]. An industry that was once number one globally disappeared in twenty years.

> **✦** "It sequentially housed dozens of large factories, including No. 1, No. 2, and No. 5, and became the officially recognized area with the greatest pollution." (Reporter, _The Undying Chemical Zombie_ [^5])

The memories of Kaohsiung residents about this period are highly fragmented. From 1971 to the 1980s, heavy industry was both a pride and a paycheck for Kaohsiung. But during the same period, local residents began noticing the thick ash falling from their roofs, and fishermen in Dalinpu started noticing fewer fish in their nets. Then came 1987.

## The Vote That Lingered: 61% to 39%

"In June 1987, the CPC's 'Fifth Light Cracking Plant' project was officially published in the media"[^8]. Residents of Houjin immediately organized after learning that the government planned to build a Fifth Light plant based on existing First and Second Light facilities.

"A villagers' meeting was held on August 5, 1987, establishing the 'Anti-Fifth Light Self-Aid Committee'"[^8]. Starting in August, residents continuously surrounded the factory, taking turns guarding the CPC gate to block construction vehicles from entering. This movement occurred alongside the lifting of martial law that year and was Taiwan's first large-scale environmental protest after WWII.

"Taiwan's first referendum on the Fifth Light plant was held on May 6, 1990. The voting results were: with a voter turnout of 66%, those firmly against the Fifth Light won with 4,499 votes (61%) over the 2,900 who agreed to negotiations"[^8].

The referendum did not stop the Fifth Light plant.

"On September 14, 1990, Mayor Hao Po-tsu promised that CPC would relocate the factory after 25 years and provide a feedback fund of NT$1.5 billion to Houjin residents, setting a record for the highest anti-pollution compensation in Taiwan"[^8]. Eight days later, "on September 22, 1990, Minister of Economic Affairs Hsiao Wan-chang announced that construction of the Fifth Light plant would begin"[^8].

The 25 years promised by Hao Po-tsu coincided with 2015. **The Fifth Light plant truly shut down on November 1, 2015**, fulfilling the promise, but for a whole generation, the residents of Houjin grew up breathing the air from the Fifth Light plant. From the surrounding in 1987 to the shutdown in 2015, this movement is known as the "28-Year Anti-Fifth Light Movement"[^8].

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Common environmental narratives focus on political alignments such as "green governance." However, the story of Houjin's resistance against the Fifth Light plant is more like another structure: **it is a story of a small village exchanging 25 years of patience for a factory closure.** There was no moment of victory in between; every year involved losses—the Fifth Light plant was still built, emissions were still released, and residents' health was still affected. The factory only closed after 25 years of losing. The biggest difference between Taiwan's environmental movements and other social movements is its time scale: it is measured in "generations," not in "terms." After the Houjin case, Dalinpu spent over 30 years protesting for relocation[^9], during which Executive Yuan President Lin Chuan-kai publicly apologized there in 2016. **The victory is not about winning one movement, but about ensuring the next generation does not have to breathe the same air.**

## The Tear Gas of Daguangpu Roundabout, on International Human Rights Day

Returning to 1979.

On July 1, "Kaohsiung City, which was originally a county-level city in Taiwan Province, merged Xiaogang Township of Kaohsiung County and was upgraded to a special municipality, becoming the second special municipality in Taiwan" [^10]. The first special municipality was Taipei City, which was upgraded in 1967. Kaohsiung was the second, twelve years later. It had 11 districts (including the newly established Xiaogang District) and a population of about one million, making it the largest industrial hub in Southern Taiwan at the time.

In the same year, January 1979, the United States severed diplomatic relations with the Republic of China. On July 1, Kaohsiung was upgraded; on August, _Beautiful Island Magazine_ was launched, with Huang Sin-chieh as publisher, Hsu Shin-liang as president, and Shi Ming-te as general manager. The magazine became a flagship for opposition movements. In early December, opposition figures applied to hold a rally and march in Kaohsiung on December 10 (the United Nations Human Rights Day) under the name "Human Rights Memorial Committee," demanding the lifting of martial law and the release of political prisoners. The authorities did not approve it.

On the evening of December 10, "because the originally planned venue, Hulin Park, was blocked, opposition figures decided to move to the roundabout where Zhongshan Road and Zhongzheng Road intersect" [^11]. The rally site was first near the Xinxing Roundabout on Zhongshan Road in Xinxing District, before moving to Daguangpu Roundabout, which was one of the most important roundabouts in central Kaohsiung at the time.

"The crowds participating in the march were surrounded by thousands of riot police near Daguangpu Roundabout and the conflict finally erupted after being subjected to intense spotlights, tear gas, and shield advances" [^12].

The confrontation lasted until late at night. Three days later, on the morning of December 13, the Military Intelligence Command launched a mass arrest across the island. Chen Chu, Lin Yi-hsiung, Lu Hsiu-lien, and Chang Chun-hung were arrested sequentially in the early morning. Huang Sin-chieh was arrested at the Legislative Yuan on the morning of December 14. Shi Ming-te fled for 26 days and was captured at a private residence on January 8, 1980. A total of 152 people were involved.

> **✦** "“**On April 18, the military court delivered its final verdict; all eight individuals were found guilty. Shi Ming-te was sentenced to life imprisonment, Huang Sin-chieh received fourteen years, and Yao Chia-wen, Chang Chun-hung, Lin Yi-hsiung, Lu Hsiu-lien, Chen Chu, and Lin Hong-hsien each received twelve years.**” (Wikipedia article on the Beautiful Island Incident [^12])

The military trial lasted for 9 days starting March 18, 1980. The defendants hired 15 defense lawyers, including Jiang Peng-chien, Hsieh Chang-ting, Yu Ching, Chen Shui-bian, Su Tseng-chang, and Chang Chun-shiu. Chen Shui-bian was responsible for defending Huang Sin-chieh. **These lawyers later all became key figures in the opposition movement**: Chen Shui-bian later became president; Hsieh Chang-ting, Su Tseng-chang, and Chang Chun-shiu served as Premier; Yu Ching served as the mayor of Taipei County; and Jiang Peng-chien was the first chairman of the Democratic Progressive Party.

"**The voices demanding the lifting of martial law, the end of party bans, and a full election of central representatives were further amplified through the military trial**" [^13]. The Democratic Progressive Party was founded on September 28, 1986, and martial law was lifted on July 15, 1987. From the tear gas at Daguangpu Roundabout in 1979 to the lifting of martial law in 1987, this period of seven and a half years was the most crucial part of Taiwan's democratic transition. **The starting point of this transformation was not Taipei, but Kaohsiung.**

### Xinyi Road, Taipei City, February 28, 1980

Before the military trial even began, events had spiraled into deeper tragedy.

On February 28, 1980 (the anniversary of the 228 Incident), a blood case occurred at the home of Lin Yi-hsiung while he was awaiting trial in the Jingmei Military Detention Center. The location was **No. 16, Lane 31, Section 3, Xinyi Road, Taipei City**, which was Lin Yi-hsiung's residence in Taipei. "Lin Yi-hsiung, along with his mother You A-mei and his twin daughters, Lin Liang-jun and Lin Ting-jun, were murdered" [^14]. His nine-year-old eldest daughter, Lin Huan-chun, was stabbed six times but survived after shielding her backpack.

**The Lin residence blood case occurred in Taipei, not Kaohsiung, nor Yilan**. Lin Yi-hsiung was from Wujie, Yilan. After being arrested during the Beautiful Island Incident in 1979 and detained at the Jingmei Military Detention Center in Taipei, he was in Jingmei on the day of the incident, far from the scene. "This case has never been solved. And since the maximum criminal prosecution period of 25 years has passed, finding the true culprits is impossible for conviction" [^14].

The connection between this case and the Beautiful Island Incident in Kaohsiung lies in the timeline; the location was 350 kilometers from Kaohsiung. The Lin residence blood case occurred 19 days before the military trial began, representing one of the cruelest pages of the White Terror and a shared scar memory for all those involved in Taiwan's democratic movement. **The tear gas at Daguangpu Roundabout in 1979, the blood on Xinyi Road in Taipei on February 28, 1980, and the verdict from the military court on April 18, 1980**—three wounds all relate to this newly upgraded special municipality of Kaohsiung.

The red ribbons from the upgrade celebration had not yet been taken down when the political history of this city was already scarred.

## The Dome of Light at Beautiful Island Station

Twenty-nine years passed.

On September 14, 2008, the Orange Line of the Kaohsiung MRT opened, and "Beautiful Island Station" was activated on the same day[^15]. This station name was deliberately chosen by the Kaohsiung City Government to designate the location of the former Dagangpu Ring Road as a subway station. A turn away, and the "Dome of Light" is located at the R10/O5 mezzanine level.

![The Dome of Light at Beautiful Island Station, 2024. Painted by Narcissus Quagliata (Narcissus Quagliata), it consists of four zones—water, earth, light, and fire—using 4,500 pieces of stained glass, with a diameter of 30 meters and an area of 660 square meters. Directly beneath the subway station is the historical location of the Dagangpu Ring Road from December 10, 1979.](/article-images/geography/kaohsiung-02.webp)
_The Dome of Light at Beautiful Island Station, 2024-09-19. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dome_of_Light_at_Formosa_Boulevard_Station_20240919.jpg)._

"The Dome of Light was hand-painted by the internationally renowned artist Narcissus Quagliata over four and a half years, reaching a diameter of 30 meters and an area of 660 square meters"[^15]. Narcissus Quagliata (born in Rome in 1942) is an authority on contemporary glass art. The dome is composed of 4,500 pieces of stained glass, divided into four sections: Water (gestation), Earth (growth), Light (glory), and Fire (destruction and rebirth). In 2012, the US travel website BootsnAll ranked Beautiful Island Station as the second most beautiful among the world's top 15 subway stations.

From the Dagangpu Ring Road to Beautiful Island Station, this transformation took 29 years. **On December 10, 1979, people were prosecuted here for a demonstration; on September 14, 2008, this place became home to a world-class art installation.** The physical location of the ring road did not change, but its meaning was rewritten twice by the people of Kaohsiung.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Turning a site of past conflict into an artistic station is the response of the people of Kaohsiung to 1979. It is a form of rewriting memory. The 228 Park in Taipei and the Taipei Human Rights Park remember through commemoration—monuments, statues, exhibitions that tell readers, "something happened here." Beautiful Island Station in Kaohsiung does something else: it is everyday life. Over thirty thousand people board and alight at this station every day; whether they look up at the Dome of Light is irrelevant. What matters is that the name has been embedded into the skeletal structure of the city's subway network. **How a city deals with its historical wounds is seen in whether it turns that location into a place people pass through daily.**

## 38 Districts: From Qijin Sandbar to the Pa'ani

On December 25, 2010, there was another upgrade.

"On December 25, 2010, Kaohsiung City, which originally covered an area of 153 square kilometers, was merged into Kaohsiung County, causing the land area to increase dramatically to 2,951 square kilometers, and the original population of Kaohsiung City, 1.52 million people, was included in Kaohsiung County, bringing the total population to 2.77447 million" [^16].

"The 11 original districts of Kaohsiung City were merged with the 27 townships and cities of the original Kaohsiung County to form 38 districts (including 3 indigenous mountain areas)." [^17]

From this day forward, "Kaohsiung City" became a city extending **from Qijin sandbar all the way to the foot of Yushan**. The 11 original districts of Kaohsiung were the urban core: Yancheng, Gusan, Lingya, Xinxing, Qianjin, Qianzhen, Sanmin, Nanzi, Zuoying, Qijin, and Xiaogang. The 27 townships and cities of the original Kaohsiung County were in the periphery: Fengshan, Daliao, Tiosung, Renwu, Dasha, Gaomei, Luzhu, Hunei, Yong'an, Qiechian; further north were Qiaotou, Yanshao, Dashu, Dasha, Tianliao, Alian, Mituo, and Zigan; to the east were Meinong, Qishan, Neimen, Shanlin, Liugui, Jiaxian; and in the deepest parts were the three indigenous mountain areas: Maolin, Taoyuan, and Pa'ani.

The story of each district is different. Qijin District, with 28,000 residents, is a sandbar 11.3 kilometers long, and ferries are the main means of transport to and from it. Maolin District, with 1,867 residents, is the least populated district in Kaohsiung City, primarily inhabited by the Rukai people, with elevations rising from 200 meters to over 1,000 meters. Fengshan District, with 355,183 residents, is the most populous district in Kaohsiung City; it was originally the county seat of Kaohsiung County and became the second-largest population area nationally after the merger in 2010 [^18]. Despite a 190-fold difference in population among these three districts, they are all administratively called "a district of Kaohsiung City."

### The Three Mountain Areas: Rukai, Pa'ani, Kanakanavu, and La'aluwa

**Maolin District**, located in the southeastern mountains of Kaohsiung, is primarily inhabited by the three communities of the Rukai people: Maolin, Donna, and Wanshan. Each community has dialectal variations in their Rukai language, and the stone-house cluster in Donna Village is the most complete [^19].

**Taoyuan District** is distributed along the upper reaches of the Laonong Stream, and its main ethnic groups are the Pa'ani people, who also traditionally inhabit the territory of the La'aluwa. The La'aluwa (Hla'alua) were long mistakenly considered a branch of the South Tsou people until "the government of the Republic of China recognized the La'aluwa as the 15th indigenous tribe of Taiwan and the Kanakanavu as the 16th tribe on June 26, 2014" [^20]. Today, there are about 400 La'aluwa, mainly distributed in Gaodong Village and Taoyuan Village in Taoyuan District.

**Pa'ani District**, situated along both banks of the Nanzi Xian Stream, is home to the Pa'ani people and the Kanakanavu (Kanakanavu) people. There are about 350 Kanakanavu today, mainly residing in Dakanuwali and Maya'li in Pa'ani District. The name "Pa'ani" comes from a legend: "Hundreds of years ago, a young man named 'Pa'ani' discovered a giant eel blocking the stream, endangering the tribe, and the people named the Nanzi Xian Stream after him to commemorate him" [^21]. In 2008, Sanmin Township was renamed Pa'ani Township, and it was upgraded to Pa'ani District in 2010.

The names "La'aluwa" and "Kanakanavu" were only officially recognized as distinct tribes on June 26, 2014. Before that, their ancestors had been recorded for over a hundred years as a branch of the "Tsou" people, which scholars referred to as "South Tsou." **Before official recognition, they were represented by others; after official recognition, they spoke for themselves.**

### Meinong: The Right Cluster of Liuhui

Following the Nanzi Xian Stream downstream from Pa'ani, one encounters Meinong. Meinong District is the only typical Hakka village in Kaohsiung, administratively part of Kaohsiung City but culturally belonging to the "**Right Cluster**" of "**Liuhui**."

Liuhui is a collective term for Hakka communities in southern Taiwan, divided into Xianfeng Cluster, Qianfeng Cluster, Zhongfeng Cluster, Houfeng Cluster, Zuofeng Cluster, and Right Cluster. The "Right Cluster" covers all or most of Meinong District, Liugui District, and Shanlin District, some parts of Jiaxian District, and parts of Qishan District. During the Zhu Yigui Incident in 1721, Hakka villages along the lower Tamsui Stream jointly organized self-defense militias, "collectively promoting Li Zhisan as Grand Marshal and dividing into six groups (Liuhui) to defend their homeland" [^22], and this organizational structure later became part of the cultural identity of the Hakka communities.

However, this story is not limited to the militia in 1721. Meinong has another history that is more recent.

"At the end of 1992, the first 'Meinong Reservoir Public Hearing,' convened by the Meinong Township Office and local associations, sparked a movement known as the 'Meinong Anti-Reservoir Movement' against the small town enemy" [^23]. The government planned to build a reservoir upstream on the Meinong Stream to supply water to the Kaohsiung area, but the dam site was located on a fault line and would damage the subtropical mother forest ecosystem of Huangdie Cui Valley, impacting traditional Hakka culture in Meinong.

The movement lasted eight years. "On March 18, 2000, Chen Shui-bian was elected president and subsequently announced that he would not build the Meinong Reservoir during his term" [^23]. The Meinong Love Village Advancement Association became a model for the sustainable development of Hakka communities in Taiwan and also drove the return movement of an entire generation of Hakka youth. Today, the paper umbrellas, board-making, Hakka cuisine, and tobacco houses under Moonlight Mountain in Meinong are the results of this town's own choices, not decisions made by others.

## Namaxia and Taoyuan Buried in That August Disaster

Returning to a more immediate wound from the Zhengming and Fan Shiku movements.

On August 8, 2009, Typhoon Morakot struck Taiwan. On that day, "the cumulative rainfall in Jiaoxiang Township was as high as 1,856 millimeters" [^24], a figure nearly equal to seven-tenths of the average annual precipitation in Taiwan. Landslide warnings began emerging from the mountains early on the 8th, but communication was cut off, and the actual disaster situation could not be grasped by outsiders for a time.

The most severe was Xiaolin Village. "The village destruction event in Xiaolin Tribe, Jiaoxiang Township, Kaohsiung County, which resulted in 474 people being buried alive" [^25]. The tribal structure of Xiaolin Village, built on the mountainside, was completely covered by a landslide; "during the August 88 water disaster when Typhoon Morakot struck, over six hundred households in Xiaolin Village, Jiaoxiang Township, Kaohsiung County, were destroyed by landslides." The survivors from Xiaolin later moved in three batches to Shansulin Daai Park, Wuli Pu Xiaolin Village, and Xiaolin Er Village, but the original geographical context of the tribe could never be recovered.

The three indigenous mountain areas—Namaxia, Taoyuan, and Maolin—were also severely affected. The connecting roads were cut off, bridges collapsed, and the tribes were isolated from the outside world for several days. Permanent housing was built in Shansulin for Namaxia; some tribal members of the original village chose to return to the mountains to rebuild, while others remained in the valley below. **From that year onward, the tribe became two tribes: one in the ancestral home and one in the permanent housing.**

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Common disaster narratives focus on "number of fatalities," "reconstruction progress," and "government efficiency." However, the impact of the '88 typhoon on the ethnic groups in the Kaohsiung mountains was deeper: it was the **severing of the tribe's geographical connection**. The rituals, land knowledge, and language transmission of the Bunun, Kanakanafu, La'aluwa, and Rukai peoples were all tied to specific geographic locations: which stream upstream held ancestral spirits, which mountain had taboos, and which hunting ground belonged to which family. Once relocated, this foundation for geographical knowledge was broken. Seventeen years after Morakot, the population of the three mountainous areas in Kaohsiung continued to drift down into the valleys; the new generation of tribal children attend schools in Shansulin and Qishan, waking up not from stone houses. **The reconstruction of the disaster-stricken areas is physical; how long will it take for the cultural loss to be recognized?**

## The Two Lines of the Aihe River

From the mountains back to the city, there is one river story.

The Aihe River originates from Bagualaotai Pond in Renwu District, flows through Kaohsiung City, and empties into the sea at Qianzhen District, with a total length of 16.4 kilometers[^26]. During the Japanese colonial period, it was called "Dagou River" or "Kaohsiung River"; after the war, it was renamed "Aihe." After the 1960s, Kaohsiung underwent rapid industrialization. With inadequate sewage systems, industrial and agricultural wastewater, along with domestic sewage, was discharged directly into the Aihe River. The Aihe became one of Taiwan's most severely polluted urban rivers; its dark gray water often carried oil slicks, and a pungent chemical smell could be detected nearby.

However, attitudes toward urban rivers in Taiwan began to change around the time of political liberalization in 1987. Media reports presented contrasts such as "Aihe remediation success versus the still-smelly Tamsui River,"[^27] although the success at that time was only preliminary. Real remediation accelerated after Lai Ching-te was elected mayor of Kaohsiung in 1998: "the sewage collection rate increased from 6% at his inauguration to 25%," and landscape improvements were made around the Aihe, including building waterfront protection, renovating the Film Library, and introducing the Love Boat.

In 2001, Taiwan Lantern Festival was held in Kaohsiung (for the first time outside of Taipei), making the lantern festival by the Aihe a yearly event. "After the 2000s, the Aihe successfully transformed into an important tourist attraction in Kaohsiung, gradually shedding the stereotype of being an 'industrial hub and cultural desert' after many years,"[^28].

But industry did not leave. CSCO remains in Xiaogang, refineries are in Nanzi, petrochemical plants are in Linyuan, and thermal power plants are in Dalinpu. The Aihe remediation success was only one aspect of this city's transformation; the other aspect is that Dalinpu is still waiting for relocation.

"The coastal industrial zone has CSCO, CPC, Taipower, and many related downstream industries, with hundreds of companies and numerous smokestacks surrounding settlements like Dalinpu."[^9] Residents "contested for over 30 years." In 2016, Premier Lin Chuan publicly apologized in Dalinpu, acknowledging the polluted environment and initiating a relocation survey. However, the environmental review controversy surrounding the relocation plan has not concluded even by 2026.

The two lines of the Aihe: on one side are tourism, lantern festivals, and the Love Boat—the story of successful remediation; on the other side are Dalinpu, Nanzi's aftermath, Linyuan petrochemicals, and 30 years of relocation struggles. **The same city is doing two things at once: one that can become a tourist photo, and another that only exists in an environmental review report.**

## Weiwu Performing Arts Center: 4,500 Panels Under an Aluminum Alloy Roof

The National Art and Culture Center in Weiwu opened on October 13, 2018[^29]. Located in Fengshan District, the site was originally a "Army Recruit Training Center" (which relocated starting in 1979). During martial law, for people of Kaohsiung, the name "Weiwu" evoked memories of "the place where my son served in the military." Dutch architect Francine Houben drew inspiration from the old banyan trees at Weiwu to design a flowing aluminum alloy roof connecting four performance halls.

![Exterior view of the National Art and Culture Center in Weiwu. Dutch architect Francine Houben designed a flowing aluminum alloy roof inspired by the old banyan trees at Weiwu, covering 35,000 square meters with 4,500 aluminum panels, making it the largest multi-purpose venue under a single roof globally.](/article-images/geography/kaohsiung-05.webp)
_National Art and Culture Center in Weiwu. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Wei-Wu-Ying_Center_for_the_Arts_01.jpg)._

"The site covers 9.9 hectares, with a building area of 3.3 hectares, featuring a curved roof measuring 35,000 square meters constructed from 4,500 aluminum panels, making it the largest multi-purpose performance venue under a single roof globally"[^29]. The center includes an opera house with 2,236 seats, a concert hall with 1,981 seats, a theater with 1,209 seats, and a performance hall with 434 seats. In 2019, _Time_ magazine selected the Weiwu Performing Arts Center for its list of "World's Best Attractions."

The story of this site—from a recruit training center to a world-class performing arts center—is similar to the story of Beautiful Island Station: people of Kaohsiung have rewritten military memories from the martial law era into artistic memories. **A city's history of martial law does not disappear; it can be repurposed.**

The year Weiwu opened coincided with the 39th anniversary of the county upgrading to a special municipality and the 39th anniversary of the Beautiful Island Incident. From the Daguangpu roundabout to Beautiful Island Station, from the Weiwu military camp to the Weiwu Theater, this city has spent nearly 40 years stitching its wounds into its own shape.

## 2.71 million people in 38 districts

In April 2026, Kaohsiung City had a population of 2.71 million, making it the third-largest special municipality in Taiwan (after New Taipei and Taipei) [^30]. Fengshan District was the most populous with 355,000 people, while Maolin District had the fewest at 1,867. The 2.71 million residents were spread across 38 districts covering 2,951 square kilometers, from Qijin Sandbar to the Namaxia indigenous communities, with elevations ranging from 0 meters to over 3,000 meters.

Population structure: Hokkien speakers are the majority (due to Han migration after the Qing court established Fengshan County in 1684); Hakka speakers are centered in Meinong (the right part of Liuhui); the Makadai people were originally indigenous inhabitants of the "Eight Communities of Fengshan" who lived on the plains, and after Sinicization during the Qing dynasty, they integrated into the Hokkien population; former residents from outside Taiwan are concentrated in Zuoying military villages (former Navy bases, the largest cluster of such villages in Taiwan) and Guomao Community (a state-subsidized housing project built on a large former military village in the south); mountainous indigenous peoples are concentrated in Maolin (Rukai), Taoyuan (Puyuh/Pa'uan, La'aluwa), and Namaxia (Pa'uan, Kanakanafu).

Compared to Keelung with 360,000 people, Kaohsiung is more than seven times larger; compared to Taipei with 2.4 million people, Kaohsiung has slightly more. However, the uniqueness of this city is not in its population size, but in its scope: **the West Coast Bay, Namaxia communities, Qijin ferry, and Meinong paper umbrellas coexist within the same administrative coordinates**. It is the only special municipality in Taiwan that simultaneously includes urban core areas, heavy industrial zones, Hakka enclaves, and three indigenous groups from mountainous regions.

> **✦** "The 11 original districts of Kaohsiung City and the 27 townships/cities of the former Kaohsiung County merged into 38 districts (including 3 special municipality indigenous areas)." (Wikipedia article on administrative divisions of Kaohsiung City [^17])

## 2018 "Han Stream," 2020 Recall

A new chapter was added to the political history of this city after its administrative elevation by 39 years.

"On November 24, 2018, Han Kuo-yu defeated Chen Chi-mai in the Nine-in-One election with 892,545 votes and a 53.87% vote share to become the third Mayor of Kaohsiung City, and was inaugurated on December 25 of that year" [^31]. Han Kuo-yu's victory overturned the Democratic Progressive Party's governance in Kaohsiung for two decades. The term "Han Stream" swept from south to north that year; his speaking style, slogans ("Goods go out, people come in, Kaohsiung makes great wealth"), and image were completely different from those of previous mayors of Kaohsiung.

But the reversal was short-lived. In May 2019, Han Kuo-yu announced his candidacy for the 2020 presidential election and was criticized as having "no heart for municipal governance." Following his defeat in the January 2020 presidential election to Tsai Ing-wen, Kaohsiung residents began signing petitions for his recall.

"The recall vote was held on June 6, 2020, with a final tally of 939,090 votes in favor and 25,051 votes against, with a voter turnout of 42.14%" [^32].

"**Han Kuo-yu became the first mayor of a county-level city in the Republic of China to be recalled**" [^32].

The 939,090 "yes" votes were nearly 50,000 more than the 892,545 votes he received during his election in 2018. The same group of Kaohsiung residents had elected and then recalled the same person within two years. A reporter's headline stated plainly: "**The first local official in Taiwan history to be recalled**" [^33].

The recall was not the focus of this article. The focus is that the recall on June 6, 2020, is part of the same political continuum as the Dagangpu Ring Road on December 10, 1979, and the Beautiful Island Station on September 14, 2008. **Kaohsiung residents do not trust "gods."** In 1979, they stood up to oppose martial law; in 2020, they stood up again to recall their incumbent mayor. The physical manifestation of public opinion in this city is more direct than in any other city in Taiwan.

## At 4:30 AM, Black Vultures Behind CSCO Smokestacks

Returning to the opening scene.

At Pre-Zhen Fishing Port at 4:30 AM, tuna longliners were unloading cargo; at 5 AM, the Gushan ferry transported the first batch of commuters from Qijin; at 6 AM, Beautiful Island Station, beneath the Dome of Light, was completely clean before the morning rush began; and at 7 AM, a bus from Zuoying High-Speed Rail Station took a group northward to Taipei for work.

Two kilometers away, smoke billowed from the smokestacks of CSCO Xiaogang Plant. A little further on, the water of Houjin Creek flowed slowly toward Kaohsiung Port. The smell of this water is different—from when residents of Houjin protested the plant in 1987 to when the Fifth Light refinery shut down in 2015. A little further still, Dalinpu had not yet been resettled.

Farther away, the mornings of Malulin, Taoyuan, and Namaxia, home to the Rukai, Bunun, Kanakanafu, and La'aluwa tribes, began above 1,000 meters in the mountains. Residents of permanent housing after the '88 typhoon woke up in the cedar forests, while indigenous people remaining in their ancestral villages awoke on the mountain.

So many things happened in this city one morning because the 2010 county-city merger placed areas that previously belonged to different geographical units into a single administrative framework. **The area from Qijin Sandbar to Namaxia tribes is the most concrete physical manifestation of Taiwan's administrative elevation.**

From Taipei, Kaohsiung is an industrial port city combined with a democratic event and a military base (Weiwu). From the sea, Kaohsiung is where grandchildren of female workers from the 1968 Export Processing Zone work at TSMC; it is where sons of people from the 1990 Houjin referendum are professors; and one of the lawyers from the 1979 Daguangpu tear gas incident later became a president. From Pre-Zhen Fishing Port at 4:30 AM, Kaohsiung is a port city **where the entire world is sleeping, but this city has already started working.**

Next time you go to Kaohsiung, don't just visit Liuhe Night Market or Building 85. Try taking the Gushan ferry at five in the morning to Qijin to see fishing boats docking. Or take the Kaohsiung MRT Red Line to R8 San Duo Business District, then transfer to the Orange Line to R10/O5 Beautiful Island Station and look up at the Dome of Light. Or go even farther by renting a car from Meino to Namaxia, driving through the mountains along the Nanzi Creek. Then you will remember one thing: **the wound this city left on December 10, 1979, is reflected in the reflection of the aluminum roof at Weiwu in 2018, the official naming of the 15th and 16th tribes in 2014, and the 930,000 votes from the recall election in 2020; all of this circulates within this city of 2.71 million people.**

Kaohsiung is not the lower body of Taiwan. Kaohsiung is a city where three layers—Taiwan's post-war industrialization, democratization, and cultural transformation—exist physically at the same time.
