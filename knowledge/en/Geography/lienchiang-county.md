# Lianjiang County: The County Furthest from Taiwan, Closest to the Cold War

> **30-second overview:** Lianjiang County is one of the two counties in Fujian Province currently administered by the Republic of China (ROC), comprising 4 townships, 5 main islands, and 36 islets, with a total area of 28.8 square kilometers. Nangan is about 50 kilometers from Fuzhou city center, and just 9.25 kilometers from the northern tip of the Beihai Peninsula (the closest point to the Chinese mainland), yet over 200 kilometers from Taipei. After the end of the Chinese Civil War in 1949, "Lianjiang County" was split in two: the county seat remained in the CCP-controlled Lianjiang, while the ROC government relocated to Nangan. From 1956, it entered a 36-year period of wartime administrative controls, including a 9 PM curfew, a two-story building height limit, and the requirement for Matsu residents to obtain travel permits to visit Taiwan. Wartime administrative controls in Kinmen and Matsu were lifted on November 7, 1992. In 2012, a referendum in Matsu passed to establish a gaming zone—the first successful local referendum in Taiwan. Thirteen years later, no casinos have been built. Today, 13,646 people live here, and the _Mazu language_ (Eastern Min) has been lost by 94% of the third generation in Taoyuan's Bade City.

## Looking North from the Mountain Top of Nangan, Fuzhou City Lies 16 Kilometers Away

If you stand on the ridge of Jiushou Village in Nangan Township and look northward, on a clear day the outline of downtown Fuzhou emerges on the horizon.

From Nangan to the nearest coast of the mainland (Beijia Peninsula), the straight-line distance is only 9.25 kilometers. Pushing further inland to downtown Fuzhou, it's about 50 kilometers[^1]. Turning around to look southward at the Taiwan mainland, the straight-line distance to Taipei exceeds 200 kilometers, and the ferry from Keelung Port to Nangan's Fuhao Port takes 8 to 10 hours.

The geographical reality of Lianjiang County is this: **it is nearly thirteen times closer to Fuzhou on the mainland than to the capital of its own country.**

![Montage of Matsu islands scenery, including Qinbi Village, Dongyin Lighthouse, battlefield trenches, and blue tears. Composite by Sleepingstar, 2012.](/article-images/geography/matsu-islands-montage.webp)

Yet this county is named "Lianjiang." Within Fujian Province of the People's Republic of China, there is also a "Lianjiang County," with its seat in Fengcheng Town downstream of Fuzhou, which was captured by the PLA in the final summer of the Chinese Civil War in 1949. The ROC government of Lianjiang County then retreated to the Matsu Islands and re-established the county office in Nangan in August 1953[^2]. **One county split in two—one half remaining under the Chinese Communist Party, the other becoming a county of Taiwan. This is unique among all 22 counties and cities in Taiwan.**

Lianjiang County does not belong to Taiwan Province; together with Kinmen County, it belongs to "Fujian Province." This province remains one of the two provinces in the ROC constitutional framework, although since 1998 the provincial government has been streamlined, and in 2019 the Fujian Provincial Government was officially defunct, with its duties transferred to the Joint Services Center of the Ministry of Internal Affairs for Kinmen and Matsu[^3]. Nevertheless, on identity cards, in household registration systems, and on map markings, people of Lianjiang are still registered as residents of "Fujian Province, Lianjiang County."

To understand this county, one must first set aside the intuition that "an island must belong to the nearest country." **Lianjiang is a county of Taiwan, but it has never been geographical Taiwan.**

## The Liangdao People Are 7,900 Years Old — Thousands of Years Older Than Confucius

In 2012, the Republic of China (ROC) military, during construction work on the northern part of Liangdao, unearthed two complete sets of human bones.

Archaeologist Chen Zhongyu and his team named them "Liangdao Person No. 1" and "Liangdao Person No. 2." Mitochondrial DNA analysis revealed that Liangdao Person No. 1 shares a common matrilineal ancestry with Taiwan's Tsai (Indigenous) and Amis peoples, and may be among the earliest ancestors of the Austronesian language family[^4].

Carbon-14 dating of the Liangdao Island Tail archaeological site indicates an age of 7,000 to 8,300 years. In 2023, the Ministry of Culture designated it as **Taiwan's 11th national archaeological site**, and it is also **the earliest Neolithic site discovered within Taiwan's territory**[^5].

Liangdao itself is a military zone, off-limits to civilians. Archaeological excavations required negotiation with the ROC military for access windows, with soldiers standing guard during the dig. **A 7,900-year-old human bone was guarded by a Cold War-era military cordon for over 70 years** — this contrast itself encapsulates the compressed history of Lianjiang County.

The Song Dynasty's Liang Keqia's _Three Mountains Gazetteer_ already records "Gan Tang" (the former name of the Matsu archipelago). The Dapu Stone Carvings in Nangan's Tieban Village bear the inscription "Zhongtong Exchange Coins, 20 Guan," referring to Zhongtong coins from the Yuan Dynasty, proving that settlements existed in Nangan during the Yuan Dynasty[^6]. Fishermen from the Mingdong region settled on different islands according to their hometowns: Dongju and Xiju belonged to Changle County, while Dongyi and Xiyi belonged to Luoyuan County[^7]. Fujianese fishermen have lived on these islands for over a millennium — they were not "discovered" by outsiders.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The mainstream narrative of Taiwan's outlying islands is "battlefield tourism," "blue-eyed tears," and "supply ships." This framing compresses Matsu into a Cold War artifact, as if the island only began to exist in 1949. The significance of the Liangdao archaeological discovery lies in stretching the timeline: **human activity in this sea area has spanned 8,000 years, and the 75 years of the Chinese Civil War are merely the final, brief segment**. The ancestral graves of Mingdong fishermen, the stone sarcophagi of Mazu legends, and the coins of the Song and Yuan dynasties all predate the Cold War — they are not annotations to Cold War history.

## The 41-Character Inscription of 1617

On the slope of Lao Tou Mountain at the southernmost tip of Dongju Island, there is a cliff-carved stone inscription.

In May 1617 (the 45th year of the Ming Wanli era), military commander Shen YouRong defended the Minhai coast and fiercely resisted Japanese pirates at Dongsha (today's Dongju Island), capturing 69 of them without losing a single soldier[^8]. In 1630 (the 3rd year of the Ming Chongzhen era), Imperial Secretary of Works Dong Yingju carved a stone stele to commemorate this battle. The 41 characters of the inscription read:

> **“After the vast wilderness of the strong Wanli era fell to the land, the sun rose in the east. Shen YouRong of Xingzhou captured sixty-nine Japanese pirates at the mountains of Dongsha without losing a single soldier. This is inscribed by Dong Yingju of the Min people.”**[^9]

This “Dapu Stone Carving” is the oldest and largest existing cliff-carved stone in the Matsu region, and is today designated a county-level historic relic of Lianjiang County. It was discovered in 1953 when the Republic of China military occupied Dongju and constructed defensive fortifications, at which time it was enclosed in wire mesh for protection[^10].

![Dongju Fuzheng Village settlement, 2011. Traditional Mazu granite houses adjacent to Lao Tou Mountain where the Dapu Stone Carving is located. Photo by WT-shared Shoestring.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Fuzheng_Village%2C_Dongju%2C_Matsu%2C_Taiwan.JPG/1280px-Fuzheng_Village%2C_Dongju%2C_Matsu%2C_Taiwan.JPG)

Shen YouRong is also recorded elsewhere in Taiwan's history. In 1604, he ordered the Dutch East India Company commander Verbeeck to withdraw from the Pescadores; this is commemorated on the “Shen YouRong Orders the Dutch Commander Verbeeck to Withdraw” stele, housed in the existing Pescadores Tianhou Temple, designated a national treasure-level cultural artifact in 2022[^11]. **The same Ming general, within 13 years, suppressed two maritime conflicts at opposite ends of the Taiwan Strait**: one against Japanese pirates, one against the Dutch.

During the Franco-Chinese War (1884–1885), French naval forces blockaded the Taiwan Strait and attacked Keelung; the Matsu archipelago, located offshore at the mouth of the Min River, was a possible supply point for the French. However, this war ultimately did not result in any actual attack on Matsu; direct contact between Matsu and modern warfare would have to wait another 70 years.

During the Qing Dynasty (1683–1895), Lianjiang County fell under the jurisdiction of Fuzhou Prefecture, Fujian Province, and the Matsu islands were administered as outer sea islands under Lianjiang County. For over 200 years during this period, Matsu was a group of islands where fishermen temporarily made their living; there was no county seat, no large-scale land reclamation, and no academies. **It was a maritime frontier of the Chinese coast, until 1949, when the Republic of China transformed this frontier into its own front line**.

## After 9 PM, No One Moves on the Entire Island

In July 1956, the Matsu War Zone Political Affairs Commission was established.

From that year until November 7, 1992, when the Matsu War Zone Political Affairs was terminated, Lianjiang County experienced **a complete 36 years of military-centralized rule**[^12]. Taiwan proper lifted martial law in 1987, but Matsu held on for another 4 to 5 years before ending the martial law system.

What was daily life like for local residents during these 36 years?

The National Cultural Heritage Database's entry "Curfew Hours, Military Orders Are Supreme" records verbatim several basic rules: "The curfew time is after 9 PM." "During curfew hours, no activities may be held anywhere on the island"; "Military and civilians going out must know the password, otherwise they will be caught — lightly punished with detention, severely with imprisonment"; "If a major accident occurs and it is absolutely necessary to go out, one must apply for a permit to the relevant unit, or inform the curfew password content in order to pass through"[^13]. The most direct sentence is written like this: "**Once a sentry detects anything unusual, he shouts 'the password'; if no response is received, he assumes it is the enemy and will shoot without hesitation**"[^13].

How many military personnel were there? At its peak, the Matsu Defense Command had a total strength of approximately **50,000 people**[^14], while the local resident population was about **10,000 people**, a military-to-civilian ratio of 5 to 1. A 28.8 square kilometer archipelago was home to the forces of 5 divisions.

Material life controls were also very detailed: radios and televisions had to be applied for before use; refrigerators, washing machines, electric rice cookers, and air conditioners were banned in Matsu; buildings were not allowed to exceed two stories; cameras could not be used freely; tires, life rings, and basketballs — these "floating objects" — were prohibited from being introduced, fearing residents would use them to swim to the mainland; firecrackers could not be set off, and betel nuts could not be chewed; pigeons could not be raised, and kites could not be flown[^15]. The Matsu area even issued its own military tickets, which had to be exchanged when traveling between Taiwan, Penghu, and Matsu.

When Chiang Kai-shek inspected Matsu in 1958, he personally inscribed four characters: "**On alert day and night**"[^16], meaning to remain vigilant at all times, even while sleeping. Today, you can still see this stone carving in Jiushan Park, Nangan Township, and the sign "Never Forget Being in Matsu" at the Matsu Pier, with slogans from the military government era scattered on the walls of the villages — "Ensure Matsu," "Recover the mainland territories," "Liberate our compatriots on the mainland"[^16]. **These slogans are physical traces left by 36 years of actual governance, not touristy decorations**.

On May 1, 1991, the Temporary Provisions Effective During the Period of Communist Rebellion were abolished; on November 7, 1992, the Matsu area officially ended the experimental period of war zone political affairs and lifted the martial law order[^12]. But even after the war zone political affairs ended, the people of Matsu still had to wait nearly two more years to come to Taiwan proper: it was not until May 13, 1994, that the entry and exit permits between Taiwan, Penghu, and Matsu were formally abolished by law.

## The "Passport" That Brought Matsu People to Taiwan 30 Years Ago

In a 2023 interview, current Lianjiang County Magistrate Wang Zhongming pulled out a yellowed document to show journalists.

That was the "Exit and Entry Permit" used by people from the Matsu Islands to travel to Taiwan proper before the martial law was lifted in 1992. He said: "This exit and entry permit was kind of like a passport; we had to apply for it every time we went to Taiwan[^17]."

To enter Taiwan proper, people from the Matsu Islands had to apply to the Ministry of the Interior's National Immigration Agency. Every detail—forms, photos, purpose, expected return date—had to be explained clearly. **For Matsu people entering Taiwan, this was administratively the same as foreigners entering Taiwan.** A county resident of the Republic of China having to obtain a passport-like document to visit their own capital is a case that is hard to find in world constitutional history.

This "passport" is a material legacy of wartime governance, and also a form of **physical embodiment of identity**: although you are a resident of Lianjiang County, Republic of China, you cannot freely enter other parts of the Republic of China. Between 1956 and 1994, people from the Matsu Islands were effectively "semi-foreigners" within the territory of the Republic of China.

> **✦** "If Taiwan doesn't want us, and we don't want to be Chinese, what should we do?"[^18]

This quote comes from a report by Mingrentang on the identity of young people in the Matsu Islands. It precisely articulates the existential dilemma of Lianjiang County in 2026: we are administratively part of Taiwan, but our ancestry, language, geography, and beliefs are closer to Fujian; we have a democratically elected county magistrate and voting rights, but our history was shaped by the Cold War frontier.

## The 2012 Gaming Referendum, 13 Years With No Follow-Up

After the end of wartime political affairs, the people of Matsu tried another form of "self-determination": local civic referendums.

On July 7, 2012, Lianjiang County held a referendum on "Whether Matsu Should Establish an International Tourist Resort with Attached Tourist Casinos." There were 7,762 registered voters, with a turnout of 40.76% (3,164 votes cast). The vote passed with 1,795 approvals (57.24%) against 1,341 rejections (42.76%), a margin of 454 votes[^19].

This was **Taiwan's first successful local civic referendum**, the third local referendum since the _Referendum Act_ took effect in 2004. The two gaming referendums in Penghu County in 2009 and 2016 were both rejected by majority vote (56.44% opposed / 60.1% opposed); the 2023 gaming referendum in Kinmen was likewise rejected by a large margin. **Only the people of Matsu said: Let's give it a try**[^20].

The reason was economic. Matsu's transportation was inconvenient, tourism had long off-seasons, and young people flowed out to Taiwan's main island for education and work. At that time, the county government partnered with Taiwan's Hau De Development Company to plan an international tourist resort in Beigan, featuring casinos, five-star hotels, and conference centers, positioning it as a Northeast Asian gaming hub close to both the Chinese mainland and Japan/Korea.

The referendum passed. And then there was no "then."

The local referendum only cleared the first hurdle. To actually open casinos, the _Gaming Casino Administration Ordinance Draft_ still needed to pass the Legislative Yuan. In December 2013, the Executive Yuan reviewed the relevant draft, but with differing positions from all sides, it reached a stalemate and was dismissed without passing[^21]. In 2015, Taiwan Hau De Company quietly withdrew from the Matsu gaming development, citing "the gaming special law has not passed." **As of today in 2026, 13 years have passed, the casino has not been built, and the gaming special law has not been enacted**[^22].

Lianjiang County Government Secretary-General Zhang Longde said in 2017: "**In recent years, Matsu no longer relies on, nor needs to rely on, the gaming dream, but instead develops tourism on its own**"[^22]. This sounds like letting go, but the fact that the central government blocked things for 13 years remains: **the people of Matsu passed their decision through democratic procedure, but without coordination from the central legislature, local will just spins in place**[^22].

This case is a meaningful observation point for Taiwan's constitutional system. Local referendums are democratic, but democracy's range was cut off at the central legislature. The people of Matsu spent 36 years emerging from the battlefield, and then another 13 years discovering that their decision still could not get past that wall.

## The Same Language, Three Names: Hokkien, Fuzhounese, and Matsu

The mother tongue of Matsu people is completely different from the Taiwanese Hokkien, Hakka, and indigenous languages of Taiwan's main island.

It is **Hokkien**, nearly identical to the Fuzhounese spoken in the city of Fuzhou[^23]. Most Matsu people are descended from Longyue County, with some from Luoyuan and various counties of Fuzhou, so this language is also called "Longyue dialect," "Fuzhounese," or "Matsu dialect"—different names for the same Hokkien language. It is completely unintelligible with Hokkien (the Taiwanese Hokkien of Taiwan's main island): the two systems have been separate for over a millennium in phonology, vocabulary, and grammar.

In 2017, the Republic of China government officially designated Matsu as one of Taiwan's national languages[^24]. In 2019, the _National Languages Development Act_ was enacted, making Matsu a legally protected language. **Taiwan's mother tongue family thus gained a "fourth" branch**—Hokkien, Hakka, the indigenous languages, and now the Hokkien branch of Matsu.

But language loss is extremely severe.

A straightforward English study of Matsu language published by Taiwan Insight states: "**Less than 30% of Matsu households currently speak in their mother tongue. Some elders believe that this is a dying language.**" (Currently, fewer than 30% of Matsu households use their mother tongue. Some elders believe this is a dying language.) The same article also records another quote: "**The ban on speaking in dialects was painful and hastened the crisis of language extinction.**" (The past policy of banning dialects was painful and accelerated the crisis of language extinction.)[^25]

The local language situation in Matsu is much more severe than on Taiwan's main island. The Wikipedia entry on Matsu language verbatim states: "**In recent years, with the opening of Matsu to tourism and the large-scale migration of people to Taiwan's main island, many local Matsu residents have begun speaking Hokkien-Taiwanese. The Matsu dialect, as a mother tongue, faces a crisis of decline, with younger generations speaking it less or not at all.**"[^26]

More urgent figures come from Taoyuan's Bade City: "**The Matsu language has been lost at a rate of up to 94% over the past three generations, placing it in the 'severely endangered' category.**"[^17]

Taoyuan's Bade City is the largest settlement of Matsu people on Taiwan's main island. In the 1960s and 70s, as fisheries declined and the textile industry rose, large numbers of Matsu people went to work in textile factories in Taoyuan's Bade City. Today, **the permanent population of Matsu Island is about 13,000 people, while the estimated 50,000 to 60,000 Matsu descendants in Taoyuan's Bade City number 4 to 5 times the island's population**[^17]. The Matsu people have a self-identification saying: "**Four townships, five islands, plus Bade.**"

The root cause of language loss can be traced back to wartime political affairs 36 years ago. Starting in 1956, after the National Army moved in, there was a strong push to promote Mandarin and literacy campaigns, with the motivation of "military-civil cooperation and joint combat." The Matsu Defense Command set up "instructors" in each village, forcing adults to learn Mandarin and children to study and become literate[^27]. Mandarin literacy was a public health success, but for the Matsu language, it was a linguistic earthquake.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The popular science narrative says "Matsu language and Fuzhounese are both Hokkien," which is linguistically correct, but it places Matsu language within a "Chinese dialect island" coordinate system. A more accurate perspective is: Matsu language is a **Hokkien coordinate on Taiwan's linguistic map**, just like Kinmen dialect (belonging to the same Hokkien branch but with different tones), Yilan Toucheng dialect, Pingtung Manzhou dialect, and Penghu Bisha dialect—all part of Taiwan's mother tongue diversity. When Matsu language loses 94%, Taiwan loses **an internal dimension of itself as a multilingual society**.

## Tourism Before and After: Matsu, Blue Tears, and Qinbi Stone Houses

In the tourism promotion of Lianjiang County, three anchors appear most frequently: Matsu, Blue Tears, and Qinbi. Each has its own original story, and its own story after tourism development.

**Mazu's story** begins with a legend. During the Song Dynasty, Lin Moying threw herself into the sea to rescue her drowning brothers, and her body drifted ashore near Nangan Island. Fishermen pulled her from the water, admired her filial piety, and buried her on the coast.

There are two versions of what happened next. The version on the Nangan Township Office website says: "**After the people of Meizhou learned of this, they crossed the sea to Nangan to bring Mazu's remains back, leaving only the tablet of her tomb.**"[^28] The legend of Matsu Tianhou Temple says her remains still lie in the spirit cave beneath the main hall of the Nangan Tianhou Temple. In 1963 (ROC Year 52), this was widely circulated locally: "**In the 52nd year of the Republic of China, disrespectful military engineers laid tiles over the tombstone; the next day, the tiles on the tombstone were found shattered, while the rest of the ground remained intact.**"[^29] **Both legends coexist**, and neither side can convince the other.

The name "Mazu Island" was later passed down orally and evolved into "Matsu Island," giving Lianjiang County its name of "Matsu." Matsu Tianhou Temple (Mangang Tianhou Temple) is located in Nangan's Mangang, facing the sea, and is the most active religious center in all of Matsu. Every year on the 9th day of the 9th lunar month, Mazu's ascension festival is celebrated, distinctly different from the pilgrimage festivals of Beigung Mazu and Dajia Mazu: Matsu's Mazu culture centers around the **tomb** (the end of Mazu's life), which is a core element absent from the branch Mazu beliefs brought by Minnan people from mainland China.

**The story of Blue Tears** is a case of a scientific phenomenon becoming a tourism engine.

The scientific name of Blue Tears is _Noctiluca scintillans_ (glowworm, glow algae), a single-celled organism in the dinoflagellate phylum. During the Minjiang River's annual flood season, terrestrial inorganic nutrients are carried into the sea, causing diatoms to reproduce in large numbers, and the glowworm feeds on diatoms, leading to a population explosion. When waves, oars, or human footsteps disturb the water, the glowworm emits a brief blue bioluminescent light, each flash lasting about 80 milliseconds[^30]. Research by National Taiwan Ocean University found that water temperatures below 27°C are key to the outbreak; the outbreak season typically runs from April to June, sometimes extending into March or September in certain years.

![Night view of Blue Tears in the Matsu Islands, May 2014. The blue bioluminescent light emitted by Noctiluca scintillans when disturbed, each lasting about 80 milliseconds. Photo by e_ella.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/0/0e/Blue_Tears_in_the_Matsu_Islands.jpg/1280px-Blue_Tears_in_the_Matsu_Islands.jpg)

After the rise of social media in the 2010s, "Blue Tears" became an overnight sensation. Matsu's annual visitor count rose from 210,000 (2019, pre-pandemic) to 224,000 (2023, post-pandemic), with Blue Tears being the main driver of the peak season[^31]. The Beihai Tunnel was originally built in 1968 as part of the "Beihai Project"—an underwater dock for patrol boats. Today, tourists row boats into the tunnel to see the blue light inside, making it the most iconic tourist experience in Matsu. **Built by the military in 1968 to hide speedboats for guerrilla warfare, the underwater tunnel became the best viewing spot for Blue Tears in the 2020s.**

But behind the Blue Tears outbreak is another story. Red _N. scintillans_ is associated with red tides, consuming dissolved oxygen in the water and having destructive effects on fisheries. Scientists believe the intensified outbreaks may be related to coastal industrialization and agricultural fertilizer use on the Chinese side. In other words, **the Blue Tears seen by Matsu residents may be proportional to the pollution density on the other side of the strait.**

**The story of Qinbi Village** is the most dramatic.

Qinbi in Nangan was originally a Min-dong stone village. "The Matsu dialect pronunciation of the word for 'turtle' is 'qin-jian,' and since the settlement is built among mountains and seas with the cliffs embedded, it is called 'Qinbi.'"[^32] About 200 years ago, the Chen family from Longxi County in Fujian crossed the sea to settle here. "On a resource-scarce island, using unworked granite as building materials, they built single-story, double-story structures with stones stacked to hold the tiles, with small, high windows to withstand wind and sea pirates."[^33] The stone blocks on the roof were for typhoon protection, and the small windows were for defense against sea pirates—**the physical environment directly determined the architectural vocabulary.** After the war, men fished and women ran shops, and as the economy grew, they developed representative Min-dong construction techniques such as "human-character masonry" and "worker-character masonry."

![Granite stone houses in Qinbi Village, Nangan, September 2024. Min-dong "human-character masonry," four-sloped red-tiled roofs, roof stones for typhoon protection, small high windows for pirate defense—architectural vocabulary directly determined by the physical environment. Photo by Foxy1219.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/5/5b/Qinbi_Village_2024-09-11.jpg/1280px-Qinbi_Village_2024-09-11.jpg)

Then in the 1970s, the fishing industry collapsed. The catch in Nangan declined sharply, and many residents moved to Taiwan's main island to make a living. "**During the 1970s fishing industry crisis, people migrated to Taiwan for survival, leaving behind a gradually desolate and decaying settlement.**"[^34] By the late 1970s (ROC Year 70s), the entire Qinbi Village had fewer than 3 to 4 households remaining.

In 2000 (ROC Year 89), the county government launched the "Qinbi Settlement Ancient House Restoration Project," completed in 2001 (ROC Year 90). "**Over 200 years, it experienced birth, decline, and rebirth; shops became tourist centers, and homes turned into cafes or guesthouses.**"[^35] Today, Qinbi is an Instagram check-in hotspot, selling coffee and souvenirs inside the Min-dong stone houses. The abandoned village of just three households now attracts tens of thousands of tourists annually.

> **✦** "**Leaving behind a gradually desolate and decaying settlement.**"[^34]

Placed side by side before and after the restoration of Qinbi, this line is deeply moving. Tourism saved the stone houses, but also turned the houses from residents' homes into backdrops for tourists. Which fate is better? No one has the right to speak for Qinbi.

## After the Soldiers Left: Black-mouthed Terns, Sika Deer, and the Dongju Lighthouse

After the end of martial law, another change emerged in Matsu: **the islands that soldiers left behind naturally returned to their original state**.

The **black-mouthed tern** is perhaps the most dramatic example. With only about 200 individuals remaining worldwide, it is listed as a Critically Endangered species (CR — the highest threat level) by the IUCN[^36]. Each year, roughly 10 to 20 birds (5 to 10% of the global population) fly to the Matsu archipelago to breed, making this site the **second-largest breeding ground in the world after Zhejiang Province in China**.

The breeding sites are located across eight uninhabited islands within the Matsu Islands Black-mouthed Tern Nature Reserve: Dongyin Twin Reefs, Beigan Sanlianyu, Zhongdao, Iron Tip Island, Baimiao, Jinsha, Nan'ao Liuquan Reef, and Jugong She Mountain — most of which were strictly off-limits to civilians during the military administration period. **By keeping humans out, nature was given space to thrive**. In recent years, threats have shifted to fishing boats illegally entering the reserve at night (resulting in mass nest abandonment in both 2020 and 2021), attacks by wild peregrine falcons, and interference from military exercises.

**Dajia Island** is another example. Located north of Beigan, sika deer were introduced to the island in January 1997 (starting with 13 individuals). After the Republic of China Armed Forces fully withdrew in 1998, the deer flourished without human interference. Today, approximately 200 to 250 sika deer inhabit the island, earning it the nickname "Taiwan's version of Nara Park"[^37]. An island that was once a military stronghold has become one of Taiwan's most unique deer habitats.

The **Dongju Island Lighthouse** (Dongchuan Lighthouse) tells an even earlier story. Built and first lit on July 12, 1872 (the 11th year of the Tongzhi era), it was constructed at the request of the British following the signing of the _Nanjing Treaty_ after the Opium Wars, which opened coastal areas for trade. Built from granite, standing approximately 19.5 meters tall, and constructed over three years, it is **the first lighthouse in the Taiwan-Fujian region built entirely of granite**[^38]. Local residents refer to the white-walled building adjacent to the lighthouse as "Baimao City," which is now a designated national historic site. The area around the lighthouse also features cannons and military installations and is now open to tourists.

8,000 years of human habitation on the islands, 150 years of lighthouse history, 36 years of wartime governance, and 15 years of blue-eyed tear tourism. The timeline of Lianjiang County is layered upon itself across just 28.8 square kilometers. **The small size of this county compared to the density of its accumulated history is entirely disproportionate**.

## Back to Nangan, Back to Fuzhou 16 Kilometers Away

Back to the opening scene.

Looking north from the hills of Shoushou Village in Nangan, the cityscape of Fuzhou floats on the horizon on clear days. Looking south, Taiwan's main island lies 200 kilometers away. The "resting swords before dawn" stone carving atop the hill remains in place, untouched since Chiang Kai-shek inscribed it in 1958. But what we see when we look out from here today is the signal tower of China Mobile, Fuzhou's high-rises, and the cross-sea bridge to Pingtan Island—not enemy territory. **The Cold War has not ended; it has merely changed its form of existence.**

In April 2026, the registered population of Lianjiang County was 13,646[^39]. This is slightly more than the 10,000 residents during the wartime period, but nearly 4/5 fewer than the 50,000 Nationalist soldiers at the height of military rule. Most young people study and work on Taiwan's main island, registering their household in Matsu and returning home to vote during Spring Festival and elections. The 50,000 to 60,000 descendants of Matsu people in Beitou, Taoyuan, are four to five times the number of permanent residents on the island. **The "people" of this county have always been elsewhere; the island is only for Spring Festival and elections.**

The Matsu language has been lost, with fewer than 30% of families still using it, and 94% of the third generation in Beitou, Taoyuan has been lost. Blue tears bring in 200,000 tourists annually, but locals remember the light controls. Qinbi Village has been restored into an Instagram check-in hotspot, but residents who left in the 1970s have not returned. The gaming referendum passed in 2012 has seen no progress by 2025. **All the stories of Lianjiang County are stories "after the soldiers left," but after the soldiers left, it did not become the imagined future.**

Next time you visit Matsu, don't just look at the blue tears.

Go to Dajia Island to see the 7,900-year-old human bone display (if the viewing window is open). Go to the old headland in Dongju to see the 41 characters of the Dapu stone carving, where Japanese pirates from 1617 and the barbed wire of 1953 lie on the same rock. Go to the Matsu Tianhou Temple in Mangan, Nangan, to light incense at the temple said to have the goddess Mazu's sacred cave underground. Go to Qinbi Village in Beigan to drink a cup of coffee, remembering that in these stone houses of 200 years, people once fished, ran businesses, left, and never returned.

Then return to that hillside and look north.

Fuzhou is 16 kilometers away. Taiwan is 200 kilometers away. **This distance will not change**, and this is the most physical legacy the Cold War has left for the people of Matsu: geography. All of Lianjiang County fits within this geographical position.
