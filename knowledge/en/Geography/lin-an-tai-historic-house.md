> **30-second overview:** The Lin An-tai Historic House originally stood on Siwei Road in Da'an District. It was dismantled in 1977 due to the widening of Dunhua South Road, moved through interim locations including the Anhe Road warehouse and under the Xintian Road elevated bridge, and was finally reassembled in Binjiang Park. It preserved some material evidence of the Qing-era Anxi immigrant compound and Minnan courtyard, while also exposing the unresolved problem that relocation preservation cannot fully carry away the original site's landscape and way of life.

In 1977, at No. 141, Siwei Road, Da'an District, Taipei, a house that had stood for about two hundred years began to be dismantled. It was not excavators that arrived first, but windows, furniture, roof tiles, wooden frames, bricks, and stones, each piece removed from its original position one by one. That year, the widening project of Dunhua South Road approached the Lin An-tai Historic House. [^1]

The straight-line distance from the original site to Binjiang Park is about ten kilometers. Yet this journey took ten years before the historic house could be re-erected on new land. [^2] The Lin An-tai Historic House thus became a story not easily summarized by "successful preservation." It was indeed preserved, and it indeed lost its original geography, neighborhood, and way of life.

> **Curator's note:** A house can be moved, but the relationship between the house and the land cannot be packed up all at once.

## First There Was a Person, Then a House

In 1754, Lin Qinming (Lin An-tai Historic House), from Anxi County in Fujian Province, brought his family across the sea to Taiwan. His fourth son, Lin Zhineng, later engaged in business in Bangka, where he established the Rongtai Store. After accumulating wealth, the Lin family built a compound on today's Siwei Road in Da'an District. The main hall was completed around 1783 to 1785, and the wing rooms were finished in 1822 to 1823. [^3]

"An-tai" was not a name added later for tourism. According to the official historical account, it is derived from the "An" of Anxi and the "Tai" of the Rongtai Store. A house name that places one's hometown and business on the same plaque also records how the immigrant family settled in Taipei. [^3]

Before becoming a museum, this house was a container for the Lin family's daily life, with a main hall, wing rooms, front courtyard, and pond, as well as the sounds of family members coming and going, cooking, washing clothes, and playing every day. A 2014 press release from the Taipei City Government's Ministry of Civil Affairs preserved the memories of Lin Qinglong (Lin An-tai Historic House), a descendant of the Lin family, recalling his childhood. He remembered being born in the historic house and growing up among the lattice doors, ancestral tablets, and the front courtyard. [^4]

If one only looks at the roof ridges, brick walls, and carvings, the historic house would become a beautiful specimen, and the fact that Lin family members once lived within would fade into the background. The truly irreplaceable aspect of the Lin An-tai Historic House lies in the fact that it was simultaneously a home, an ancestral house, the center of rural life, and later a piece of private land cut into by road construction.

## The City Needs a Road, the House Needs a Location

In 1976, the Taipei City Government faced the choice of demolishing the Lin An-tai Historic House due to the widening of Dunhua South Road. At that time, the historic house had not yet been listed in the historic site preservation registry, and part of the building was located within the planned road construction zone. The official research report records that about three-fifths of the rear buildings fell within the project scope, covering approximately 170 square meters, thus sparking a debate over on-site preservation versus relocation reconstruction. [^5]

This debate was not as simple as "preservationists versus developers." Some suggested rerouting the road, changing to an underground passage, or building an elevated highway, while others advocated dismantling and rebuilding elsewhere. Experts, architects, government agencies, and the Lin family each faced different costs. A road is a public facility of the city, while an ancestral home is a living space that a family cannot replace with public engineering language. [^5]

In 1967, the Ministry of the Interior convened relevant departments and experts to discuss the preservation of the Lin An-tai Historic House, including transportation, education, documentation, and urban planning. The official research report records that the meeting proposed the conclusion of on-site preservation, but the Taipei City Government subsequently pushed forward the relocation plan. [^5]

In 1978, the Lin family agreed to the demolition. This decision cannot be simplified into the Lin family voluntarily donating the historic house, nor can it be written as the government unilaterally carrying out a rescue. The official research records the tug-of-war between property rights, compensation, living conditions, and the planned road zone. When a house is not listed as a historic site, there is no fair answer to what strength a family can use to ask the city to stop. [^5]

## The House's First Move Was Actually Three Moves

According to a press release from the Taipei City Government's Ministry of Civil Affairs, the dismantling project that began in 1977 first moved the structural components to a temporary warehouse on Anhe Road, then to under the Xintian Road elevated bridge, and finally found a reconstruction site in the Binjiang Street area. From dismantling to rebuilding, the entire moving process took about ten years and was not completed in one go. [^4]

The Taipei Times coverage made this process sound more like a disordered logistics project. The dismantling of the historic house took five months, the components were delayed at the Anhe Road warehouse due to land disputes, then moved to under the Xintian Road elevated bridge, where the wooden frames and columns were damaged by leaking water. Six years later, the government found a new site in Binjiang Park. [^2]

The difficulty of moving was not just about distance. The historic house was originally a directional and orderly structural system. Roof tiles pressed against the roof frame, wooden columns and brick walls interlocked, and doors and windows matched daily use dimensions. When each piece was taken apart, the original relationships were also broken.

Official research data indicates that after multiple relocations and reassemblies, some old wooden components were damaged, and the original roof tiles were no longer used. [^6] This detail is more worth remembering than "complete preservation." Preservation is not about sending every piece of material safely to a new home, but about deciding which traces can still be honestly preserved after the loss has already occurred.

For engineers or restoration workers, the challenges of this case can be broken down into several levels. The first level is identification, where every roof tile, every piece of wood, and every brick and stone must be recorded with positional information before dismantling. The second level is preservation, where components cannot simply be piled together, but must face moisture, transportation, and waiting-induced damage. The third level is reassembly, where the new site's foundation, orientation, drainage, and usage needs force restorers to make choices between old materials and new engineering.

Therefore, coding is not simply a construction technique, but a way of converting architectural memory into traceable data. It allows later generations to know where each component originally was, and lets people see which parts have already been lost. If there were only photographs after reconstruction, readers would easily think the house had never been interrupted. Saving dismantling records, material lists, and damage conditions together is the responsible technical documentation for historical buildings.

This also explains why relocation preservation cannot be judged by the final appearance alone. Academic research points out that on-site preservation is usually considered the best way to preserve historical buildings, while relocation preservation is an unavoidable measure under the conflict between urban development and cultural preservation. The research summarizes 28 relocation preservation cases from the Japanese colonial period to the present, reminding us that relocated buildings may lose their original environment and face questions of material authenticity and engineering technology. [^7]

Therefore, the Lin An-tai Historic House in Binjiang Park is not simply a "copy of the original site" or a "fake historic site with only the appearance left." It is a historical building that was once dismantled, damaged, reinterpreted, and finally composed of partial old materials and new engineering decisions. Its authenticity is not only about whether the bricks are old, but also about whether people are willing to speak out about the disconnection caused by relocation.

## The Original Feng Shui Landscape Could Not Be Moved

The Lin An-tai Historic House originally faced northeast and southwest, with a mountain called Anshan in front, protective hills on both sides, and a crescent pond in the main courtyard representing the Ming Tang. These geographical relationships are not decorative backgrounds, but a traditional folk method of understanding direction, wind, water, and family order. [^3]

After being moved to Binjiang Park, the original geographical environment no longer exists. The official historical account does not pretend that this gap can be completely eliminated, but records the current site with an artificial hill called Guruzhu Mingshan, slopes, and gardens symbolically recreating the original Anshan and Ming Tang. [^3] The English official history page also clearly states that after the historic house was moved away from Siwei Road, the original feng shui landscape changed, and the current garden is a re-presentation of that landscape relationship. [^8]

There is a subtle but crucial difference here. Re-presentation is not restoration. An artificial hill can help people understand the original feng shui vocabulary, but it cannot summon back the hills, fields, neighboring houses, and daily life of the Qing era. The garden is therefore both a preservation project and a public acknowledgment: some things can only be explained, not transported.

The official architectural guide divides the park into blue and red groups of scenic spots, including Yue Mian Pond, Waiyu, Antai Hall, main hall, dougong brackets, cloud walls, Lugushiyuan Garden, Aibo Pavilion, Feishi Steps, and Guruzhu Mingshan. [^9] These names give today's visitors a path to walk, and turn the historic house from a family space into a public educational space.

## Three Scales: Materials, Landscape, and Public Memory

The preservation data of the Lin An-tai Historic House can be read at three scales. The smallest scale is materials, including wooden frames, bricks, tiles, stones, and doors and windows. This scale concerns whether the components are still present, whether the original positions can be identified, and how to compensate after damage. The middle scale is the architecture, including the main hall, wing rooms, front courtyard, Yue Mian Pond, and walls. This scale concerns whether the spatial order can still be understood. The largest scale is the city, including Siwei Road, road widening, warehouses, under the elevated bridge, and Binjiang Park. This scale concerns how a house is changed by public engineering, and who has the right to decide which place is worth preserving. [^5] [^6]

| Reading Scale | Observable Data                                                  | Problems That Preservation Cannot Omit                             |
| ------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Materials     | Wood, bricks, tiles, stones, doors and windows, and coding       | Proportion of old materials, damage status, and identifiability    |
| Architecture  | Main hall, wing rooms, front courtyard, Yue Mian Pond, and walls | Spatial orientation, living lines, and craftsmanship relationships |
| City          | Original site, relocation route, project land use, and new site  | Land relationships, decision-making power, and public memory       |

These three scales also explain why a single photograph cannot bear the entire preservation task. Photographs can record the appearance, surveys can record dimensions, coding can trace components, oral data can fill in life experiences, and official reports can leave the policy and engineering decision-making context. Different data cannot replace each other, but can correct each other. This layered data approach is still important for today's cultural heritage digitization, because a three-dimensional model on the internet without the original site, materials, and user context may still just be a beautiful model that has lost its historical relationship.

> **Curator's note:** Preservation is not about putting all data into one display case, but about letting different types of evidence match each other.

## From Ancestral Home to Public Daily Life

In 2000, the Lin An-tai Folk Culture Museum was officially opened to the public. In 2010, after the garden was expanded, it became one of the exhibition halls of the Taipei International Flora Expo. [^8] The house thus changed to another form of life: no longer serving only one family, but allowing strangers to come in and understand the Minnan residential architecture of the Qing dynasty, family life, and the changes in Taipei City.

The more the historic house looks like an easily accessible scenic spot, the more its opening will bring another contrast, and the easier it is for people to forget that it was not originally here. Visitors take photos by the crescent pond, seeing the garden in Binjiang Park and the reassembled courtyard. Without the historical account, research, and relocation records, the original Siwei Road would only recede into the background.

The better way to visit is to treat the route as a reduced preservation map. First, look at the walls and entrance, and note how the historic house uses doors, walls, and the front courtyard to divide the inside from the outside. Then walk to the Yue Mian Pond and the main hall, and observe the relationship between the water surface, the courtyard, and the main building. Finally, look at the roof ridges, dougong brackets, concave-convex ridge doors, and painted details, and put them back into the context of architectural craftsmanship and family life, rather than just treating them as decorations. The official guide lists these nodes, just providing a way to read the space without relying on a guide. [^9]

If the original site is also imagined, the visit will gain an invisible coordinate. Siwei Road is not an exhibit in the park, but the starting point for understanding why the house had to be moved. Binjiang Park is not a substitute photo of the original site, but a new public scene after reconstruction. Both places must exist at the same time, so that the history of the Lin An-tai Historic House will not be compressed into a beautiful ancient house. This way of reading also reminds us that visitors are not just watching a completed work, but identifying a city relationship that has been cut off, preserved, and re-explained. The walls at the entrance, the water surface in the courtyard, the scale of the main hall, and the decorations on the roof ridges each preserve different levels of information. Connecting them back to the original site and the relocation process will make it clear that the cultural value of the historic house is not only hidden in the most delicate carvings.

The registration data on the National Cultural Heritage Database lists the Lin An-tai Historic House as a historical building, and records its original site, historical background, and opening status. [^10] This registration action itself also shows that cultural heritage is not only obtained by "looking very old," but through investigation, registration, research, and management, turning the value of a house into a memory that society can share.

> **Curator's note:** The most worth-seeing thing about the Lin An-tai Historic House today is not only the roof tiles and carvings, but also the cost of the verb "preservation" left on the spot.

## Institutional Memory Left by This Case

The influence of the Lin An-tai Historic House is not only within the walls of Binjiang Park. The official research of the Taipei City Government places it in the context of Taiwan's historical building relocation preservation, from the original site dispute, expert meetings, component dismantling, to the reassembly project, organizing it into a case that can be studied, compared, and reviewed. [^5] This makes the question of "whether historic sites can be moved" no longer just a temporary dispute of a single house, but a problem that public policy must deal with.

A 2006 master's thesis uses the Lin An-tai Historic House incident as a turning point, and organizes the relocation preservation cases in Taiwan afterward. The research does not treat later relocations as progress in general, but asks one by one about the reasons for relocation, construction methods, legal basis, and the results after relocation. [^7] This approach is worth noting, because it breaks down "successful preservation" into several inspectable issues: how much of the original spatial relationship remains, whether the materials are still identifiable, whether the new location can explain the old life, and whether public use allows the building to continue to generate meaning.

For today's urban engineering, this case also provides a reverse reminder. If cultural heritage is only seen after the road alignment, land rights, and construction period are all fixed, preservation usually only has the choice of dismantling or disappearing. A better approach is to put historical buildings, surrounding landscapes, and community memory into the planning early, and compare the costs of on-site preservation, road rerouting, undergrounding, partial preservation, and relocation preservation before deciding which loss should be borne by whom. This is not about making every old house an untouchable restricted area, but about not mistaking "moving away" as a cost-free neutral plan.

The Lin An-tai Historic House preserved some material evidence of the Qing-era Anxi immigrant family establishing a compound in Taipei, and also preserved the spatial form, architectural craftsmanship, and life imagination of the Minnan courtyard. It also preserved the record of how the government, experts, and the family struggled when Taipei faced the conflict between urban roads and historical buildings in the 1970s. [^3] [^5]

The original land was not preserved. The street view of Siwei Road, the original fields, neighbors, and family daily life did not arrive at the new site with the building. Academic research treats the Lin An-tai Historic House as a representative of relocation preservation not because this method has no drawbacks, but because this case turns the drawbacks themselves into a problem that later cultural heritage discussions cannot skip. [^7]

When entering Binjiang Park, one can treat it as an ancient house, or treat it as a moved file. The first view allows people to appreciate the architecture, and the second view allows people to pay attention to the relationship between the architecture and the land. Both views are necessary, and if either is missing, the Lin An-tai Historic House will be described too easily.

What is preserved is not necessarily the original place, but the willingness of people to acknowledge the loss after losing the original site. The Lin An-tai Historic House is therefore not only a house that was left behind, but also the first question that Taipei was forced to answer between road widening, urban renewal, and cultural memory. This question has not been moved elsewhere, and it is still waiting at the original site for people to ask again. For this reason, the Lin An-tai Historic House is worth being treated as an entrance to Taiwan's modern urban history, rather than just a historic site name on a travel list.
