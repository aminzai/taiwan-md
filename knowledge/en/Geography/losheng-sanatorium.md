> **30-second overview:** Losheng Sanatorium was established in 1930 at the border of Xinzhuang and Guishan as Taiwan's first and, during the Japanese colonial period, only public Hansen's disease isolation hospital. It is not merely an old campus preserving Japanese-era wards and hillside scenery, but a living community where residents established families, religion, work, and mutual-aid orders during long-term isolation. In the 1990s, the selection of the site for a metro depot brought "transportation infrastructure" and "staying at home" into direct conflict; the 2000s preservation movement then pushed a hospital once isolated by society to the center of public debate on medical policy, housing rights, patient autonomy, and cultural heritage.

## Not an Ordinary Hospital

Looking toward the hillside from the Huilong Station area, what first appears is not an isolated monument but a compound unfolding along the terrain. It once had wards, medical spaces, dormitories, kitchens, bathhouses, a welfare center, a library, religious sites, and a crematorium. Together these facilities formed a community capable of sustaining long-term life. Official cultural heritage records therefore regard it as a cultural landscape where medical history, public health history, and social life history overlap — not simply a collection of old buildings.[^1]

The name "sanatorium" cannot obscure its institutional background. Today's standard term "Hansen's disease" was once called leprosy, a condition long surrounded by fear, religious imagination, and stigma. Losheng's history encompasses both the gradual transformation of treatment technology and the state's governance method of separating patients from families and society. Understanding this campus requires asking two questions simultaneously: What problems did the isolation policy of the time aim to solve? How did the isolated people rearrange their lives within the system?

## 1930: The State Built an Isolation System on the Hillside

Losheng Sanatorium was constructed by the Japanese colonial government in 1930 as Taiwan's first and only public Hansen's disease isolation hospital during the colonial period. It was sited at Dingshueijiao, near Huilong, on the border of Xinzhuang and Guishan in Taoyuan — close to the city yet deliberately distanced from ordinary residential areas. The Ministry of Culture's Taiwan World Heritage candidate site data notes that the early campus housed patients in five wards. The postwar government continued operations, expanding the number of wards to over sixty.[^2]

_Image: Wikimedia Commons, "Birdview of Rakuseiin (c. 1932).jpg." This historical image suits reading alongside this section's founding background; actual license see image sources at end._

This spatial arrangement reflected the disease imaginary of the time: patients were viewed as a group requiring isolation, not as residents who could receive treatment in general communities. Isolation occurred not only in medical examination rooms but extended to marriage, returning home, work, and death. Residents might spend most of their lives within the hospital, so the institution gradually ceased to be a short-term inpatient facility and became a home with its own paths, neighborhoods, and memories.

## Wards Are Not Hospital Rooms, but Residents' Residences

Losheng's most notable spatial feature is that its wards do not fully resemble modern hospital multi-bed rooms. Cultural heritage data mentions that the campus utilized the sunny hillside to arrange buildings; early wards were organized as detached or small clustered units where residents lived long-term. The campus also installed water supply and drainage, disinfection facilities, communal kitchens and bathhouses.[^1] These facilities served the isolation system on one hand, yet on the other enabled patients to establish daily order within restricted bounds.

Residents were not merely recipients of care. The campus had work, religion, shopping, socializing, and mutual aid; residents also tended gardens, repaired houses, and cared for one another. Some wards were named after Taiwan's prefectures, creating a spatial memory that compressed the island's place names onto the hillside. This life history reminds us that if we only photograph roof tiles, arched windows, and old trees without explaining who ate, worked, quarreled, prayed, and bid farewell here, we silence the residents once again.[^3]

_Image: Wikimedia Commons, "Losheng Prayer hall.jpg." The prayer hall reminds us that campus life consisted not only of medical facilities but also of faith spaces where residents faced disease, death, and mutual accompaniment; actual license see image sources at end._

## From Chaulmoogra Oil to Multi-Drug Therapy: A Turning Point in Medical History

Losheng also preserves the shift in Hansen's disease treatment concepts. Early treatment used chaulmoogra oil and similar methods with limited effect. From the mid-20th century onward, antibiotics and multi-drug therapy gradually changed the disease's treatability. The Taiwan Association for Human Rights' compiled Losheng materials place this trajectory in a broader public health history: from forced isolation and institutional internment to later outpatient and open measures, the campus witnessed the transformation of disease policy.[^3]

Medical progress did not immediately eliminate social exclusion. Even when the disease became treatable, residents still faced family estrangement, work disruption, difficulty returning home, and social prejudice. This is one of the most important contrasts in the Losheng story: drugs could reduce infection and symptom risks, but could not automatically repair relationships fractured by decades of isolation, nor decide for residents where they should spend their later years.

## Policy Changed, but Home Did Not Disappear

Postwar Taiwan continued isolation and internment policies, expanding wards in the 1970s. The transformation chronicle of Ministry of Health and Welfare-affiliated hospitals records that during the provincial government era, wards were added to sixty with 970 beds, and Losheng's resident population peaked. As Hansen's disease control and drug treatment advanced, medical institutions' functions gradually transformed.[^4]

For policy, this may be a progress narrative from "internment" toward "treatment." For residents, it is another complex experience. Many lived in the campus for decades, familiar with every tree, every slope, and every neighbor. When the government explained that the campus's function had changed, it could not be directly inferred that residents could leave without cost. For a community forcibly formed under an isolation system, the place of residence is simultaneously a trauma site and a home.

## 1990s: Metro Depot Collides with Residents' Later Years

In the 1990s, Losheng's land was planned as the Zhonghe-Xinlu Line metro depot. Taiwan Association for Human Rights data notes that a large portion of the roughly thirty hectares was designated for depot use, and the most historically valuable building clusters fell within the engineering scope.[^3] This was not a simple monument demolition dispute, because residents still lived inside. The depot siting simultaneously involved engineering efficiency, resident resettlement, information disclosure, and housing choice.

The Control Yuan later pointed out that the handling of the Losheng Sanatorium campus preservation case involved cultural heritage assessment, metro engineering siting, and administrative procedure problems, issuing corrective measures to relevant agencies.[^5] This investigation shifted the controversy from "someone opposes the project" to more concrete systemic questions: Did the government identify cultural heritage early? Did residents receive sufficient information before decisions? Did engineering and resettlement plans place elderly life needs at the core?

## Post-2004: The Preservation Movement Rewrites the Problem's Name

Preservation actions gradually expanded in the early 2000s. The Youth Losheng Alliance, resident self-help organizations, local cultural-history workers, human rights groups, students, and cultural heritage professionals jointly intervened, making "preserving a few buildings" no longer the sole issue. Public Television Service reports recorded the Youth Losheng Alliance's involvement in 2004 and the process of residents organizing a self-help association under engineering pressure.[^6]

![Former Losheng Sanatorium Administration Center site](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/1/1a/The_former_Losheng_Sanatorium_Administration_Center_20070322.jpg/1280px-The_former_Losheng_Sanatorium_Administration_Center_20070322.jpg)

_Image: Wikimedia Commons, "The former Losheng Sanatorium Administration Center 20070322.jpg." The administration center's remaining space corresponds to this section's preservation controversy; actual license see image sources at end._

The preservation movement's greatest power lay in making residents narrative subjects again. Residents were not objects to be preserved, nor numbers obstructing construction. They were long-term inhabitants, people who could explain the campus's history, and people with the right to participate in relocation, resettlement, and restoration decisions. This shift linked cultural heritage and human rights: a building's value cannot be calculated apart from the people living within it.

## "Preservation" Is Not Keeping Empty Houses After Moving Residents Out

In 2007, the government decided to preserve part of the campus buildings, with a plan including preservation, demolition, and reconstruction of different space types. Ministry of Culture data later explained the policy direction with a plan for preserving 39 buildings, partial demolition, and reconstruction.[^2] However, the word "preserve" still demands pursuit: preserve the facade, the house, the courtyard, or the life relations? If only empty, unused buildings remain, the campus may become a ruin for viewing, yet lose its original meaning as a living place.

In 2012, the Minister of Culture visited the campus and proposed establishing a medical museum. Media reports at the time noted about one hundred residents still living in the campus, and preservation groups continued monitoring the metro engineering's impact on buildings and foundations.[^7] This discussion presents preservation's dilemma: a museum can let society see Hansen's disease history, but a museum cannot replace residents' rights to residence, privacy, and autonomous living.

## Why Losheng Is a Cultural Landscape, Not a Nostalgic Scenic Spot

In 2009, Losheng Sanatorium was registered as a cultural landscape. Official cultural heritage descriptions place its value at several levels: it bears witness to Hansen's disease control and modern public health. Residents formed a complete living settlement under restrictive conditions. Architecture, natural slopes, and residents' memories jointly constitute the spirit of place. Preserving it also enables society to confront disease stigma and human rights history.[^1]

Therefore, Losheng is unsuitable for packaging merely as "Japanese-era old houses" or "photo hotspots." Japanese-style buildings are certainly visible architectural features, but their importance lies not only in age but in how they carry the ways institutions entered bodies and lives. Wards, bathhouses, kitchens, religious spaces, and paths to the crematorium compose a map of treatment, isolation, faith, death, and mutual aid.

## A Living Medical History

The Ministry of Health and Welfare Losheng Sanatorium's recent public information still records residents, Hansen's disease care, and international exchange, showing Losheng is not a cultural heritage belonging only to the past. In 2026 the hospital reported the visit of the International Hansen's Disease Ambassador, with residents participating in exchange. This reminds us that preservation work is not erecting a monument for an already-ended history, but continuously caring for those still living in the campus.[^8]

National Taiwan Normal University's compiled Losheng chronology places the 1930 founding, postwar expansion, 1990s development controversy, and 2000s preservation movement on a single timeline.[^9] This timeline matters because it refuses to cut Losheng into two unrelated stories of "colonial architecture" and "metro resistance." The former explains how the campus formed; the latter explains who has the right to decide its future.

## What to See When Visiting Losheng

To understand Losheng, the first glance can be at architecture, but the second should be at relationships between buildings: Why are wards distributed along the slope? How did public facilities let residents maintain daily life? Which spaces once belonged to medical staff, which were established by residents themselves? How do the building counts on the preservation register correspond to residents' life memories?

One should also remember that Losheng remains a place with residents and medical functions. Visits must not treat residents as photographable exhibits, nor enter living spaces without consent. Truly respecting a cultural landscape means not turning it into a more convenient tourist spot, but understanding its formation includes forced isolation and residents' capacity to create life under constrained conditions.

Visitors can further divide "seeing" into three layers. The first layer is material: wood structure, brick walls, arched windows, slopes, old trees, and roads, explaining how architecture responded to climate, hygiene, and terrain. The second layer is institution: who could enter, who had to stay, how items were disinfected, how wards were managed, explaining how public health became spatial rules. The third layer is memory: how residents named different wards, how they established religious and mutual-aid networks, how they transformed a campus originally carrying exclusionary meaning into a livable home. Only with all three layers together does a visit avoid reducing Losheng to a set of beautiful but silent old photographs.

Guiding should also avoid treating Hansen's disease as sensational material. Disease history can discuss transmission, treatment, and isolation, but should not manufacture viewing stimulation through patients' physical appearance or personal ordeals. A more responsible approach uses the contemporary standard name "Hansen's disease," explains historical documents' old terms and their stigma, and regards residents as people with names, relationships, work, and choice capacity. This linguistic shift is not political correctness decoration, but corrects how medical institutions once reduced patients to "cases." One can also begin understanding from objects residents left and space usage patterns: how furniture was arranged in wards, how potted plants lined slopes, how communal bathhouses and kitchens enabled socializing, and how religious spaces provided language for facing disease and death. These details may not be directly visible on building exteriors, yet are inseparable contents of the cultural landscape. They let us know that Losheng's history was not written unilaterally by policy documents, but accumulated through residents' repeated use, adjustment, and memory of the everyday.

## After Preservation, Who Can Still Live Here

The cultural heritage system addresses registration, restoration, and management problems, but Losheng's particularity lies in "residence" being inseparable. For ordinary monuments, restoration and opening for visits may be the main goal. For Losheng, residents remain the contemporary bearers of the site's history. Restoration materials, visitor routes, activity noise, photography rules, and medical privacy all need boundaries drawn between cultural display and resident life. This is not treating residents as people needing protection without decision-making power, but acknowledging their knowledge and rights formed through long-term use of the campus.

From the preservation engineering perspective, the terrain, structural, and circulation issues left by metro construction also make it impossible for Losheng to return to a completely "original state" without external pressure. New Taipei City Cultural Affairs Bureau preservation promotion meeting records show that engineering monitoring, building perimeter platforms, Huilong Station connection, and campus restoration require ongoing coordination among cultural, transportation, and medical agencies.[^11] Preservation is thus a form of long-term governance, not an administrative act ending with a single announcement.

More broadly, Losheng offers Taiwan's memory politics a rare case. It does not simplify history into colonial rule relics, nor write the postwar government as a single role. Japanese-era isolation policy, postwar expansion, drug treatment, metro development, and post-democratization civic action all left traces on the same ground. National Taiwan Normal University's research project discusses Losheng and Taiwan's Hansen's disease policy from a global perspective, illustrating this place is not just local history but a case comparable with global Hansen's disease isolation systems, medical modernity, and human rights transformation.[^10]

## A Timeline Linking Medicine, Development, and Preservation

Losheng's key turning points were not single sudden events, but different systems successively layered on the same hillside. The timeline below lists only nodes directly relevant to this article's main thread, avoiding misreading complex history as a straight line with only progress and no cost.[^2] [^5] [^9]

```tw-timeline
1930 | Public sanatorium established | Japanese colonial government establishes Hansen's disease isolation hospital at Xinzhuang-Guishan border
Postwar | Internment scale expanded | Wards and beds increased, campus gradually becomes long-term residential living settlement
1990s | Metro depot planned | Zhonghe-Xinlu Line depot siting causes conflict between engineering needs, resident housing, and cultural heritage preservation
2004 onward | Preservation movement expands | Residents, youth, human rights groups, and cultural-history workers turn demolition dispute into public decision-making and human rights issue
2009 | Registered as cultural landscape | Official preservation recognition incorporates architecture, slopes, living settlement, and medical human rights values
Sources: National Cultural Heritage Database, Control Yuan, National Taiwan Normal University Losheng Chronology
```

This timeline's point is not to select a "true starting point" for Losheng, but to remind readers: the space created by isolation policy later simultaneously became a relic of medical transformation, residents' home, and a cultural and human rights site that urban construction must confront.[^1] [^6]

## The Questions a Home Leaves Behind

Losheng Sanatorium's most important legacy may not be a particular house, but a question forcing Taiwan to rethink: When public interest conflicts with a vulnerable person's home, who has the right to define "necessary sacrifice"? When medical policy once isolated people, does later society have the responsibility to let them participate in how their history is preserved?

## Curator's Note: Don't Preserve Only the "Visible" Losheng

When curating Losheng, I deliberately shift gaze from the most easily photographed roof tiles, wooden windows, and old trees. These materials are certainly important because they let people recognize the campus's age and terrain. However, the National Cultural Heritage Database lists Losheng as a cultural landscape precisely because value lies not in individual buildings but in the overall relationship of medical facilities, residential settlement, religious sites, natural slopes, and residents' life memories.[^1] If an exhibition only makes architecture prettier but moves residents' life, labor, faith, and mutual aid to an appendix, preservation may again replicate the "institution naming people" mode of viewing.

Therefore, this article chooses to juxtapose three material types: official cultural heritage data lets us know the institutional language of registration and preservation. Narratives left by residents, human rights groups, and public media let us see how isolation entered families, bodies, and daily life. Administrative meetings and academic research let us pursue responsibility allocation among metro engineering, cultural governance, and human rights.[^3] [^5] [^6] [^10] They are not entirely identical, nor should they be compressed into a conflict-free story. Curation's work is not manufacturing false consensus for different positions, but clearly marking where each material speaks from, what it sees, and what blanks it leaves.

I would also take "residents still live here" as the starting point of exhibition design, not an ethical reminder added at the end. Ministry of Health and Welfare Losheng Sanatorium's public reports show the campus still has residents connected to care and exchange activities today. This means visitor routes, photography methods, sound, and activity arrangements may all touch real residents' privacy and daily life.[^8] For Losheng, the best exhibition is not necessarily the most information-dense, but one that lets visitors, while understanding institutional history, know they are entering a place where people still live and still need respect.

Finally, Losheng should not be shelved only in the "trauma site" cabinet. Residents certainly endured isolation and stigma, but they also established neighborliness, work, religion, and care relationships within constraints. Viewing them only through a victim framework again strips their subjectivity as life creators and history interpreters. To me, what Losheng most deserves to preserve is not a consumable tragic story, but a public site continuously questioning how public construction, medical governance, and residential dignity should be jointly decided. This is also the question curators must leave for audiences, not answer for them.

This campus, originating from the state's isolation system, ultimately became a public site of human rights and cultural preservation. It reminds us that architecture records not only designers' intentions but also how residents transformed institutional space into a life world. Seeing Losheng is not only looking back at a disease history, but learning how to put human dignity back at the decision-making center among urban construction, medical governance, and cultural heritage.

## Further Reading

To further understand Losheng, one can read along three paths. The first is medical history, tracing Hansen's disease's institutional transformation from isolation and internment to multi-drug therapy. The second is spatial history, observing how wards, public facilities, slopes, and the metro depot jointly shaped the campus. The third is human rights history, reading resident self-help, youth participation, and administrative procedure disputes to understand how the preservation movement changed public decision-making language.
