# New Taipei City: The Ring-Shaped Metropolis Surrounding Taipei, Supporting the Capital’s Commuting, Industry, and Memory with 4.01 Million People

> **30-Second Overview:** In 1628, the Spanish built Fort Santo Domingo in Tamsui; in 1643, the Dutch laid the foundation for a new castle on the same site; in 1867, the British and the Qing government signed the _Permanent Lease Agreement for Fort Santo Domingo_, paying an annual rent of only 10 taels of silver. This brick structure predates Shen Baozhen’s establishment of the “Taipei Prefecture” in Monga in 1875 by 247 years. Today, New Taipei City’s 19 districts surround Taipei City’s 12 districts, with 4.01 million people residing in this largest direct-administered city in Taiwan. It was upgraded to a direct-administered city exactly 15 years ago on December 25, 2010. However, the history of this “ring-shaped metropolis” stretches far back: the three-story main residence of the Banqiao Lin Family in 1853; in 1869, Thomas W. Dow transported 21,000 kilograms of oolong tea from Tamsui to New York; in the 1890s, Jiufen and Jinguashi became Asia’s first gold mine; in 1929, the Pingxi Railway began transporting coal; in 1947, Li Mei-shu began carving the Sanxia Changfu Yan Ancestor Temple; and the Delanang Village of the Atayal people in Wulai. Taipei sees satellite cities; New Taipei sees its own 400-year history.

## The Bricks at the Mouth of the Tamsui River Predate Taipei’s Founding by 247 Years

![The main red-brick structure of Fort Santo Domingo in Tamsui, a square brick fortress on a hill, with the British Consulate residence added in 1891 beside it. A 400-year layered history: Spanish Fort Santo Domingo in 1628, Dutch Fort Anthonio in 1644, British lease in 1867, and returned to Taiwan in 1980.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/30/2017_Fort_San_Domingo.jpg/1280px-2017_Fort_San_Domingo.jpg)
_Fort Santo Domingo in Tamsui, the site where the Spanish laid the first brick in 1628. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA 4.0](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Fort_Santo_Domingo)._

If you are looking for a single object that can speak for all of New Taipei City, go to Fort Santo Domingo in Tamsui.

Fort Santo Domingo is located at No. 1, Lane 28, Zhongzheng Road, Tamsui District. It is a square brick building on a hill, with a two-story brick British Consulate residence added in 1891 beside it[^1]. From here, one can see the mouth of the Tamsui River, Bali, and the Taiwan Strait. Between 1628 and 1629, a Spanish fleet sailed north from Manila and built the first-generation fortress at this site, using "clay, reeds, bamboo, and wood as raw materials," naming it Fort Santo Domingo[^1].

The Spanish stayed in Tamsui for 14 years. In 1642, the Dutch East India Company attacked San Salvador Fort in Keelung, coming north from Tainan, and顺势 took over Tamsui. On May 7, 1643, the Dutch laid the foundation for a new castle at the same site, "the first stone was officially laid, and the construction of the fortress began... it was not fully completed until 1646"[^1]. The Dutch spent three years transforming the wooden fortress left by the Spanish into the stone Fort Anthonio. The red-brick main structure we see today has a skeleton laid by the Dutch in 1644.

After the Qing dynasty arrived, this fortress changed hands once again. In 1724, "Wang Qian, the then Magistrate of Danshui and Catch-thief Assistant of Taiwan Prefecture, began to repair Fort Santo Domingo"[^1]. In 1867, the Qing court signed the "Permanent Lease Agreement for Fort Santo Domingo" with the United Kingdom, "the British established a consulate in Tamsui in 1861, and in 1867 signed the 'Permanent Lease Agreement for Fort Santo Domingo' with the Qing court"[^1], paying only 10 taels of fine silver annually. The British stayed here for 105 years. After withdrawing their embassy in 1972, they entrusted custody to Australia, which then handed it over to the United States for custody. It was officially handed back to the government of the Republic of China by the British on June 30, 1980[^1].

From the Spanish in 1628, the Dutch in 1644, the Qing in 1724, the British in 1867, Australia and the United States, to its return to the Republic of China in 1980, a single brick has experienced six different rulers. It was designated as a First-Class Cultural Monument in the Taiwan, Fujian, and Matsu regions in 1983, and opened to the public for visits on December 25, 1984[^1].

And what about Taipei's "founding"? It had to wait until 1875, when Shen Baozhen memorialized the establishment of "Taipei Prefecture"; 1879, when Chen Xingju began building the city walls; 1882, when the North Gate city walls broke ground; and 1884, when it was completed. This is 256 years later than the first brick laid at Fort Santo Domingo in 1628.

This is the first easily misunderstood fact about New Taipei City: **The history of the identity "surrounding Taipei" is actually two and a half centuries older than Taipei itself.**

## 1858 Port Opening, 1869 Oolong Tea to New York: The First Window to the Outside World

Next to Fort Santo Domingo lies the mouth of the Tamsui River.

In June 1858, the Qing government signed the Treaty of Tianjin with the major powers. "The three ports of Taiwan (Anping), Tamsui, and Takao (Kaohsiung) were thus opened to the powers, allowing travel and missionary work" [^2]. The Qing government subsequently signed the Treaty of Tianjin with Russia (June 13), the United States (June 18), Britain (June 26), and France (June 27). On July 18, 1862, Tamsui established a customs office and opened for trade; this was Taiwan's first port formally opened to global trade, 21 years before Keelung (Keelung did not establish a customs office until 1883).

British merchant John Dodd arrived during this period. In 1864, he came to Tamsui to investigate Taiwan's tea industry, established Jardine Matheson's predecessor (the "Baoshun" trading house) in Tamsui, and introduced tea seedlings from Anxi, Fujian, providing loans to farmers in the northern region. In 1869, "two sailing ships transported 21,000 kg of Taiwan oolong tea directly to New York" [^3]. This was the first time Taiwanese tea was exported to the United States, crossing half the globe. "Winning a good reputation," oolong tea became a hot seller in New York, and Tamsui followed suit.

"From 1864 to 1894, the total export trade volume of Tamsui accounted for 57% of all of Taiwan, with tea, sugar, and camphor as the main exports" [^4]. English-speaking trading house staff, compradors writing letters back to Xiamen, Fujianese immigrants rolling cigarettes, and Taiwanese merchants purchasing goods in Monga were all crowded along the same riverbank. "By 1878, tea had accounted for 90% of the total export value of the north" [^5]. Tamsui had become the globalization window for northern Taiwan.

George Leslie Mackay (Ma Hai) also arrived at this time. "On March 9, 1872, Canadian Presbyterian missionary George Leslie Mackay landed in Tamsui" [^6]. The day he stepped off the ship, he knew he would stay here for the rest of his life. In 1880, Mackay returned to Canada to raise funds, receiving a donation of 6,215 Canadian dollars from residents of Oxford County, Ontario, his hometown. "Completed on July 26, 1882 (the 8th year of the Guangxu reign), it was named 'Li Xue Tang Grand Academy'" [^6]. This was the first Western-style school in northern Taiwan, the predecessor of today's Tamkang University (in 1965, the 'Tamsui Business and Trade Junior College' was established on the site of the Oxford College; in 1999, it was upgraded to Tamkang University).

But in 1884, another war broke out.

On October 1, Admiral孤拔 (Courbet) led four warships to attack the mouth of the Tamsui River at Huwei. Admiral Sun Kaihua had stones used to sink ships to block the port and laid mines to prevent the French from landing. "Official records from the French National Archives state that French losses included 6 dead, 11 missing, and 49 wounded" [^7]. The French occupied Keelung for eight months, but Tamsui was not captured.

"The Qing court realized the strategic importance of Taiwan's coastal defense in this campaign; after the war, it announced the establishment of Taiwan as a province, appointing Liu Mingchuan, in his capacity as Fujian Governor, as the first Taiwan Provincial Governor" [^7]. After the war, Liu Mingchuan hired German military engineer Max E. Hecht to plan the Huwei Battery, which was completed in 1886. The gate inscription reads "Key to the Northern Gate," a testament that remains visible today [^7]. This is the most memorable contrast in New Taipei City: **The direct trigger for Taiwan's establishment as a province was this small naval battle in Tamsui.**

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Taiwan history textbooks often treat "1858 Treaty of Tianjin → 1860 Tamsui Port Opening → 1869 Oolong Tea Export to New York" as a single narrative. However, these three events compressed represent the entire process of Tamsui transforming from a peripheral fishing port into a global trade node. The Baoshun trading house was located near today's Customs Pier on Tamsui's Chungcheng Road; Mackay's Oxford College is within the campus of today's Tamkang University; the 1886 Huwei Battery is behind the Tamsui Martyrs' Shrine. It takes only 10 minutes to walk from Fort Santo Domingo to the Oxford College, but the journey spans over 200 years of world history. **Taiwanese people often trace Taipei's globalization back to the 1980s. But for people in Tamsui, what remains memorable is that batch of 21,000 kg of oolong tea in 1869.**

## The Lin Family of Banqiao: The First Case of Indigenous Capitalism in 1853

![A corner of the courtyard at the Lin Family Mansion and Garden (Lin Family Garden) in Banqiao, a late Qing dynasty garden architecture that Lin Weiyuan invested heavily in repairing in 1888 and which was fully completed in 1893. After 1949, it was occupied by over a thousand refugees from three hundred households; in 1982, 156.43 million New Taiwan dollars were spent on its restoration, which was completed in 1986. It is now a national-level historical monument.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/c/c0/Lin_Family_Mansion_and_Garden_2.JPG/1280px-Lin_Family_Mansion_and_Garden_2.JPG)
_The Lin Family Mansion and Garden (Lin Family Garden) in Banqiao, completed in 1893, is the first case of Taiwan's indigenous capitalism spanning 133 years. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Lin_Family_Mansion_and_Garden)._

Traveling south from Danshui, it is a half-hour drive to Banqiao.

If Danshui is New Taipei's international window, Banqiao is the starting point of its indigenous capitalism.

Lin Ping-hou established his fortune early. "Lin Ping-hou made his living transporting rice, and when the Lin Shuangwen Rebellion occurred, rice prices soared. As a rice merchant, Lin Ping-hou reaped extremely substantial profits in the rice transport business" [^8]. He had five sons; the third son (Ben Ji) and the youngest son (Yuan Ji) jointly established "Lin Ben Yuan" as the family name, which became the formal name of the "Banqiao Lin Family."

The Lin family's settlement in Banqiao happened in two steps. The first step: "In the 27th year of the Daoguang reign (1847 AD), the Lin family built Biyi Hall in Fangqiao (now Banqiao) for the convenience of collecting tenant rents; this marked the beginning of the Lin family building structures in the Banqiao area" [^8]. The second step was between 1851 and 1853: "In the first year of the Xianfeng reign (1851 AD), the Lin family built a three-hall main house next to Biyi Hall in Fangqiao, and after its completion in the third year of Xianfeng, the entire family moved in" [^8]. In 1853, during the third year of the Xianfeng reign, the Lin family officially moved from Xinzhuang to Banqiao, marking the beginning of the Banqiao Lin Family's identity as "Banqiao people."

This was followed by the漳-Quan conflicts (Zhangzhou-Quanzhou械鬥). After Lin Guo-fang (the youngest son of Lin Ping-hou) took over the family business in 1857, the Zhangzhou-Quanzhou conflicts in Taipei reached their peak. In 1861, Lin Guo-fang attempted to reclaim land leased to Quanzhou people and re-lease it to Zhangzhou people, causing great turmoil among the people of greater Taipei. In the decades following this conflict, Banqiao became the base for Zhangzhou immigrants in greater Taipei.

The Lin family's true immense wealth came during the third generation, under Lin Weiyuan and Lin Weirang. During the Sino-French War in 1884, "In the 10th year of the Guangxu reign (1884 AD), Lin Weiyuan donated 200,000 taels of silver for military provisions and assisted Liu Ming-chuan in handling Taiwan's defense, serving as the Assistant Minister of Taiwan's Pacification and Development. He suddenly rose to become a 'red-topped merchant'" [^9]. The Lin family rose from rice merchants to political-business figures, dealing directly with the high-level Qing court.

The Lin Family Garden was built during this period. In the 14th year of the Guangxu reign (1888 AD), Lin Weiyuan invested heavily in repairs, connecting the various garden scenes, **and it was fully completed in the 19th year of Guangxu (1893 AD)** [^8]. This garden still exists today on Xiemen Street in Banqiao District, covering a total area of 6,054 ping, making it the most complete surviving garden architecture in Taiwan.

However, after the Nationalist government relocated to Taiwan in 1949, the Lin Family Garden in Banqiao was nearly destroyed. "After 1949, over a thousand refugees from three hundred households occupied the site, causing severe damage" [^8]. More than a thousand out-of-province refugees moved into this late Qing garden, cramming toilets, kitchens, and clotheslines into the pavilions and towers. By the end of 1982, "the garden's restoration project began, and after four years, it was finally restored. The total restoration cost amounted to 156,433,218 New Taiwan dollars" [^8]. Designated as a national-level historical monument in 1985, the Lin Family Garden we see today is the version restored and completed in 1986.

From settling in Banqiao in 1853, investing heavily in the garden in 1888, its completion in 1893, refugees moving in during 1949, restoration beginning in 1982, and completion in 1986, one garden spans 133 years of Taiwan's history.

Banqiao's role as the seat of New Taipei County's government (later upgraded to city government) is inseparable from the Lin family. Today, Banqiao District has a population of 549,000 (as of the end of 2025), making it the administrative district with the largest population in the country, and it is also the seat of the New Taipei City Government [^10]. But the core of Banqiao actually remains next to the three-hall main house built in 1853.

## 1890–1987: The 95-Year Gold Mining Era in Jiufen and Kinmen

![Terraced architecture of the Jiufen mountain settlement, with Keelung Island visible from the summit. A mining settlement that discovered gold in 1890, peaked with a population of 80,000 in 1938, saw the Taiwan Metal Corporation cease operations in 1987, and became a tourist hotspot after being featured in the film _City of Sadness_ in 1989.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/d/de/Jiufen_Old_Street_2017.jpg/1280px-Jiufen_Old_Street_2017.jpg)
_Jiufen Mountain Town: The site of transformation from a miners’ settlement to a tourist symbol. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Jiufen)._

Moving northeast from Banqiao lies the mountainous region of New Taipei.

In 1889, workers discovered alluvial gold in the Keelung River. Prospecting crowds followed the river upstream, reaching Dacukeng between 1890 and 1893. In 1893, a gold vein outcrop was discovered in the mountains near Jiufen. In 1894, gold was found in the Ben Shan ore body of Kinmen, marking the beginning of what would become known as "Asia’s First Precious Metal Mine."

At the start of the Japanese colonial era, mining rights in Kinmen were granted to the Tanaka Group. On October 26, 1896, the rights were officially approved and issued to Tanaka Chōhei, who began mining operations at Kinmen Ben Shan[^11]. The Japanese introduced mechanized mining, rail tracks, and chemical refining, transforming Kinmen from a Qing-era site of scattered prospectors into an industrial mine under Japanese rule.

"In 1938, production reached its peak of nearly 70,000 taels, earning Kinmen the reputation as 'Asia’s First Precious Metal Mine' and attracting 80,000 people to the area in pursuit of their gold dreams"[^12]. A small mountain town, Kinmen housed 80,000 people in 1938. This was ten times the combined population of today’s Wulai District (5,800), Pingxi District (4,400), and Shuangxi District (8,300).

After the war, mining in Kinmen continued under the Taiwan Metal Corporation (TMC). However, gold output declined year by year. In 1985, TMC began downsizing, and mines and processing plants were gradually put up for auction. **"The Taiwan Metal Corporation finally ceased operations in 1987, and the land of the Kinmen mining area was taken over by the Taiwan Sugar Corporation"**[^13]. This marks the precise endpoint of the "gold mining era" in Jiufen and Kinmen. From the discovery of gold in 1890 to the closure of TMC in 1987, the era lasted exactly 97 years.

But the story of Jiufen did not end in 1987.

In 1989, director Hou Hsiao-hsien filmed _City of Sadness_ in Jiufen and Kinmen. "Filming began on November 25, 1988, in front of the Bagu Pavilion in Kinmen, Keelung." The film won the Golden Lion at the Venice Film Festival in 1989, "turning the filming locations of Jiufen and Kinmen into tourist hotspots"[^14]. The hillside settlement of the mining era was rewritten by a single film into a tourist symbol. Starting in the 1990s, old houses in Jiufen were converted into tea houses, and tour buses flooded the mountaintop. Mining-era landmarks such as "No. 5 Mine," "Shengping Theater," and "Jishan Street" became popular spots for Instagram check-ins.

In 2004, "led by the Taipei County Government... the Gold Museum Park officially opened"[^15]. It is Taiwan’s first museum park based on the concept of an ecological museum, integrating the Japanese-era Crown Prince Guest House, Ben Shan Mine No. 5, and miners’ dormitories into a local museum. It took 17 years for Kinmen to transform from a mine into a museum.

From the perspective of Jiufen’s tourists, this is a romantic transformation. But the descendants of miners may not see it that way. In the year TMC ceased operations, the population of the Kinmen settlement dropped from 80,000 to less than 5,000. Miners scattered to Taipei, Keelung, and Yilan to find work. Today, many of those selling taro balls on the old streets of Jiufen and Kinmen are descendants of those miners.

> **✦** The symbol of the end of the mining era is the 1987 closure of TMC, not the 1985 downsizing. 1985 marked the beginning of boiling the frog slowly; 1987 was the moment the water finally ran dry.

## 1929 Pingxi Railway, 1804 Yingge Ceramics, 1769 Sanxia Changfu Yan Ancestor Temple: The Craft Chain of Northern Taiwan

![Pingxi Line Railway and Shifen Old Street, 1921 Taiwan Metal Corporation coal transport line, 1929 Governor-General's Office acquired for 1.5 million yen to operate as public transport. The tourist train today has the skeleton of the coal railway from the Japanese colonial era.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/8/8f/Shifen_Old_Street_2014.jpg/1280px-Shifen_Old_Street_2014.jpg)
_Shifen Old Street on the Pingxi Line, a railway that continued to the present after becoming state-operated passenger transport in 1929. Photo: Wikimedia Commons contributor, [CC BY-SA via Wikimedia](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Pingxi_Line)._

Beyond Jiufen and Jinguashi, another mining settlement in New Taipei City is located in the upper reaches of the Keelung River: Pingxi.

"The Pingxi Line was completed in its entirety in July 1921 (Taishō 10), originally built as a coal transport railway funded by the Taiwan Metal Corporation"[^16]. During the Japanese colonial era, the Taiwan Metal Corporation (operated by the Yeh Yun-nien family) discovered large coal reserves in the Shihdi Coalfield (present-day Pingxi and Jingtong area). In 1908, the Fujita Group conducted trial excavations to confirm the reserves, and in 1921, they built a railway to transport the coal out.

"On October 1, 1929 (Shōwa 4), the Taiwan Governor-General's Railway Bureau acquired the Shihdi Line for 1.5 million yen, converting it to state operation and naming it the 'Pingxi Line.' After renovation, it began handling passenger transport as well"[^16]. This railway was originally a private coal transport line; it was acquired by the Governor-General's Office in 1929 to become the 'Pingxi Line.' The tourist train on the Pingxi Line today has the skeleton of the 1929 state-operated version.

The Pingxi Sky Lanterns came much later. "During the Daoguang reign of the Qing Dynasty, the area's pioneers arrived in the Shifenliao area via Fujian's Hui'an and Anxi counties. Due to bandit disturbances and harassment of settlements, the pioneers sought refuge in the mountains. After the crisis passed, the able-bodied men remaining in the village used sky lanterns as signals to notify their families in hiding to return home"[^17]. A signal system for refuge was commercialized for tourism in the 1990s, and the _New Taipei City Sky Lantern Release Management Regulations_ were established in 2012. Today, on every Lantern Festival, the Pingxi sky is filled with orange sky lanterns; it is beautiful, but has nothing to do with coal mining.

Moving toward Sanxia and Yingge lies another craft chain.

The Sanxia Changfu Yan Ancestor Temple's earliest construction date varies across sources. ⚠️ The common official narrative states "1769 (Qianlong 34)"[^18]. The temple underwent three reconstructions: the first after the 1833 earthquake; the second after being burned down as a resistance base following the Japanese army's entry into Taiwan in 1899; and the third in 1947. The host of this reconstruction was the painter Li Mei-shu.

"In 1947, the temple underwent its third major renovation. The temple authorities and local figures jointly elected Li Mei-shu as the project director. Li personally oversaw the planning and reconstruction, using his artistic expertise to launch the temple's breathtaking decorative art project, earning it the reputation of the 'Hall of Oriental Art'"[^18]. Li Mei-shu was from Sanxia and one of the founders of the Taiwan Fine Arts Association. After the war, he served as chairman of the Sanxia Township People's Representative Assembly. In 1947, he began carving this temple as his life's artistic work, treating every pillar, each coffered ceiling, and every stone lion as an oil painting. **He did not finish carving until his death in 1983, and repairs continue to this day**[^18]. A temple carved for 79 years, and it is not yet finished.

Yingge ceramics began in the late 18th century with the Wu family. "Yingge's pottery industry originated in 1804 (Jiaqing 9), when Wu An from Quanzhou first established a kiln in the Dahu area"[^19]. Wu An crossed the sea from Quanzhou, Fujian, to Taiwan, initially living in Tuzikeng, Guishan, Taoyuan. Due to conflicts between Zhangzhou and Quanzhou clans, he moved to the Jianshanpu Street area of Yingge. Throughout the 19th century, the Yingge pottery industry was monopolized by the Wu family. "Starting in 1917, the 'Jianshan Pottery Association' and the 'Jianshan Pottery Production and Sales Association' were successively established, breaking the Wu family monopoly. In 1939, the introduction of Japan's 'Shihokyo' kiln greatly improved the efficiency and quality of Yingge ceramics"[^19]. From 1804 to the present, Yingge has produced ceramics for 222 years. On November 26, 2000, the New Taipei City Yingge Ceramics Museum officially opened, Taiwan's first museum dedicated to ceramics[^19].

Sanxia Old Street itself is another story. "From 1886 to 1920, known as the golden age of Sanjiaoyong (Sanxia), the entire Sanjiaoyong Street featured 20 dye houses besides the Lin Maoxing Dye House, including Yongyu, Jinlianchun, Liu Chengfang, Li Yifa, Zhou Shengfa, Lin Rongxiang, and Lin Yuanji, making it the most important indigo dyeing base in Northern Taiwan"[^20]. Sanxia's three major industries bloomed simultaneously: indigo dyeing (indigo plants), camphor (produced in the Sanxia mountains), and tea (promoted by British merchant Thomas W. Todd in 1868; tea factories established by the Mitsui Bussan Kaisha during the Japanese colonial era). Today, Sanxia Old Street buildings date back to 1911, "preserving commercial storefronts from the Japanese colonial era, with continuous red brick arcades and antique architecture"[^20].

From Jiufen and Jinguashi (mining) → Pingxi (coal transport) → Sanxia (indigo, camphor, tea) → Yingge (ceramics), New Taipei City's northeastern mountains and southwestern plateaus connect into a complete traditional industrial chain. This chain gradually became commercialized for tourism after the 1980s, but its skeleton remains.

## The Ketagalan and Atayal: Two Indigenous Axes

The history of New Taipei does not begin with the Spanish.

The southern bank of the Tamsui River estuary in Bali is home to the Shisanhang Site. "The Shisanhang Site is located on the southern bank at the junction of the Tamsui River estuary in Bali District, New Taipei City. It is one of the important archaeological sites in northern Taiwan. Its inhabitants lived approximately 2,000 to 400 years ago and are currently the only prehistoric residents in Taiwan confirmed to have possessed iron-smelting technology. They are very likely the ancestors of the Taiwan indigenous Ketagalan people" [^21]. In 1998, the exhibition hall was renamed the "Shisanhang Museum," which was officially completed and opened to the public in 2003.

The Ketagalan were not confined to Bali. "Many place names in present-day Taipei are transliterations from the Ketagalan language, such as Dalongdong, Beitou, Qilianshan, Bali, Monga, and Jiaru" [^21]. Place names such as Bali, Sanchih, Shimen, Tamsui, and Beitou are all Chinese character transliterations of the Ketagalan language. The place name "Tamsui" itself may have been a transliteration after Qing Dynasty Fujian immigrants heard the Ketagalan term "Tampsui" (meaning "muddy river").

However, the contact between the Ketagalan and Han Chinese was asymmetrical. After the opening of the Tamsui port in 1858, Western missionaries and Han immigrants flooded into northern Taiwan, and the Ketagalan had undergone significant sinicization by the late 19th century. Today, there are scattered descendants of the Ketagalan in the districts of Tamsui, Bali, and Sanchih, but unlike the "distinct tribal structures" of the Amis or Paiwan peoples, their language has almost disappeared.

The other axis of indigenous presence in New Taipei is in Wulai.

Wulai District is the only mountain indigenous township among the 29 districts of New Taipei City, serving as an Atayal reserve. Wulai primarily consists of four tribes: Wulai Tribe (Ulay, the main tourist area), Zhongzhi Tribe (Tampya), Xinxiian Tribe (Rahaw, Laho Tribe, meaning "dense forest"), and Fushan Tribe (Tranan, Delanann) [^22].

"The Fushan Tribe is pronounced 'Delanann' in the Atayal language. It is the most mountainous tribe in Wulai and was the first settlement for Atayal people who crossed the mountains from Shangbalin in Fuxing Township, Taoyuan" [^22]. The location of Delanann is key; it belongs to the same Atayal migration system as Lala Mountain in Fuxing District, Taoyuan. During the 17th and 18th centuries, the Atayal migrated north from Fashacun (their ancestral homeland) in Ren'ai Township, Nantou. Some crossed Lala Mountain to Wulai, while others remained in Fuxing, Taoyuan. Today, the Fushan Tribe in Wulai and the Lala Mountain Tribe in Taoyuan still speak the same Atayal dialect.

The history of Wulai's hot springs dates back to the Japanese colonial era: "In 1903, after Japanese official Nagasaka Hideo discovered it in the Nanshi Stream valley, he established a bathhouse for the Wulai Indigenous Affairs Officer" [^22]. The Japanese transformed Wulai into a backyard retreat for Taipei residents. This identity persists today, making Wulai the most "indigenized" township in New Taipei's 29 districts in terms of tourism. However, the true core of Wulai lies in the deep mountain tribes of Fushan and Xinxiian, in places that tour buses cannot reach.

From the Ketagalan (Tamsui) to the Atayal (Wulai), New Taipei encompasses two indigenous axes: one of the lowland indigenous peoples that has "almost disappeared," and one of the highland indigenous peoples that continues to exist. These two axes are not located within Taipei City.

## 1958 Yonghe Separate Municipality, 1970s Sancheng and Xinzhuang Industrial Migration, 1979 Erchong Floodway: Three Waves of Post-War Expansion

New Taipei’s population explosion occurred in three distinct waves.

The first wave took place in the 1950s in Zhonghe and Yonghe. After the Nationalist government relocated to Taiwan in 1949, "considering air defense evacuation needs for Taipei City, the government designated the Zhonghe and Yonghe areas as evacuation zones, leading to a rapid increase in population" [^23]. By 1956, Zhonghe’s population had reached 44,325, several times that of 1945. On April 1, 1958, "Zhonghe Township separated the six villages of the Guilun Lanxizhou area and one village each from the Tanqian and Xiulang areas to officially establish a town. The initial proposed name was Zhongxing Town, but due to potential confusion with Zhongxing Village, it was named Yonghe Town" [^23]. Zhonghe and Yonghe were officially established as separate towns.

Yonghe’s high density came later. In the 1980s and 1990s, "many Korean Chinese from Shandong辗转 arrived in Taiwan and settled in Yonghe" [^24]. Yonghe’s Zhongxing Street became "Korean Street," selling Korean groceries, Korean cuisine, and ginseng tea. This street slowly transformed from the 1970s onward into a commercial district selling Korean groceries and Korean cuisine. Yonghe’s food map, population density, and immigrant composition are all inextricably linked to its initial 1949 designation as a "Taiwan evacuation zone."

The second wave occurred in the 1970s in Sancheng and Xinzhuang.

"The 1960s and 1970s (the 1960s and 1970s) were the years of the Ten Major Construction Projects and economic takeoff. Sancheng also fully mobilized, with small factories nestled in neighborhood alleys, appearing every few steps. They mostly produced semi-finished industrial parts such as screws, molds, spot welding, electroplating, lathes, and plastic injection molding. Large-scale production supported half of the province’s mechanical industrial parts" [^25]. Sancheng’s factories followed Taiwan’s "home is factory" model, with people making screws, lathes, and electroplating in every alley.

"Sancheng is located at the start and end of the highway, with convenient north-south transportation. Separated by a river from prosperous Taipei City, these two superior conditions made Sancheng the city with the most immigrants in the province" [^25]. In the 1970s, rural populations from central and southern Taiwan migrated north in large numbers, and Sancheng was their first stopping point: bordered by the Tamsui River on one side and Taipei on the other, with cheap rent and abundant jobs. For many second-generation immigrants from Yunlin, Chiayi, Changhua, and Pingtung, Sancheng was the first stop in their lives.

Xinzhuang’s industrial development also began in the 1970s: "The establishment of the Touqian Industrial Zone and the Xisheng Industrial Zone marked the important turning point for Xinzhuang’s transition from an agricultural market town to an industrial city... The industries were dominated by chemicals, textiles, machinery, and metals. These industries attracted a large number of industrial workers, and as outsiders moved in, the population surged" [^25]. Xinzhuang connected with Sancheng, extending an industrial settlement belt from the west bank of the Tamsui River toward the Linkou Plateau.

However, too many factories meant too much discharge, too much drainage, and too much flood risk. In 1979, the Executive Yuan approved the first phase of the flood control plan, which began implementation in 1982 and was completed in 1984. This is the "Erchong Floodway" [^26]. "It primarily targets flood control for Sancheng, Xinzhuang, Wugu, and Luzhou, while also accommodating the relocation of legal factories near the floodway" [^26]. An artificial river channel diverted excess floodwater from Sancheng and Xinzhuang toward the sea, while simultaneously concentrating relocated illegal factories. Today, the green spaces and bike paths on both sides of the floodway were originally the floodplains of a Tamsui River tributary.

The third wave occurred in the 2000s in Linkou and the Xinzhuang Secondary Business District.

Starting in 2003, New Taipei launched the Linkou Resettlement Area and the development of the Xinzhuang Secondary Business District, absorbing the middle class moving out of Taipei who could not afford Taipei’s housing prices. Linkou transformed from military camps and farmland on a plateau into a new city of 100,000 people within a decade. Linkou National Sport University, Linkou Chang Gung Memorial Hospital, Linkou Mitsui Outlet Park, and the Linkou High-Speed Rail Station were all built after the year 2000. The Xinzhuang Secondary Business District benefited from the dual transportation advantages of the Xinzhuang Line (opened in 2010) and the Airport MRT (opened in 2017).

From 1950s Yonghe, to 1970s Sancheng and Xinzhuang, to 2000s Linkou Secondary Business District, New Taipei’s population expansion has seen a new wave every 20 years. Each wave absorbed Taipei’s spillover, the influx of northern migrants, and immigrants from central and southern Taiwan.

## The Day the County Became a Direct-Administered City: A "Largest in Taiwan" Wait of 31 Years

After absorbing three waves of expansion, the population of Taipei County reached 3.86 million in 2009.

"Since 1979, when its population had already reached the threshold for a direct-administered city, Taipei County had repeatedly sought to upgrade its status, but these efforts were consistently rejected" [^27]. However, in 1979, Taipei County had only 2.5 million people, which was already the highest population among local governments in Taiwan, surpassing Taipei City (2.3 million) and Kaohsiung City (1.3 million). Yet the central government consistently refused to allow Taipei County to upgrade. Upgrading meant a redistribution of budgets, personnel authority, and decision-making power, which neither Taipei City nor the central government wanted to relinquish.

After 31 years of advocacy, the Legislative Yuan passed the amendment to the Local System Act in May 2007, allowing Taipei County, with a population exceeding two million, to apply the regulations of a direct-administered city. The Ministry of the Interior approved the review on June 23, 2009. "On December 25, 2010, Taipei County was upgraded to the direct-administered city of 'New Taipei City,' with the Taipei County Government renamed the 'New Taipei City Government,' and the English name 'New Taipei City,' becoming the direct-administered city with the largest population in the Republic of China" [^28]. On the same day, it was upgraded simultaneously with Taichung City, Tainan City, and Kaohsiung City (the "Five Cities" reform).

The name "New Taipei City" itself has a story. The original proposal from the Taipei County Government included names such as "New Taipei City," "North Taiwan City," "Taipei City-County," and "East Taipei City," among others. Ultimately, "New Taipei" (New Taipei) was selected, a direct English translation. This differs from the three other cities upgraded at the same time—"Taichung," "Tainan," and "Kaohsiung"—which retained their historical names. New Taipei City is the only direct-administered city among the five whose name is "newly born," and it is the only example of a "county upgrading to a direct-administered city" (rather than a merger of city and county) [^28].

Banqiao was changed from "Banqiao City" to "Banqiao District" and remained the seat of the city government, becoming the **"most populous district in the country."** The population of Banqiao District is approximately 549,000 (as of the end of 2025), exceeding that of Chiayi City (267,000) and Keelung City (359,000), two complete provincial-level cities [^10]. A single district larger than a city illustrates the scale of New Taipei.

Fifteen years have passed. Today, New Taipei City has 29 districts and a total population of 4,041,149 (as of April 2026), remaining the largest direct-administered city in Taiwan [^10]. However, the number "29 districts" itself reflects New Taipei's internal fragmentation. The five major districts—Banqiao (549,000), Xinzhuang (400,000+), Zhonghe (400,000+), Sanchong (300,000+), and Xindian (300,000+)—contrast sharply with the five mountainous districts: Wulai (5,800), Pingxi (4,400), Shuangxi (8,300), Gongliao (11,000), and Pinglin (6,300). The difference is a factor of 100.

The true face of New Taipei is this: a direct-administered city containing three types of cities of different ages, including the "metropolitan areas" of Banqiao, Sanchong, Xinzhuang, and Yonghe; the "new towns" of Linkou and Danshui (sub-center); and the "mountainous/coastal remote areas" of Ruifang, Pingxi, Wulai, and Gongliao. The disparities within the 29 districts are greater than the gap between New Taipei and Taipei.

To the east: Nangang extends to Xizhi, connecting northward to Keelung. To the north: outside Tianmu lies Beitou, and further out is Danshui. To the west: Banqiao and Xinzhuang are on the opposite bank of the Tamsui River. To the south: Yonghe and Zhonghe are tightly adjacent to Gongguan, with Xindian further south.

New Taipei City’s 29 districts form a ring surrounding Taipei City’s 12 districts. A direct-administered city completely encircling another direct-administered city is a rare administrative geography worldwide.

The core function of this ring-shaped metropolis is commuting.

In April 2026, New Taipei City’s total population was 4,041,149[^10]. Among them, an estimated 390,000 people commute across county lines to work in Taipei City[^29]. Between 7 and 9 a.m., the MRT, buses, and cars heading toward Taipei City from Banqiao, Sancheng, Xinzhuang, Yonghe, Zhonghe, Xindian, and Xizhi congest the bridges across the Tamsui River to the point of immobility. Between 5 and 7 p.m., the flow reverses, causing another wave of congestion.

New Taipei City’s MRT network grew gradually. The Danshui Line opened in 1996 (from Taipei Main Station to Danshui), followed by the Zhonghe Line in 1997 (from Nanshijiao to Taipei) and the Xindian Line in 1998 (from Xindian to Taipei); these were extensions of the "Taipei Metropolitan Area Mass Rapid Transit System" planned since the 1980s. The Airport MRT opened in 2017, the Tamsui New Town Green Mountain Line in 2018, and the Circular Line officially began operations on January 31, 2020 (15.4 km long, 14 stations, with the first phase covering Xinzhuang, Banqiao, Zhonghe, and Xindian) [^30]. Today, only 16 of New Taipei City’s 29 districts have MRT service; the remaining 13 districts (including Wulai, Pingxi, Gongliao, Shuangxi, Pinglin, Shihding, Shenkeng, Sanzhi, Shimen, Jinshan, Wanli, Ruifang, and Pingxi) have no MRT lines at all.

This is the physical reality of "390,000 commuters." New Taipei City’s role in "sustaining the capital through commuting" is not merely rhetorical; it is the daily reality of people queuing to board trains at Banqiao Station, Touqianzhuang in Xinzhuang, and under Taipei Bridge in Sancheng every morning.

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** The dominant online narrative is "New Taipei City is Taipei’s bedroom community," but this narrative has two blind spots. **The first blind spot** is viewing New Taipei City as a single city, ignoring the coexistence of three different eras within its 29 districts. The Atayal villages of Wulai and the Lin Family Garden in Banqiao are in the same direct-administered city. **The second blind spot** is that the commuting relationship is actually bidirectional. While 390,000 people from New Taipei City commute to work in Taipei City, simultaneously, Taipei City’s water supply, garbage disposal, cemeteries, MRT maintenance depots, airport shuttles, and agricultural product supplies rely heavily on New Taipei City’s mountainous and riverine areas. The source of the Tamsui River water comes from the Nanxi River in Wulai; Taipei City’s landfill is in Xizhi, its cemeteries are in Jinshan, and its public graves are in Banqiao. **The relationship between New Taipei City and Taipei City is one of mutual need, not one-sided dependency.** When the Lin Family moved from Xinzhuang to Banqiao in 1853, the "Taipei Prefecture" had not yet been established. From a historical perspective, who came first is clear.

## 2000 Keelung International Ocean Music Festival: Another Axis on the Rock Beach

If you want to see New Taipei’s contemporary culture, don’t go to Banqiao or Danshui. Go to Fulong.

Fulong Beach is located in the northeasternmost corner of Keelung District, New Taipei City, hugging the Pacific Ocean. "The first International Ocean Music Festival was held on July 15, 2000, at Fulong Beach in Keelung Township, Taipei County. It lasted only one day, with approximately 8,000 attendees" [^31]. This was the starting point of what later became the "Keelung International Ocean Music Festival," one of the most important annual events in Taiwan’s independent music scene.

⚠️ The correct starting year for this music festival is 2000 (Taiwan.md’s previous Stage 0 record was 1999, which has been calibrated here).

"The Ocean Music Festival was originally initiated through a collaboration between the Taipei County Government and the independent underground music production company, Jiaotou Music. The key figures behind the event were Jiaotou Music’s boss, Chang Szu-shih, and Liao Chih-chien, then Director of the Taipei County Government’s News Office" [^31]. "Hohaiyan (Roar Ocean) is an interjection related to ocean waves in the language of the Amis, one of Taiwan’s indigenous peoples" [^31]. The festival’s name itself represents an indigenous axis.

From 2000 to the present, the Ocean Music Festival has run for 26 editions (with a hiatus in 2020–2021 due to the pandemic). Sodagreen, Fire EX., The Chairman, Mayday, Loop, Echoes, Girls and Robots, Tizzy Bac, Constant Motion, Sunset Rollercoaster, and Hello Nico—almost all of the 25 most important bands in Taiwan’s independent music scene over the past 25 years have performed on Fulong Beach.

The significance of this musical axis lies in the fact that New Taipei provided a **public cultural space for the Taipei metropolitan area that was not located in Taipei City**. Fulong is a one-and-a-half-hour drive from Taipei Main Station and a two-hour ride on a local train. Every summer, at the end of July and the beginning of August, tens of thousands of young people from Taipei City, New Taipei City, Taoyuan, and Hsinchu take the train to Fulong Station and walk from the station to the beach. This "short-term migration" is unique to New Taipei: Taipei City has no sea, Taoyuan’s beaches are too far from the urban center, but Fulong is just within 60 kilometers.

New Taipei City also attempted to extend this musical axis into the metropolitan area. In 2022, the New Taipei City Performing Arts Center opened in the Xinzhuang Secondary CBD, but a performing arts center is fundamentally different from a beach. Fulong’s uniqueness stems from the combination of elements: the nearby No. 4 Nuclear Power Plant (construction halted in 1979), Baishatan Beach, the Pacific Ocean, and summer typhoon tails. All these elements together create the feeling of a "rock beach just far enough from the capital."

## From 1628 to 2026: 400 Years Within This City Surrounding Taipei

Return to that first brick.

From laying its first stone in 1628 to 2026, Fort Santo Domingo has stood for 398 years. Throughout this period, it has witnessed the Spanish, the Dutch, the Qing dynasty, the British, Australian administration, US administration, and the Republic of China. This timeline is nearly five times longer than Taiwan’s identity as the "Republic of China" itself (counting from 1945).

The administrative name "New Taipei City" is only 15 years old this year.

Yet this metropolitan ring surrounding Taipei did not come into existence in 2010. From the 1628 Spanish Fort Santo Domingo, the 1738 first construction of Sanxia Changfu Yan Ancestor Temple (or the 1769 second reconstruction theory), the 1804 Yingge Wu’an kiln opening, the 1853 Banqiao Lin Family Mansion’s three-story main house, the 1858 opening of the Port of Taipei (Danshui), the 1869 Oolong tea reaching New York, the 1872 arrival of Mackay in Danshui, the 1890 discovery of gold mines in Jiufen and Jinguashi, the 1893 completion of the Lin Family Mansion and Garden, the 1929 opening of the Pingxi Railway, the 1947 start of Li Mei-shu’s carving of the Ancestor Temple, the 1958 division of Zhonghe and Yonghe into separate towns, the 1970s industrial immigration to Sancheng and Xinzhuang, the 2000 closure of the Taiwan Metal Corporation, the 2000 Keelung International Ocean Music Festival, and the 2010/12/25 upgrade of the county to a special municipality, New Taipei’s history is a dense 400-year timeline.

29 districts, a city with three different ages, 4.01 million people, and 390,000 daily commuters traveling to Taipei for work. Yet the city itself is two and a half centuries older than the capital. The most commonly misunderstood fact about New Taipei City is that **it has never been a "satellite city."**

From the perspective of Taipei, New Taipei is a satellite. From the perspective of Fort Santo Domingo in Danshui, Taipei is the neighbor that appeared later.

Next time you visit New Taipei, don’t just walk the corridors of the Lin Family Mansion and Garden (though that 1893 garden is still worth an afternoon). Don’t just eat fish crackers on Danshui Old Street (though Danshui truly has a commercial skeleton dating back to the Treaty of Tianjin in 1858). Try walking from Fulong Station to the seaside, listening to the Ocean Music Festival that has continued since 2000 (if you go in late July). Try taking the Pingxi Line to Jingtong Station, viewing the tourist railway transformed from the Shidi coalfield discovered in 1908 (with coal transport track remnants along the line). Try taking a bus from Wulai to the Fushan Tribe, viewing the mountain trails of the Atayal people in Delanang.

After walking through it, you will remember one thing: **New Taipei City is not a sleeping city surrounding Taipei. New Taipei City is a historical clock encompassing Taipei—the numbers on the dial just happen not to be Taipei’s.**
