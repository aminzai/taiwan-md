> **30-second summary:** Opening on June 30, 2026, the Sanying Line runs 14.29 kilometers with 12 stations, stretching from Dingpu in Tucheng through Sanhsia to Yingge, making transfers to the Tamsui–Xinyi line and Taiwan Railways more direct. Each train has only two cars with a capacity of 330 passengers; after opening, buses still handle last-mile connections outside stations, serving morning and evening time slots as well as old streets and campuses. This line shortened travel distances, but also brought capacity, noise, and land acquisition and residents' housing rights issues into the same transportation network. **Reading focus:** This is not just a lazy guide to station routes, but an observation of how transfers, capacity, and land governance occur simultaneously, starting from "how a line enters daily life."

At 1:00 a.m. on December 24, 2023, on Jianguo Road in Sanhsia, a crane placed the final U-shaped girder for the Sanying Line. The New Taipei City Mass Rapid Transit Engineering Bureau called this moment "He Long" (union of dragons): the 14.29-kilometer elevated line finally connected Tucheng, Sanhsia, and Yingge into one continuous track. [^1]

Two and a half years later, at 2:00 p.m. on June 30, 2026, trains departed from Dingpu Station, and the Sanying Line officially began operations. It was not a suddenly appearing tourist line, but a public works project that started construction on July 21, 2016, went through testing and inspection, and finally obtained operating permits. [^2] [^3]

The most worth writing about this line is not just "Sanhsia and Yingge finally have MRT." It is more like a city negotiation raised into the air: the government promises to shorten commutes, connect to Taiwan Railways and the Tamsui–Xinyi line, while local communities must answer questions about safety, noise, pedestrian space, and what the area around the stations will become before the trains pass through.

## One Line, Connecting Two Transfer Systems

The Sanying Line starts at Dingpu Station in Tucheng, connecting to the Taipei Metro Tamsui–Xinyi line. The terminus is Yingcai Fude Station in Yingge, with 12 stations along the route, totaling 14.29 kilometers, with a planned budget of NT$50.2 billion. The line passes through Tucheng, Sanhsia, and Yingge, reserving tracks for future extension to Taoyuan's Bade and connection to the Taoyuan Green Line. [^2]

Its geographic significance cannot be simply summarized as "connecting Sanhsia and Yingge to Taipei." The Sanying Line arranges originally scattered transfer actions into a sequence: transferring to the Tamsui–Xinyi line at Dingpu, transferring to Taiwan Railways at Yingge, and in the future connecting the Taipei–Taoyuan living circle to the same track network. The New Taipei City Mass Rapid Transit Engineering Bureau estimates that commuting time between Sanying areas and Taipei City center can be reduced by about 20 minutes. [^2] [^4]

Yingge Station especially shows the ambition of this design. LB08 Yingge Station is located on the south side of Taiwan Railways Yingge Station, about 130 meters apart on foot, planned to be connected via an elevated walkway to Yingge Farmers' Association, then transfer to Taiwan Railways through the Farmers' Association passage. This is not simply adding another platform, but changing originally motorcycle, shuttle bus, or walking movements into a node that can be handed over within the city. [^5]

## Why Does an Elevated Line Take So Long

The entire Sanying Line is elevated, and the route description released by the engineering bureau shows it must cross over Freeway No. 3, Taiwan Railways, Dahan River, Sanhsia River, and Yingge Creek, as well as other traffic and water conditions. The government summarized the difficult construction sections as 8 locations in the opening press release before operation, including crossings over freeways, railways, and rivers. [^2] [^4]

Elevated does not mean lifting problems into the air and making them disappear. Bridge piers must be placed between existing roads and settlements, beams must be hoisted without affecting traffic and railway operations, and electrical systems must be tested segment by segment after civil construction is completed. After completing the full line union in December 2023, the project still needed to proceed with track laying, high-voltage power supply, signal and communication equipment installation, followed by system testing. [^1]

This is also why "He Long" is not "ready to ride tomorrow." It is a visible engineering milestone, but not the end of safety verification. In April 2026, the city government stated that the Sanying Line completed stability testing and reported for initial inspection. After completing the Ministry of Transportation and Communications inspection on June 7, there were still operational improvement items to complete, until obtaining the operating permit on June 23, before officially announcing the opening. [^2] [^6]

The inspection details reported by the media show that the Ministry of Transportation and Communications divided the improvement items into 4 mandatory operational improvements, 6 general note improvements, and 5 follow-up suggested improvements. The inspection was not only about documents, but also included on-site inspections and "train cannot operate, passengers injured and need emergency rescue" simulation drills. [^7]

> **Curator's note:** The true completion day of a metro is not the day the last beam is hoisted, but the day it knows what to do next when someone is injured, equipment fails, or passengers get lost.

## After Opening, the City First Welcomes Crowds

The initial Sanying Line operated with free trial service. The New Taipei City Government Mass Transit Engineering Bureau announced that trial service would start at 2:00 p.m. on June 30, with free rides until August 31. The government regards this period as a stage to respond to local expectations and collect riding opinions. [^3]

The Central News Agency also recorded another number on the first day of trial operation: from 2:00 p.m. to 8:00 p.m. on June 30, 20,731 people rode within 6 hours. The Sanying Line uses a fully automated driverless system, with two-car train formations, and peak headway as short as 2.2 to 3 minutes. The operations team also called the trial operation a passenger load stress test, used to observe train doors, stopping, acceleration and deceleration, and passenger flow. [^8]

This number cannot be directly equated to daily commuting needs. Free rides, first-time experiences, and summer vacation crowds may all increase first-day volume. The same article by the Central News Agency also mentioned that some YouBike stations had bike shortages, which were later supplemented by the operations unit. In other words, the first test of opening does not only happen inside the train, but also in the last kilometer outside the station. [^8]

The English Taipei Times also added another dimension: during the trial operation period, daily operating hours were from 10:00 a.m. to 8:00 p.m., with planned extension to 6:00 a.m. to midnight for formal service, with fares calculated by section according to Taipei and New Taipei Metro, about NT$20 to NT$35. These differences remind us that free trial rides are a way to go online, not the complete picture of future services. [^4]

## After Opening, Why Still Take Buses

After the Sanying Line opened, buses did not retire, but were rearranged into the capillary network of the rail system. The New Taipei City Transportation Bureau announced before opening that it had completed transfer planning for all 12 stations, newly opened the Sanying 1 line and the frog bus from Yingcai Fude Station to Ming Chuan University Taoyuan Campus, and adjusted the Sanying 2 line, 706, 707, 731, 889, and the frog bus from Beida Community to Dingpu Station. [^11]

This arrangement answers questions that the Sanying Line itself cannot answer: not everyone lives next to the station, and not every destination is at the station exit. The Sanying 1 line connects Yingge Station, Ceramics Old Street Station, Guohua Station, and Taiwan Railways Yingge Station. The 731 extends to Ceramics Museum, New Taipei Core Square, and Hakka Cultural Park. Buses are therefore not "old systems that have not been eliminated after the metro was built," but tools that push the station service radius into alleys, campuses, and scenic spots. [^11]

The initial schedule more directly exposes this division of labor. During the trial operation period, the Sanying Line was not operating all day, and the Sanying 2 line extended some trips from 5:00 a.m. to 10:00 a.m. and from 8:00 p.m. to 10:00 p.m. to the Dingpu Station of the MRT and then turned back, to take care of the time periods when the MRT was not yet in service and the original passengers traveling between Tucheng. This shows that "having MRT" and "being able to rely only on MRT" are two different things. [^11]

For commuters, the real path is not a blue line, but a series of choices: taking a bus from home to the station, taking the Sanying Line to Dingpu, and then walking to the Tamsui–Xinyi line. Or directly taking the existing bus to Yongning during off-peak hours. The Beida Special Zone especially will not suddenly have only one way after the Sanying Line opens. The Transportation Bureau still listed 916, 932, 910 as the main external express buses for the Beida Special Zone in February 2026, and added a weekday 7:10 a.m. trip for the "Beida Community - Dingpu Station" frog bus, aiming to disperse peak loads on existing routes. [^18] [^19]

After opening, some people still choose buses, which does not necessarily mean the MRT failed, but may mean that transfer distance, waiting time, peak congestion, and destination location are all different. The New Taipei City Transportation Bureau's opening announcement also retained the frog bus from Beida Community to Dingpu, and allowed the Sanying 2 line to extend to Yongning during the trial operation morning and evening. Individual reports used 916 as an off-peak commuting choice. The former is a policy arrangement, the latter is personal experience, and neither can be inferred as the general result of the entire line's commuters. [^11] [^13] [^18]

> **Curator's note:** The MRT is responsible for pulling the city straight, while the bus remembers that the city is not so straight.

## Two Cars, Small and Dense or Too Small Capacity

The Sanying Line train has only two cars per train, with official basic data listing about 36 meters in length, 330 passengers capacity, and bidirectional driverless operation. The Central News Agency also recorded that during the trial operation, peak headway was as short as 2.2 to 3 minutes. Looking at these two pieces of data together, the design trade-offs of the Sanying Line are clear: it is not a long single-car train carrying all passengers, but a shorter formation with dense headway operation. [^8] [^12]

| Observation item      | Known data                                     | Conclusions that cannot be directly derived                                                               |
| --------------------- | ---------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Formation             | Two cars                                       | Cannot directly explain that peak hours are definitely insufficient                                       |
| Single train capacity | 330 people                                     | Cannot directly equate to hourly capacity                                                                 |
| Headway               | Trial operation peak as short as 2.2–3 minutes | Cannot treat trial operation headway as fixed headway for all time periods                                |
| Capacity adjustment   | Can increase frequency through dense headway   | Whether sufficient to meet long-term growth still depends on actual passenger volume and operational data |

Two cars raise questions, not because "two cars" itself equals error, but because the population of Sanying areas, transfer needs, and free trial operation crowds make people worried about future peak capacity bottlenecks. The report compiled positive and negative opinions from the community: one side worried about repeating the congestion experience of the Wenhua Line in early days, while the other side believed that short formations can be compensated by headway, and the Sanying Line's mission is more about connecting Sanhsia to Dingpu and Yingge transfer nodes. These are public discussions, not completed operational capacity evaluations. [^13]

Therefore, the question worth observing is not "whether two cars are good or not," but whether the operator publicly releases passenger volume, load factor, waiting time, and bus transfer data for different time periods. If trains can stably transport passengers through dense headway, two cars may be a reasonable configuration for medium-capacity systems. If peak queuing and waiting times continue to expand, the problem will shift from train appearance to headway, platform capacity, and subsequent public decision-making on expansion. These all require data after operation, not conclusions based on photos from the first day of opening.

## Stations Are Not Just for Waiting: Placing Local Stories on Trains

The public art of the Sanying Line is not an added decoration outside the stations, but a narrative arranged from the beginning of the project. The Ministry of Culture reprinted the New Taipei City Mass Transit Engineering Bureau data, pointing out that 360 neighborhood residents were invited to be the first art experience officers along the line, guiding visitors into stations and artworks. All 12 stations are also included in the overall design of green buildings and local culture. [^20]

Painted trains allow "every station has a story" to start moving. The New Taipei City Mass Transit Engineering Bureau launched two themed trains in June 2026, with random dispatch, so passengers do not necessarily encounter the same car every time: the Hakka culture theme uses geometric blue dye, May snow camellia flowers, and Dahan River hydrology as visual clues. The New Taipei City Art Museum theme uses silver-white, light and shadow refraction, and virtual and real lines to extend architectural imagery to the train body. [^21]

Interesting things happen inside the car. The later released indigenous-themed trains put river imagery, Amis weaving, Atayal "Little rascal," and Paiwan "Kamehameha" dolls into the journey. The NTNU Station platform also plays Amis-style music. These painted trains use random dispatch, so they are not always encountered on every ride, but instead add a little excitement to the search for Easter eggs during commuting. [^22] [^23]

## Easy Access Does Not Mean the Place Is Ready

The Sanying Line passes through Sanhsia Old Street, Yingge Ceramics Old Street, and New Taipei City Art Museum, among other landmarks. These places originally existed, and the MRT only changes the cost and path to reach them. When access becomes easier, increases in tourists, store rearrangements, and re-evaluation of housing and commercial real estate may follow. But these changes cannot be simply summarized as "driving development." [^3] [^4]

The official urban renewal plan for the area around Yingge Station treats LB08 and Taiwan Railways Yingge Station as transfer nodes for the Taipei–Taoyuan metropolitan living circle, and promotes land reuse and station activation through TOD and station city concepts. This is a future image in policy language. The true success or failure still depends on whether the elevated walkway is easy to walk, transfers are intuitive, pedestrian space outside the station is continuous, and whether the original residents living here can use these improvements. [^5]

Before the Sanying Line opened, another word of concern from local communities was "noise." A United Daily News report from January 2026 pointed out that train testing raised residents' concerns about noise and vibration along the line, with relevant units setting up measurement points at Sanhsia Junior High School, Yingge Junior High School, and surrounding communities, and discussing improvements such as sound barriers, tracks, and line restoration. These are local opinions and handling progress reported in the report, and cannot be rewritten as "the entire line exceeds noise standards" or "the project has caused definite damage." [^9]

This distinction is important. Public works projects need measurement data, and also need people living next to the tracks to describe "what they hear every day." The former answers whether it meets standards, while the latter reminds us that there are still life feelings beyond standards. Neither can replace the other.

> **Curator's note:** The MRT pulls the distant close, but also gathers originally scattered problems to stations, under bridges, and by residents' windows; "convenience" and "cost" are not mutually exclusive, but two sides of the same project.

## Maisheng Garden: Land Issues Behind a Station

The Maisheng Garden controversy of the Sanying Line is often written as "whether to set up a station here," but it actually involves station location, depot, urban planning, and land acquisition at the same time. First, let's clarify the name: many official and media materials write it as "Maisheng Garden," while daily discussions often write it as "Mai Zi Garden." This article takes "Maisheng Garden" as the main name, and treats both as different spellings of the same area. [^14] [^15]

A 2015 report by "Our Island" pointed out that Longpu Village includes Liucuiping, Maisheng Garden, and Long'enpu, and at that time, the newly planned Maisheng Garden urban plan covered about 128 hectares, of which 82 hectares were planned for expropriation and development. The New Taipei City Government explained that the plan was to respond to the Sanying Line and deal with illegal factories, while residents worried about farmland, generational housing, and living networks being broken up. The report also recorded the Interior Ministry's Regional Plan Committee raising doubts about the population and scope of the plan. These are the plans and controversies from 2015, and cannot be directly treated as the completed land situation in 2026. [^14]

A 2016 report focused the dispute on station location and development necessity: residents and local representatives questioned the setting of multiple stations within a short distance, believing that Maisheng Garden's population density was not high, and worrying that the MRT would become a reason for expropriation and land development. The government responded that the original Maisheng Garden urban plan had been suspended, Longpu Village's BL05 station would still be constructed, and the route and station location also involved relocation costs, road conditions, and restrictions around the Sanying Bridge. There are two sets of understanding of public interest here, and we cannot just leave one of them. [^15]

The controversy did not automatically disappear with the train going online. In 2020, Longpu Village residents still protested the expansion of expropriation for the BL05 depot land, and the Citizen Action Audio-Visual Database recorded residents believing that of the 14.1 hectares of land, the actual use of MRT facilities and the joint development area should be distinguished, and questioning the "MRT development zone" and developer allocation. The New Taipei City Government and MRT Bureau explained that the depot facilities covered about 12.25 hectares, plus BL05 entrances and exits and the track for entering and leaving the depot, totaling about 14.1 hectares, and that the area was reasonable compared to other depots of the same scale. [^16]

This controversy cannot be simplified as "residents oppose the MRT." From the report, residents' position is to support transportation improvements, but oppose using transportation projects as a pretext to expand land acquisition, change farmland use, or let local communities bear the asymmetric costs and benefits of development. The Taiwan Association for the Promotion of Human Rights also recorded in 2015 that the focus was on opposing forced relocation, past expropriation experiences, and the housing rights of century-old settlements. [^17]

For the Sanying Line, the problem left by Maisheng Garden is: who does a line serve, and who pays the cost first for this line? If you only look at the shortcut after opening, you will see the saved time. If you look at the depot and land together, you will also see farmland, housing, property rights, joint development, and information transparency. The public nature of the MRT must be demonstrated both on the platform and at the land negotiation table.

## Don't Treat the Opening Day as the End of the Story

The timeline of the Sanying Line has an easily overlooked rhythm: the feasibility study was approved in 2012, the main project started construction in 2016, the full line union was completed in 2023, and it did not enter formal operation until 2026. These nodes are not simply engineering progress, but the process by which local expectations are gradually institutionalized. [^2]

| Node              | What happened                                                      | What this node represents                                                                           |
| ----------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| September 3, 2012 | Executive Yuan approved the feasibility study                      | The Sanying Line entered formal planning from local needs                                           |
| July 21, 2016     | Main project started construction                                  | The route moved from paper plans to road, bridge, and station construction                          |
| December 24, 2023 | Full line union completed                                          | The elevated structure was connected, but track and electrical testing still needed to be completed |
| June 7, 2026      | Completed Ministry of Transportation and Communications inspection | Before opening, safety and operational conditions were subject to external inspection               |
| June 30, 2026     | Official opening and trial operation                               | The results of the project began to be truly used by passengers and the city                        |

The most interesting thing in this table is not "how long it waited," but that each completion cannot replace the next completion. The feasibility study cannot replace construction, the union cannot replace testing, and the inspection cannot replace the actual use by passengers. The time of public works is often not a straight line, but a process of turning promises into verifiable conditions layer by layer. [^1] [^2] [^7]

Therefore, the story of the Sanying Line should not be condensed into "built for ten years and finally opened." This sentence has a sense of time, but does not explain why the project needs to go through different institutions: the Executive Yuan's plan approval, the MRT Engineering Bureau's construction, the New Taipei MRT Company's operation, and the Ministry of Transportation and Communications' inspection and operating permit. These institutions jointly completed the line, but each also left their own responsibility boundaries.

> **Curator's note:** The opening day is suitable for taking photos, but what is truly worth remembering is those "still need to test again" that cannot be captured in a single photo.

## From One Shortcut, Seeing How the Taipei–Taoyuan Living Circle Grows

The current terminus of the Sanying Line is Yingcai Fude Station, but the tail track reserves the possibility of future extension to Taoyuan's Bade. The official route description connects this extension to the Taoyuan Green Line, Taoyuan International Airport, High-Speed Rail, and regional intercity rail transfer. The Taipei Times reported that the Bade extension section is planned to be about 4 kilometers, with a target completion in 2034. These are all planned futures, not existing services. [^2] [^4] [^10]

If the extension is realized, the Sanying Line will change from "the first MRT line in Sanhsia and Yingge" to a section of the Taipei–Taoyuan rail network. But the city will not automatically become more equal just because the line is drawn longer. Whether there are sufficient frequencies, whether transfer distances are reasonable, whether the area around stations allows pedestrians to pass safely, and whether housing and housing prices push out original residents, these issues will determine whether a line is truly a public service or just a fast track passing through local areas.

Going back to that midnight in December 2023, when the crane completed the positioning of the final U-shaped girder. At that time, what was completed was "connecting." On June 30, 2026, what was completed was "going out." What really needs to be observed next is whether, after the train passes by every day, the people of Sanhsia, Yingge, and Tucheng can reach their desired places with less time, and participate in the city decisions under the tracks with a bigger voice.

The best ending of the Sanying Line is not that everyone says it brings development, but that commuters, students, tourists, shop owners, and residents along the line can all say how this line changed their own paths. They can also point out which promises have not yet been fulfilled.
