# Luo-di Mountain: A 53-Meter Coastal Mud Volcano, a Lunar Landscape Left by Military Restrictions

> **30-Second Overview:** Luo-di Mountain in Mida, Kaohsiung, is only about 53 meters high, yet it compresses mud volcanoes, gray mudrock badlands, and coastal views onto one small hill. Two small mud vents remain at the summit, while military relics such as bunkers, trenches, and guard posts line the trail. A roughly 2-kilometer loop allows readers to see a mountain that transitioned from a restricted military zone into a nature park, preserving both geological processes and human history. [^1] [^2] [^6] [^7]

## A Hill That Is Not Tall, But Rich in Layers

Luo-di Mountain is located in Luo-di Village, Mida District, Kaohsiung City, measuring about 800 meters east-west and 600 meters north-south, with a peak height of approximately 53 meters. By these figures, it resembles a small hill rather than a mountain requiring long climbs; however, the Kaohsiung City Government and local authorities regard it as one of Taiwan's badland hills closest to the coast. [^1] [^2] [^7]

What makes it truly special is that it places several landscapes, usually studied separately, within the same walking range: mud volcanoes and mudrock erosion gullies unfold near the summit, while bunkers and trenches are hidden among the vegetation, with vantage points facing Mida District, the port, and the Taiwan Strait. Luo-di Mountain is not merely a natural scenic spot, nor is it solely a military ruin; it is a place that requires reading both geology and history simultaneously. [^3] [^7] [^8]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The most compelling aspect of Luo-di Mountain is not that it "resembles the Moon," but that the same trail layers two timelines: mud slowly piles up to form the mountain, while military installations leave human tension on the hill.

## How Mud Volcanoes Form Badlands

The Ministry of Economic Affairs' Geological Survey and Mining Management Center explains that terrestrial mud volcanoes typically require mudrock strata, groundwater, high-pressure fluids or natural gas, and fissures allowing fluids to move upward. After deep fluids rise and mix with groundwater to form slurry, they erupt, vent, or flow from the surface, and sediments gradually accumulate around the exit. [^4]

Geological data for Luo-di Mountain shows that it belongs to a mudrock area, where extensive mudrock badlands are visible nearby. The Mida District Office describes it as an extension of the Nanhua mudrock layer, noting that mudrocks have fine grains and are impermeable, leading to steep gullies and exposed surfaces after concentrated rainwater runoff. This explains why the surface appears gray-white and cracked, while vegetation is concentrated in more stable areas. [^1] [^3]

However, "mud volcano" is not an answer that can simplify all causes. The Mida District Office specifically reminds that the formation of Luo-di Mountain may differ from that of Wushan Mud Volcano. Therefore, this article only uses general geological encyclopedic descriptions of mud volcanoes and does not treat the common internet concept of a "mud intrusion body" as a locally confirmed cause for Luo-di Mountain. [^1] [^4]

The park currently records two small mud vents, each about 3 to 5 centimeters in diameter. They are not lava vents from volcano movies; they are small-scale surface activities that require close observation. Data from the Kaohsiung City Government also warns that both the mud vents and the mudrock hills are fragile landscapes and should not be touched, climbed on, or damaged. [^1] [^8]

## From "Concave Hill" to Military Restricted Zone

The name of Luo-di Mountain itself carries topographical memory. Local government records indicate that the 1742 _Reconstruction of Fujian Taiwan Prefecture Gazetteer_ already labeled it as "Aodi Mountain"; and the stories preserved by the Kaohsiung Municipal Museum of History also explain the central depression of the hill as "Aodi." [^5] [^10]

This area is connected to Mida's prehistoric cultural context. Local records state that the Tsian Song culture layers were studied by Japanese archaeologists Naoshi Kokubun as early as 1941, and pottery fragments found in farmland by local resident Sun Zhi-mu in 1954 were later confirmed as Tsian Song cultural relics. Related excavated materials include stone blades, stone axes, pottery, ornaments, and shells, dating back approximately 1,400 to 2,000 years. [^5]

These archaeological findings do not mean that visitors can search for or take artifacts in the park. On the contrary, they remind us: the story of Luo-di Mountain already contained traces of life, settlement, and land use long before it was planned as a trail. [^5]

In modern times, the relatively high position of Luo-di Mountain gave it military value. Kaohsiung City Government data indicates that the eastern side of the hill is near Gangshan Airport, while the western side faces the Taiwan Strait; it has been designated as a military restricted zone since the Japanese colonial period and has maintained long-term military use after WWII. Today, facilities such as bunkers, trenches, ditches, and guard posts can still be seen on the mountain. [^7] [^8]

After the military withdrew its guards in 2006, Luo-di Mountain was managed by the Mida District Office and gradually transitioned into a nature park. This transition was not about clearing away military history to reconfigure a recreational park, but rather allowing the original mudrock topography, vegetation, and military relics to enter public view together. [^1] [^7]

## Legend Does Not Equal Geology, But It Is Equally Important

The "Legend of Luo-di Mountain" by the Kaohsiung Municipal Museum of History, written by Chang Che-nan, records a local story featuring Shoushan (壽山), Luxhan (祿山), Jade Emperor (玉皇大帝), and Lei Gong. The story says that Luxhan, for boasting of his towering height, was cut in half by Lei Gong and fell into Miluo Port, forming the central depression of Luo-di Mountain; the remaining half became what is later called Banping Mountain. [^10]

This legend cannot replace the geological explanation of the mud volcano, but it preserves another type of local knowledge: how adults answered children's questions about "why there is a small mountain by the sea" using familiar mountains, deities, and place names. Geological encyclopedias discuss high-pressure fluids and slurry deposition; legends speak of mountain gods and Lei Gong. By placing both narratives together, Luo-di Mountain becomes not just a measured topography, but also a living scene observed, questioned, and narrated by local residents. [^4] [^10]

## A Walk to Read Three Scales

The main loop trail of Luo-di Mountain is about 2 kilometers long, with an altitude range from approximately 23 to 53 meters and a drop of about 30 meters, taking about one hour to walk. The ground includes paving stones, wooden boardwalk steps, and asphalt; the outer northern loop and the southwestern dirt path have safety ropes installed in some sections. This information comes from hiking trail records, and actual conditions may change due to construction, rainy season, or maintenance. [^6]

The most worth slowing down for is the moment moving from green shade to exposed mudrock. The path in the previous section might have tree shade and native plants, but the next turn suddenly presents gray-white gullies. Both the Kaohsiung Tourism Network and the Kaohsiung City Government state that the park features a combination of mudrock badlands, native vegetation, coastal views, and military facilities; this alternation of "green—gray-white—camouflage" is Luo-di Mountain's most vivid visual rhythm. [^2] [^7] [^8]

The bunkers at the high point later became observation decks. Official records state that from above, one can view Mida District, Dàxiǎo Gangshan (大小岡山), Nanliao, Yong’an, and Xingda Port on the Taiwan Strait when the weather is good. Previously, the height served military observation; now, it allows general visitors to view the coast and sunset—the function has changed, but the memory left by the location has not disappeared. [^2] [^7]

The northern side of the park originally had a mountain ditch, which was later supplemented with a tiered scenic suspension bridge to allow the trail to cross topographical differences. The bridge itself is a facility, but it also reminds visitors: this route is not about leveling the hill and paving it into a flat road, but about arranging the walk along the original topography. [^6] [^7]

> What is most worth seeing at the observation deck is not just "how far one can see," but how the power of viewing has been redirected: the same high point, once used for defense, now invites ordinary people to stand and look at the sea.

## A Journey of Mountains, Sea, and Local Culture

The Mida District local journey curated by the Ministry of Education's "Walking Read DOC" arranges the Tsian Song Museum, Luo-di Mountain Nature Park, and Nanliao Coastal Light Corridor on a single route, also listing straw mats, conical hats, and shadow puppetry as extended cultural content. This is not a fixed itinerary set by the park, but rather a local curatorial proposal: first understanding the land through prehistoric culture, then traversing the mud volcanoes and military relics, and finally returning to the coast and fishing villages. [^13]

If Luo-di Mountain were scheduled as a half-day or full-day trip, "ascending the mountain" and "descending to the sea" could be seen as two sides of the same coastal area. The mountain offers views of mudrocks, vegetation, and military sightlines; the coast offers views of ports, fish farms, and local industries. This connection does not require cramming sights in, but rather making the geographical relationship itself the main theme of the journey. [^6] [^13]

## Conservation Is Not Automatic After Opening

Environmental reports from 2012 recorded that locals wished to include Luo-di Mountain with Dàxiǎo Gangshan and Jīguān Mountain in Shoushan National Natural Park, citing mud volcano topography, military relics, and shell mound sites. They mentioned that geological and ecological surveys were planned at the time, but no definite timeline for inclusion was set. This is a historical record of policy discussion and cannot be interpreted as Luo-di Mountain currently belonging to Shoushan National Natural Park. [^12]

Landscape registration data lists weathering and erosion as threats requiring attention. For visitors, the most direct conservation action is small: stay on existing trails, do not trample the mudrock hills, do not touch the mud vents, do not move stones or artifacts, and do not use military relics as climbing structures. The value of badlands lies in their fragility—the fact that they are still being rewritten by rainwater—and protecting them is not about fixing all changes, but about preventing human interference from accelerating their disappearance. [^3] [^8]

## Visitor Information

Information listed on the Kaohsiung Tourism Network includes 24-hour operation, trails, observation decks, public restrooms, and parking lots; the park address is at the summit road in Luo-di Village, Mida District, Kaohsiung City. The Kaohsiung City Government data suggests turning onto Summit Road from Provincial Highway 17. Visitors should check official announcements or the latest conditions before departure, as opening hours, facilities, and trail status may change. [^2] [^7]

If only the main loop is walked, it takes about one hour; if one wishes to closely examine mudrock gullies, mud vents, bunkers, plants, and sea views, more time should be reserved. Pay special attention to dirt paths and exposed mudrocks after rain, and adjust the route according to on-site notices. [^6]

## A Final Look at Luo-di Mountain

Luo-di Mountain's height of only 53 meters is sufficient for one to see geological processes, prehistoric life, military strategy, and local openness layered onto a single small hill. Its value is not established by "grandeur": the fragility of the badlands, the smallness of the mud vents, and the silence of the bunkers prevent it from being like a neatly organized postcard view. [^1] [^3] [^5]

As one walks down from the observation deck, the sea breeze still blows from the west, and the mudrock gullies are slowly changing shape during the rainy season. The military boundary has been removed, but geological time has not. Luo-di Mountain does not leave a beautiful conclusion of "former restricted zone turned scenic spot," but rather a more memorable reminder: some places can be seen precisely because humans once learned to temporarily refrain from touching them.
