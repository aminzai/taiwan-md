> **30-second overview:** In 2020, Taichung's first MRT Green Line was suspended due to a coupler fault just six days into its trial run, and did not officially open until the following year. This 16.7-kilometer elevated line connects Beitun, Xinxing Road, and the THSR Taichung Station, ushering a city long dependent on buses, scooters, and cars into an era where transfers, safety, and walking must be discussed. What truly changed was not just where people boarded, but how Taichung began to reimagine its own distances through the lens of "the next stop."

On November 16, 2020, Lu Shiue-yen announced the start of the Taichung MRT Green Line's trial service, marking the city's first-ever MRT. Over 70,000 passengers entered the stations that day, as if a grand urban tasting session—people were not merely riding, but verifying whether Taichung could truly become a rail-based city.[^1]

Six days later, the semi-permanent coupler axle in the train compartment fractured, grounding all 18 trains. The Green Line, originally scheduled to officially open by the end of 2020, did not resume service until April 25 of the following year.[^2]

The most noteworthy aspect of this line is precisely that it did not unfold according to the script laid out at its ceremony. Taichung MRT is not a full stop at "finally, we have an MRT." Instead, it is a city with wide roads, many scooters and cars, and a sprawling layout that, for the first time, had to place safety, transfers, and trust all on the same map.

## A City Starts with "Having No MRT"

Taichung’s MRT plan did not appear as a sudden blueprint. Official network data traces the planning origin back to the 1990s. The Wu-Ri-Wenxin Beitun Line was approved as the initial network by the Executive Yuan in 2004. In 2008, the Ministry of Transportation and Communications, the Taichung City Government, and the Taipei City Government signed construction and operation agreements. Construction began in October 2009. [^4]

This period was long, long enough for the line’s name, construction units, and the city’s imagination to go through several rounds. The Green Line ultimately became an approximately 16.7-km elevated medium-capacity transit system, starting from Beitun Terminal, looping around the west side of the urban area, and connecting to Taichung HSR Station, with a total of 18 stations. It does not directly enter the TRA Taichung Station; this choice reminds people that the MRT is not a magic wand that solves all transportation problems at once, but rather a skeleton chosen first for its feasibility. [^5]

> What a single MRT line truly changes is not just commuting time, but the way people judge "whether this place counts as the city center."

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> The counter-intuitive nature of the Taichung Green Line lies not in it being faster than other cities, but in turning the debate "Does Taichung need an MRT?" into a daily life question that can be personally verified every morning.

The scale of the project is also easily obscured by the word "opening." _Railway Gazette_ reported that Green Line construction began in 2009, with a route length of approximately 16.7 km and total costs of approximately NT$59.3 billion, of which the central government bore about NT$32.8 billion. The trains use an unmanned driving system, with a fleet consisting of 36 sets of two-car trains. [^5]

The same project required construction above dense urban blocks. Project records from escalator and elevator supplier Otis mention that the project included 117 heavy-duty escalators and 59 elevators, with some station construction spaces only about 5 to 6 meters from surrounding buildings. These numbers are not just equipment lists; they explain why the Taichung Green Line has always felt like a surgery running parallel to existing streets. [^6]

## The Crowd on the First Day, The Crack on the Sixth Day

On the first Saturday of trial operations, CNA recorded that the Green Line had already seen 47,094 passengers by 2 p.m., with the day’s total ridership estimated to potentially break 90,000. Taichung HSR Station, Beitun Terminal, and City Hall Station became the three hottest nodes. Songzhu Station also added holiday shuttle buses connecting to the Xinshe Flower Sea and the Taichung International Flower Carpet Festival. [^1]

This scene looked like a city queuing to welcome the future. Passengers held ticket cards, took photos of the train cars and elevated tracks, and MRT merchandise was also sold at the "Little Green Welfare Society" at Taichung HSR Station. The MRT was treated as a transportation tool, but also as a city souvenir one could take home.

But on November 22, the first trial operation ended early in the evening. Subsequent inspections by the Taichung MRT revealed that the shaft of the traction device in the semi-permanent coupler between the EMU train cars had fractured. The Taichung MRT Company’s chronicle records that all 18 trains were halted for inspection and repair. The city government established an accident review committee, followed by the replacement and upgrade of the shaft and a 300-km operation test. [^2]

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> The trust of a new city is not won on the day of opening. It grows slowly only after the failure, when "what broke, how it was fixed, and who confirmed it" are explained clearly, piece by piece.

CNA’s timeline makes this delay clear. The Green Line was originally expected to open in December 2020. After the failure, it did not resume trial operations until March 25, 2021, and did not officially operate until April 25. This is not simply an engineering delay, but the most difficult kind of delay for public transport: passengers have already seen the stations and ridden the trains, yet still cannot treat it as a reliable daily routine. [^2]

From an engineer’s perspective, the lesson of this line is specific. Testing is not a ritual before opening, but work that must continue to be revisited after opening. From a passenger’s perspective, it is another issue: a line can be very new, but it cannot require passengers to use their own bodies to complete the final verification for it.

## The Green Line Is Not Just Connecting Two Endpoints

The Green Line is easiest to understand as a shuttle linking Nangang to the High-Speed Rail station. That understanding is not wrong, but it is too narrow. The line simultaneously folds Nangang’s residential neighborhoods, Songzhu’s railway transfer point, the commercial activity along Wenhsin Road, the administrative core around the city government, and the THSR Taichung Station into a single fare and timetable.

The line also makes Taichung’s urban scale tangible. Elevated tracks pass overhead, and stations with escalators lift passengers from street level to another height. Places that sit merely adjacent on a map now share the concrete unit of “the next stop.” The city is no longer described only by road length, but also by the waiting time between stations.

Railway Gazette notes that the Green Line runs along the western side of downtown and does not serve Taichung Railway Station. That gap makes it more of a first skeleton than a finished network. The Taichung City Government MRT Engineering Bureau currently includes the Green Line’s extension to Dapeng, Changhua, and the subsequent Blue, Orange, Purple, and Red lines in its overall rail network vision.[^4] [^5]

## The Impact of an MRT Line Is Not Just in Boarding Numbers

MRTs are often discussed in terms of ridership and housing prices, but neither alone represents urban transformation. A 2022 study using a hedonic pricing model based on residential transaction data from 2012 to 2021 analyzed the residential market in administrative districts the Green Line passes through. It found that for every 100 meters increase in distance from a residential property to an MRT station, the unit price decreased by an average of 0.4%. The study also noted that in areas with more developed bus networks, the marginal improvement in accessibility provided by the MRT was smaller. [^7]

Placing this result back into Taichung’s transportation conditions makes it even more interesting. Taichung originally had buses, scooters, and wide roads; the Green Line was added to a system with existing transportation habits. Whether it can change lives depends on whether people are willing to switch to buses at their doorstep, transfer at Songzhu or HSR stations, and leave the last leg to walking.

In other words, changes in housing prices near MRT stations are just one result. The more important question is whether stations truly allow people to ride scooters less, allow children to walk to the station entrance on their own, and give non-drivers more options in Taichung. The success of transportation infrastructure should not be measured only by "how much nearby prices increased," but also by asking "who gained an additional way to arrive."

## The City Above, the Everyday Below

Most of the Green Line runs on elevated tracks, making each station a vertical gateway into the city. Passengers first walk from the sidewalk, street corner, or bus stop to the station entrance, then rise along escalators and elevators before finally reaching the platform. Otis's project records note that the Green Line has 18 stations in total, and during construction, 117 heavy-duty escalators and 59 elevators had to be installed without disrupting daily vehicular and pedestrian traffic.[^6]

This vertical circulation comes at a cost. For those in a hurry, the distance from the stairwell is the transfer cost. For those pushing strollers, using wheelchairs, or carrying luggage, whether the elevator is easy to find determines whether they can use the MRT at all. The accessibility of the MRT does not begin when the train arrives at the platform—it is decided from the very first step from the sidewalk to the entrance.

The Taichung Green Line thus serves as a reminder suspended above the city: tracks can span across city blocks, but daily life cannot be left hanging in midair. The sidewalks, shelters, bus connections, and nighttime lighting around each station will determine whether an elevated MRT is a convenient part of everyday life or merely a beautiful line on a map.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> An elevated MRT lifts the train high, but it does not lift passengers off the ground. The real urban engineering still happens in the stretch of road before the entrance.

## How Stations Connect Two Modes of Transport

Photos of the City Hall Station platform make the spatial logic of the elevated MRT concrete. The train stops above the city, while below the platform remains the daily intersection of offices, commerce, and roads. What passengers see is a train entering the station; what the city truly bears is how to connect the station entrance, escalators, platform, and ground pedestrian paths into a flow that does not require repeated guessing. [^10]

Photos of Songzhu Station present another problem. The MRT and TRA are not abstract two nodes; they must be connected by exits, stairs, covered spaces, and directional signs. When travelers drag luggage or transfer during peak hours, the transfer distance directly becomes a person’s rating of public transport. This explains why the value of a route cannot be judged solely by train speed, but also by whether the doors between the two systems are truly opened together. [^11]

From an urban design perspective, the station bodies in the pictures are not background; they are the result of transportation policy. Elevated tracks lift the main transportation function, making the ground level require safer crossings, clear signage, and continuous pedestrian spaces. If the station body only completes the train function but not the walking function, the MRT will still be cut into isolated segments of equipment.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> The most useful time for pictures is not to add scenery to the article, but to turn the abstract statement "transfers are important" into a visible door, a stretch of road that must be walked.

## In 2023, Safety Issues Return Outside the Train Doors

On May 10, 2023, a Green Line train collided with a crane arm that had fallen from a construction site onto the tracks near Fengle Park Station, causing the death of passenger Lin Shu-ya and injuring 10 others. After the accident, the Taichung MRT proposed strengthening emergency hand signals, allowing passengers to prevent doors from closing, and making it easier for on-board personnel to access tool bags. It also planned to add station emergency buttons and devices better able to identify obstacles. [^8]

This accident pushed the safety boundary from inside the train to the city. The MRT company can manage stations, trains, and tracks, but construction sites next to elevated tracks, crane arms, and construction management also directly enter the passenger’s travel path. When the MRT passes through dense urban blocks, every project in the city can become part of the rail system.

Accident investigation and subsequent improvements should not be treated as an appendix to the opening story. The Taichung MRT chronicle records that the Railway Transportation Safety and Accident Investigation Council released the accident investigation report in 2023, and all 5 recommendations proposed by the city government and the Taichung MRT have been completed. This "completion" still needs to be continuously tested, because safety is not a closing certificate, but whether everyone knows what to do before the next anomaly occurs. [^2]

> The most important equipment of the MRT, besides the sensors on the tracks, also includes the shared reaction of every person knowing when to stop.

## What Passengers Will Ultimately Remember

The Taichung MRT official chronicle shows that the Green Line broke through cumulative ridership of 10 million in September 2022, created a record of 59,000 passengers during the 2023 New Year’s Eve transport rush, and set a new monthly ridership record in December 2024. By January 2026, official data recorded cumulative ridership breaking 60 million, with overall passenger satisfaction in 2025 at 95.6%. These numbers show that the Green Line has gradually shifted from a novel experience to daily use. [^2]

But what passengers truly remember may not be 16.7 km, nor a specific ridership record, but the light and shadows at Wenzheng Forest Park Station, the transfer at Songzhu Station, the train on the HSR platform, or one rainy day when the escalator entrance was crowded with people who had not yet learned how to ride the MRT.

The urban significance of the Taichung MRT is hidden in these unglamorous repetitions. Some people ride the train to work for the first time; some take children to the HSR station; some no longer need to find parking spots just to see an event. Fixed tracks turn the city’s future into an action that can be repeated every day. When station bodies, platforms, transfers, and streets are strung together by the same journey, the MRT truly transforms from an engineering term into urban life.

## Transfers Are Not an Add-On, but the Second Track of the Green Line

The 18 stations of the Green Line will not complete a journey on their own. For most passengers, the real route runs from home to the bus stop, from the bus stop to the MRT entrance, from the platform to the destination, and then from the last stop into the office, school, or mall. Every handoff is an opportunity to recalculate the time. If any segment lacks shelter, lacks clear signage, or has an overly long wait, passengers may return to the familiar scooter.

The importance of Songzhu Station and Taichung HSR Station lies in the fact that they make the Green Line more than a single path between two ends. The former puts the MRT into the TRA transfer network; the latter places city rail, high-speed rail, shuttle buses, and intercity transport on the same itinerary. The value of these nodes is not how large the station body itself is, but whether vehicles of different speeds can connect within a single trip. [^1] [^4]

The next question for the Taichung MRT Green Line is therefore not as simple as "how many more lines to build." As the network grows longer, fare systems, headways, transfer distances, and information design must all grow together. If each line is convenient on its own but requires a long walk to transfer, the city may gain more options rather than easier access to daily life. This is also why the city government plans the Green Line extension together with the Blue, Orange, Purple, and Red lines within an integrated network. [^4]

From an engineering management perspective, transfers remain the clearest test of whether public infrastructure is user-centered. Train punctuality can be proven by system records, but whether passengers are willing to ride again depends on whether they can safely cross the street after alighting, whether they know where the next bus departs, and whether they can avoid detours when carrying luggage. The Green Line's urban score will ultimately rest on these details that never appear in ribbon-cutting photos.

## After the Green Line, What Else Must Taichung Answer?

The first line brought Taichung into the MRT era, but did not automatically make everyone an MRT passenger. The connection between the Green Line and buses, the walking environment around stations, the transfer between TRA and HSR, and whether future networks like the Blue Line and Red Line can proceed as planned will determine whether this line ultimately becomes an isolated exhibit or the skeleton of urban transportation. [^4]

This is also the most memorable contradiction of the Taichung MRT: it declares that the city is changing via elevated tracks, yet still must rely on buses, walking, and existing roads to complete every journey. The MRT did not replace the city; it rearranged the originally scattered transportation choices.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> What Taichung truly waits for is an urban daily life where "one can arrive without driving," not just another MRT. The Green Line has begun to answer, but has not finished answering.

The failure in November 2020 taught Taichung that an MRT must first learn to correct itself. The crane arm accident in 2023 taught it that MRT safety cannot be confined within station walls. As the Green Line continues to accumulate passengers, it continues to turn the city’s promise into a route that can be checked.

The destination is not Taichung HSR Station, nor the next color on the future network map. For passengers, the true destination is a day when they no longer need to first ask "does Taichung have an MRT," but can directly check when the next train arrives.
