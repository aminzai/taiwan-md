> **30-Second Overview:** In 2020, the first Taichung green line began trial operation but halted on its sixth day due to a coupler failure, only officially opening the following year. This 16.7 km elevated route connecting Beitun, Wenxin Road, and HSR Taichung brought a city long reliant on buses, scooters, and cars into an era that required discussion of transfers, safety, and walking. What it truly changed was not just where people boarded, but how Taichung began to redefine its distance using "the next stop."

On November 16, 2020, Lu Hsiao-yan announced the start of trial operations for the Taichung MRT Green Line; this city had its first MRT. On that day, over 70,000 passengers used the line, resembling a large urban tasting event where people were not just riding, but confirming whether Taichung could truly become a city moved by rail. [^1]

Six days later, the semi-permanent coupler between train cars snapped, leading to the complete suspension of all 18 trains. The green line, originally scheduled for official opening at the end of 2020, did not resume service until April 25 of the following year. [^2]

What makes this line most worth writing about is that it did not follow a ceremonial script. Taichung MRT is not a period mark signifying "finally having an MRT"; it is a place with wide roads, many scooters and cars, and a dispersed urban layout, which had to put safety, transfers, and trust onto the same map for the first time.

## A City That Started with "No MRT"

The plan for the Taichung MRT did not appear as a sudden blueprint. Official network data traces the planning origins back to the 1990s. The Wuri-Wenxin Beitun Line was approved by the Executive Yuan as an initial network in 2004, and construction and operation agreements were signed by the Ministry of Transportation, Taichung City Government, and Taipei City Government in 2008, with construction beginning in October 2009. [^4]

This period was long, long enough for the line's name, construction contractors, and urban imagination to change several times. The Green Line ultimately became a roughly 16.7 km elevated mass transit system, running from Beitun Main Station southwards past the western part of the city to HSR Taichung Station, totaling 18 stations. It did not directly connect to Taiwan Railway Taichung Station; this choice also reminds people that the MRT is not magic that solves all transportation problems at once, but rather selecting a skeleton that can be realized first. [^5]

> A single MRT line changes not just commuting time, but the way people judge whether "this place counts as an urban center."

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The counter-intuitive nature of the Taichung Green Line is not that it was faster than other cities, but that it turned the debate of "Does Taichung need an MRT?" into a daily life issue that people can personally verify every morning.

The scale of the construction was also easily overshadowed by the "opening." Railway Gazette reported that the green line construction, which began in 2009, spanned approximately 16.7 km with a total cost of about NT$59.3 billion, of which the central government bore about NT$32.8 billion. The trains use an automated driving system, with a fleet consisting of 36 two-car sets. [^5]

The same project was constructed above dense urban blocks. Project records from Otis, a provider of elevators and escalators, mention that the construction included 117 heavy-duty escalators and 59 elevators, with some station construction sites only about 5 to 6 meters away from surrounding buildings. These figures are not just equipment lists; they illustrate why the Taichung Green Line has always felt like an operation running parallel to existing streets. [^6]

## The Crowds of Day One Versus the Cracks of Day Six

On the first Saturday of trial operations, Central News reported that by 2 PM, the green line had recorded 47,094 passengers, with the total daily ridership estimated to exceed 90,000. HSR Taichung Station, Beitun Main Station, and City Hall Station were the three busiest nodes, while Songzhu Station also served as a connection point for holiday shuttles to Xinsha Flower Sea and the Taiwan International Flower Carpet Festival. [^1]

This scene was like a city queuing up to welcome the future. Passengers held tickets, photographed the train cars and elevated tracks, and MRT merchandise was sold at the "Little Green Welfare Society" in HSR Taichung Station. The MRT was treated as transportation and also as an urban souvenir that could be taken home.

But on November 22, the first trial operation ended early in the evening. Subsequent inspections by Zhongjie found that the traction device axis of the semi-permanent coupler between train cars had snapped. Zhongjie's official records state that all 18 trains were grounded for maintenance, and the city government established an accident review committee, which later replaced the axle with an upgraded version and conducted a 300 km operational test. [^2]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> The trust of a new city is not won on the day it opens. It is slowly built by clearly explaining "what broke, how it was fixed, and who verified it" after the failure.

Central News’ timeline clearly documented this delay. The green line was originally scheduled to open in December 2020, but after the failure, trial operations resumed on March 25, 2021, with official operation beginning on April 25. This was not merely a construction delay; it was the kind of delay that public transport handles most poorly: passengers had seen the stations and ridden the trains, yet could not yet treat it as reliable daily transit. [^2]

From an engineer's perspective, the lesson from this line is concrete. Testing is not a pre-opening ceremony but ongoing work that requires looking back even after opening. From a passenger's perspective, it is another issue: a line can be new, yet cannot demand passengers complete its final validation with their own bodies.

## The Green Line Is Not Just Connecting Two Endpoints

The green line is most easily understood as a shuttle from "Beitun to HSR." This understanding is not wrong, but it is too narrow. It simultaneously places Beitun's residential areas, the TRA transfer at Songzhu, commercial activities on Wenxin Road, the administrative core around City Hall, and HSR Taichung Station within one ticketing system and service interval.

This line also made the urban scale of Taichung tangible. The elevated track passes above the streets, and stations with escalators lift people from ground level to another height. Places that were merely adjacent on a map now have the concrete unit of "the next stop." The city is no longer described only by road length, but also by the waiting time between stations.

Railway Gazette pointed out that the green line bypasses TRA Taichung Station in its western loop. This gap makes it more like an initial skeleton than a completed network. The City Government's MRT Engineering Bureau currently plans to extend the green line to Dakeng and Changhua, integrating subsequent lines such as the Blue, Orange, Purple, and Red Lines into the overall network vision. [^4] [^5]

## The Impact of One MRT Line Is Not Just Measured by Ridership

The MRT is often discussed in terms of ridership and housing prices, but neither can represent urban transformation alone. A 2022 study that created a feature price model using residential transaction data from 2012 to 2021 analyzed the housing market along the green line's administrative areas and found that for every 100 meters increase in distance from an MRT station, the average unit price decreased by 0.4%. The study also noted that where bus networks were more developed, the marginal improvement of accessibility from the MRT was smaller. [^7]

Placing this result back into Taichung's transportation conditions is even more interesting. Taichung already had buses, scooters, and wide roads; the green line added a system to an area with established transit habits. Whether it could change lives depended on whether people were willing to switch from a scooter at their doorstep, transfer at Songzhu or HSR Station, and complete the last leg by walking.

In other words, changes in housing prices near MRT stations are just one outcome. The more important question is whether the station truly reduced the need for a short scooter ride, allowed children to walk to the station entrance, and provided non-drivers with more options in Taichung. The success of transportation construction should not only be measured by "how much nearby property increased," but also by "who gained an additional mode of arrival."

## The City Above the Stations, The Daily Life Below

Most sections of the green line are elevated, making the stations a vertical urban entrance. Passengers first walk from the overpass, intersection, or bus stop to the station structure, then ascend via escalators and elevators, before finally reaching the platform. Otis project records state that the 18 Taichung MRT stations required installing 117 heavy-duty escalators and 59 elevators while not interfering with daily traffic and pedestrian flow. [^6]

This vertical movement has its costs. For those in a hurry, the distance to the stairs is the transfer cost. For those pushing strollers, using wheelchairs, or carrying luggage, whether an elevator is easy to find is a prerequisite for using the MRT. Accessibility of the MRT does not begin when the train reaches the platform; it is determined by the first stretch from the sidewalk to the entrance.

The Taichung Green Line thus serves as a reminder hung above the city: tracks can cross blocks, but life cannot be suspended. The sidewalks, rain shelters, bus transfers, and nighttime lighting around the stations determine whether an elevated MRT is a convenient daily routine or just a pretty line on a map.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Elevating the train does not lift the passenger off the ground. True urban engineering still happens on the stretch leading up to the entrance.

## How Stations Connect Two Modes of Transport

The photo of City Hall Station platforms makes the spatial logic of elevated MRT concrete. The trains stop above the city, but below the platform is the daily life where offices, commerce, and roads intersect. What passengers see is a train arriving at the station; what the city truly manages is connecting the station entrance, escalators, platform, and ground pedestrian path into a seamless flow that requires no guesswork. [^10]

The photo of Songzhu Station presents another issue. The MRT and TRA are not abstract nodes; they must be connected by entrances/exits, stairs, shelters, and directional signs. When travelers drag luggage or transfer during peak hours, the transfer distance directly becomes a score given to public transport. This also explains why the value of a line cannot be judged solely by train speed, but also by whether the gates between two systems are truly open together. [^11]

From an urban design perspective, the stations in the pictures are not mere backdrops; they are the result of transportation policy. The elevated track raises the main transport function, making the ground level more reliant on safe crossings, clear signage, and continuous pedestrian space. If a station only completes the train function but fails to complete the walking function, the MRT will remain fragmented into isolated equipment.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> When pictures are most useful, it is not to add scenery to an article, but to turn the abstract concept of "transfers are important" into a visible gate and a path that must be walked.

## Safety Issues Return Outside the Doors in 2023

On May 10, 2023, a crane falling from a construction site onto the track near Fengle Park Station struck the green line train, causing passenger Lin Shu-ya to die and 10 people to be injured. After the accident, Zhongjie proposed measures such as strengthening emergency signals, allowing passengers to stop the doors from closing, and making it easier for onboard staff to access tool bags, while also planning to install emergency buttons at stations and devices that are more sensitive to obstacles. [^8]

This incident pushed the safety boundary from inside the train to within the city. The MRT company can manage the station, the train, and the track, but construction sites, cranes, and construction management next to elevated tracks also directly enter passengers' routes. When the MRT passes through dense urban blocks, every piece of infrastructure in the city can become part of the rail system.

Accident investigation and subsequent improvements should not be treated as an appendix to the opening story. Zhongjie’s official records state that the safety department released an accident investigation report in 2023, addressing all 5 suggestions made by the city government and Zhongjie. This "completion" still requires continuous verification because safety is not a closing certificate; it is knowing what to do before the next anomaly occurs. [^2]

> The most important equipment of the MRT, besides sensors on the track, includes the collective response that everyone knows when to stop.

## What Passengers Will Remember in the End

Zhongjie's official records show that the green line surpassed 10 million cumulative passengers in September 2022, reached 59,000 passengers during the New Year period in 2023, and set a new monthly record in December 2024. By January 2026, official data recorded cumulative ridership exceeding 60 million, with overall passenger satisfaction reaching 95.6% in 2025. These figures show that the green line has moved from a novel experience toward daily use. [^2]

However, what passengers truly remember may not be the 16.7 km or a specific ridership record, but the light and shadow at Wenxin Forest Park Station, the transfer at Songzhu Station, the train on the HSR platform, or when an escalator entrance was crowded with people who hadn't learned how to ride the MRT during a rainy day.

The urban significance of the Taichung MRT is hidden in these unglamorous repetitions. Some people took their first commute by it; some brought children to HSR Station; some no longer needed to look for parking for an event. The fixed track turned the city's future into a daily, repeatable action. Only when the stations, platforms, transfers, and streets are connected by one journey does the MRT truly become urban life, rather than just an engineering term.

## Transfers Are Not Extra Credit; They Are the Green Line's Second Track

The 18 stations of the green line do not complete a journey in isolation. For most passengers, the real route is from home to the bus stop, from the bus stop to the MRT entrance, from the platform to the destination, and then walking into the office, school, or mall from the last station. Every transfer requires recalculating time. If any segment lacks shelter, clear signage, or has too long a wait, passengers may revert to their familiar scooters.

The importance of Songzhu Station and HSR Taichung Station lies in them making the green line more than just a single path between two ends. The former integrates the MRT into the TRA transfer network; the latter places urban rail alongside high-speed rail, feeder buses, and intercity travel on one itinerary. The value of these nodes is not determined by the size of the station itself, but by whether different modes of transport can connect in a single outing. [^1] [^4]

The next question for the Taichung Green Line is not simply "how many more lines to build." As the network grows longer, ticketing, service intervals, transfer distances, and information design must all grow together. If each line is convenient on its own, but transfers require a long walk, the city gains more options, not easier accessibility. This is why the government planned the green line's extension along with the Blue, Orange, Purple, and Red Lines in the overall network. [^4]

From an engineering management perspective, transfers are the best measure of whether public construction is user-centric. Train punctuality can be proven by system records; whether passengers are willing to ride again depends on whether they can safely cross an intersection after exiting, know where the next bus comes from, and travel without detours while carrying luggage. The urban performance of the green line will ultimately rest on these details that do not appear in ribbon-cutting photos.

## What Taichung Must Answer After the Green Line

The first line ushered Taichung into the MRT era, but it did not automatically make everyone an MRT passenger. The connection between the green line and buses, the walking environment around stations, the transfers with TRA and HSR, and whether future networks like the Blue and Red Lines can proceed as planned will determine if this line is an isolated exhibit or the skeleton of urban transport. [^4]

This is the most memorable contradiction of the Taichung MRT: it uses elevated tracks to declare that the city is changing, yet it still relies on buses, walking, and existing roads to complete every journey. The MRT did not replace the city; it re-organized previously dispersed transportation choices.

> Taichung is truly waiting for an urban routine where one "can arrive without driving," not just another MRT line. The green line has begun answering, but not finished.

The failure in November 2020 taught Taichung that an MRT must first learn to correct itself. The crane accident in 2023 taught it that MRT safety cannot be confined within the station walls. As the green line continues to accumulate passengers, it continues to turn the city's promises into verifiable routes.

The destination is not HSR Taichung Station, nor is it the next color on a future network map. For the passenger, the true destination is one day when they no longer have to ask "Does Taichung have an MRT?" but can directly check what time the next train arrives.
