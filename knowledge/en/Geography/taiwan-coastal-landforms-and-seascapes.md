> **30-Second Overview:** From the summit of Yushan (3,952 meters) to the Ryukyu Trench in the eastern offshore waters (-7,507 meters), the vertical drop exceeds 11,000 meters, within a horizontal distance of less than 150 kilometers. This figure makes Taiwan one of the islands with the steepest topographic gradients in the world—even more "compressed" than jumping from the summit of Mount Everest into the Mariana Trench. And all of this took only 5 million years.

## A Slow-Motion Collision: 5 Million Years, and Still Not Finished

In April 2023, Yehliu Geopark released a heart-wrenching figure: the neck of the Queen's Head has only 120 centimeters remaining.

Fifteen years ago, her neck circumference was 143 centimeters. Losing 1–2 centimeters every year, at this rate, this 15-million-year-old geological queen may only be able to stand for about five more years. A team from National Taipei University of Technology is developing "nature-mimicking sandstone reinforcement technology" in an attempt to extend her life, but all geologists know the truth: you cannot stop the breathing of an island.

📝 **Curator's Perspective:** The most fascinating thing about Taiwan's coast is not its beauty, but its "aliveness." This is not a landscape postcard; it is a documentary being filmed in real-time. With 15,000–18,000 earthquakes annually, an annual crustal uplift of 5–7 millimeters, and 1–2 centimeters of weathering and erosion per year: this island is simultaneously growing taller and being worn down.

The story of the Queen's Head is actually the story of the entire coastline: **Taiwan's coast is simultaneously being born and disappearing.**

## An 11,459-Meter Vertical Drop: The World's Most Compressed Geological Theater

Let us understand Taiwan's coast from a different perspective.

Yushan, at 3,952 meters, is the highest peak in Northeast Asia. Less than 150 kilometers east of it, on the seabed, the Ryukyu Trench reaches depths of 7,507 meters. From the summit to the seafloor, the vertical drop is 11,459 meters.

What does this mean? If you placed Mount Everest (8,849 meters) into this drop, it would only fill 77% of the space. Furthermore, while the base width of Mount Everest exceeds 400 kilometers, Taiwan packs a much larger vertical space into less than half that horizontal distance.

Five million years ago, the Philippine Sea Plate began colliding with the Eurasian Plate at a rate of 7–8 centimeters per year. This collision has not stopped to this day. Taiwan is not merely a remnant "created" by plate collisions; it **is** the active site of the collision right now. The marble of the Qingshui Cliff formed 250 million years ago, originally lying quietly in the deep sea. 5 million years of compression pushed it up nearly a thousand meters, before a fault sliced through it, creating a vertical precipice with an 8_00-meter drop.

Standing on the Suhua Highway looking down, every white rock beneath your feet was once the remains of marine life.

## Four Coasts, Four Personalities of One Island

Because the angle and force of this collision are uneven, the four coasts of Taiwan have developed distinctly different "personalities."

### East Coast: Violent

The east is the frontal zone of the collision: mountains rise directly from the seabed. The 21-kilometer continuous cliff of the Qingshui Cliff is a rare global example of a "sea cliff" landscape. There are no sandy beaches here, no buffers—only rock and a head-on confrontation with the Pacific Ocean.

Shitiping in Hualien resembles an unearthed time capsule: terraced coral reefs where each "step" marks a different sea-level era. The highest coral reef dates back 120,000 years, recording the peak sea level of the last interglacial period.

### West Coast: Gentle, but Disappearing

The western coast is the backside of the collision: the Zhuoshui River carries 40 million tons of sediment into the sea annually, filling in the plains of western-central Taiwan over millions of years. Here, there are lagoons, wetlands, and Taiwan's largest sandbars.

But this gentle coast is paying a price.

📝 **A Disappearing Island:** Waishanding Islet, Taiwan's largest sandbar, measured 3,205 hectares in 1984. By 2020, only 1,138 hectares remained: a 65% reduction in 36 years. Research estimates it will submerge below sea level by 2028. The cause is not just natural erosion: sand trapping by the Formosa Plastics Group (CPC) refinery's reclamation projects and upstream dams has cut off the "blood supply" of sediment to Waishanding. It once served as a barrier for oyster farmers along the Yunlin-Changhua coast, with hundreds of people residing there in the 1960s. Today, only a lighthouse remains on the islet.

The Yunlin coast retreats by 10–15 meters every year. This is not a distant prophecy of climate change; it is happening now.

### North Coast: A Geological Classroom

The northern coast is a masterpiece of differential erosion. Hard and soft rock layers alternate in exposure, as waves selectively hollow out the soft rock and leave the hard rock behind, carving the incredible shapes of Yehliu.

The neck of the Queen's Head thins by 1–2 centimeters annually. The Elephant Trunk Rock fractured in December 2023. The disappearance of these landmarks is not an accident, but a geological inevitability: sea erosion never pauses for tourists.

### South Coast: A Tropical Paradise in Countdown

Kenting is Taiwan's only tropical coral reef coast. Walking along Kenting Main Street, the white rocks beneath your feet are the skeletal remains of corals from 120,000 years ago: after the seas retreated following the last interglacial period, the crust pushed these ancient reefs up by over 200 meters.

The modern coral reefs beneath the surface host 60 species of reef-building corals and over 1,200 species of fish, with a biological density comparable to the Amazon rainforest. However, in the summer of 2020, Taiwan experienced its largest recorded coral bleaching event. A team from Academia Sinica recorded 28,250 coral colonies across 62 sites in Taiwan, reaching a chilling conclusion: Xiao Liuqiu would lose 55% of its coral, while the Northeast Coast and Kenting would lose approximately 30%.

> "The corals of the Northeast Coast, which escaped disaster in 1998, experienced their first large-scale bleaching on record in 2020."
> — Dr. Kuo Chao-yang, Biodiversity Research Center, Academia Sinica

The irony is profound: the warm waters that allow Taiwan to possess coral reefs are now the very thing killing them.

## Two Clocks of One Island

Taiwan's coasts run on two simultaneous clocks.

**The Geological Clock** moves upward: an annual uplift rate of 5–7 millimeters means that in ten thousand years, the Qingshui Cliff will be 50–70 meters higher. New coral reefs are growing beneath the sea; within one square centimeter of coral tissue live 1 to 5 million zooxanthellae cells, working day and night to turn sunlight into calcium carbonate skeletons. A coral colony with a diameter of 3 meters may have grown for 300 years, and it will witness the next 300.

**The Human Clock** moves downward: Waishanding Islet has shrunk by 65% in 36 years. The Queen's Head likely has about five years left. The 2020 coral bleaching took away 30–55% of the corals. Sea levels rise by 3.4 millimeters annually, but due to land subsidence, the relative sea-level rise on the southwest coast reaches as high as 5–7 millimeters per year.

📝 **Curator's Perspective:** The story of Taiwan's coast is not as simple as "humans destroying nature." This island itself is a continuous 5-million-year process of "destruction"—plate collisions, faulting, and marine erosion. Human activities (sand trapping by industrial projects, warming from carbon emissions) are merely adding a new variation to the natural geological rhythm. The problem is not destruction itself, but the speed: things that geology completes in millions of years, we are accomplishing in decades.

On the 1,600 hectares of water in the Qigu Lagoon, 2,000–3,000 [black-faced spoonbills](/en/nature/black-faced-spoonbill/) arrive every winter to overwinter, accounting for over 60% of the global population. The fiddler crabs on the western mudflats possess precise biological clocks, beginning to wave their large claws for courtship 30 minutes before the tide recedes—even when moved to a laboratory, they continue to follow the tidal rhythms of their original habitat for weeks.

These creatures do not know that Waishanding Islet is disappearing. They simply live according to their own clocks.

And Taiwan's coastline—still rising slightly with every earthquake, still retreating slightly with every wave. Simultaneously being born, simultaneously disappearing. Just like everything that lives.
