> **30-second overview:** Between 1885 and 1890, Taipei already saw attempts at public water supply involving well-drilling, filtration, and disinfection. The 1907 Xindian Creek headworks brought modern tap water into the city. Post-war island-wide supply coverage rose from roughly 10% to 93.1% in 2013, yet water never became something that "just naturally appears." Today's faucet is still connected to intake points, treatment plants, tens of thousands of kilometers of pipelines, inter-regional dispatch, and the trade-offs made during every drought.

## Before You Turn the Faucet

In 2024, the Feitsui raw-water pipeline began conveying water to Taipei. Over a century earlier, the city was still figuring out how to let people drink cleaner water. The surviving Caoshan Waterway system and the Museum of Drinking Water let this infrastructure history exist not only in chronologies but in architecture and pipe fittings you can walk up to. The Taipei Water Department's institutional history records that the Museum of Drinking Water officially opened in 2000, and in 2021 the "Secrets of Good Water" events brought the Caoshan Waterway heritage back into urban life.[^1]

The Taipei Water Department designates the well-drilling, filtration, and disinfection work inside the city walls during the Liu Ming-chuan period (1885–1890) as the origin of Taipei's public water supply. In 1907, construction began on the Xindian Creek headworks, giving the modern water utility a skeleton capable of sustained expansion.[^1]

This starting point is well suited to dismantle a habitual imagination: tap water is not a pipe slapped next to a water source, but the transformation of water into shared urban infrastructure. Water must be withdrawn, treated, measured, stored, pressurized, and delivered into every building. If any segment fails, the faucet remains nothing but a metal handle.

## How an Island Gets Water to Every Home

In the early post-war period, Taiwan's tap water supply coverage was about 10%. The Water Resources Agency's historical compilation shows coverage reaching 78% in 1980, 90% in 1990, and 93.1% in 2013. This was not the achievement of a single plant, but the cumulative result of years of expansion, consolidation, and pipeline extension.[^2]

After the Nationalist government relocated to Taiwan in 1949, the island's water supply system faced war damage first, not a ready-made modern water network. Water Resources Agency records show that, with U.S. aid funding, the government began repairing 77 rural water systems at the end of 1949 (Minguo 38), completing them by May 1950 (Minguo 39), first restoring output and served population to pre-war levels.[^10]

This history contains a turning point easily obscured by daily life. Today's faucet looks like a Doraemon gadget — plugged into the wall, turn it and water flows. In reality, post-1949 supply work began with repair, then proceeded through urban facility renovation, rural water subsidies, and the long-term construction of the Engineering Corps and the Public Works Bureau, gradually evolving into a regional supply network. The 77 rural systems repaired in 1949 are estimated to have benefited 190,000 people. The Provincial Government established the Engineering Corps in 1952, expanded it into the Public Works Bureau in 1958, giving water supply an organization capable of sustained advancement through annual plans and public budgets.[^11]

This does not mean restarting Taiwan's water history from 1949. The Japanese colonial period had already left waterworks and treatment facilities; post-war government actions included repair, expansion, integration, and redistribution. Treating the faucet as a Doraemon gadget — the interesting part is not mocking that imagination, but seeing how many engineering decisions connect the invisible pipe inside the wall.

📝 Curator's note: After the KMT came to Taiwan, water didn't suddenly appear from inside the wall. First someone repaired the 77 systems the war broke; only later came the faucet you thought "should have been there all along."

In 1963, a WHO expert team visited Taiwan to study water supply construction and development plans. In 1974, the Taiwan Provincial Government converted scattered local supply systems into large-scale and regional systems, merging 128 plants island-wide to establish the Taiwan Water Corporation. Local plants were placed into a larger network; supply changed from a single town's affair into cross-regional governance.[^2]

This consolidation also changed the meaning of "water shortage." Shortage is not only the sky failing to rain; it can also be a region's supply and demand losing balance. Hydraulic engineering can store wet-season water for the dry season; pipelines let different systems back each other up. Water experts note that Taiwan's various water uses do not all come from reservoirs — river diversion and groundwater also sit in the supply structure.[^7]

## The Plant Is Not the Destination; the Pipeline Is Daily Life

The Taiwan Water Corporation's statistical yearbook breaks supply into a fully traceable system: water sources and output, served population and coverage, water sales volume, treatment and storage facilities, pipeline length, and capacity analysis of various pipe categories. The yearbook's table of contents looks administrative, yet reveals tap water's truest face — it is not a product, but a chain of public assets requiring maintenance.[^3]

Taiwan Water currently maintains island-wide supply through 141 supply systems and manages 486 treatment plants of various types. In its sustainability data, the company acknowledges that some plants have operated for many years with gradually aging equipment, and therefore plans to modernize 21 plants. As of end-2024, 6 have been completed.[^4]

Water reaching the treatment plant has still not arrived home. Raw-water turbidity can spike dramatically in short periods due to typhoons and heavy rainfall, increasing treatment difficulty and cost. Treated water must still pass through transmission mains, distribution reservoirs, booster stations, and alleyway pipelines. This is why water safety cannot be judged only at the plant outlet — the pipe network, community water towers, and household endpoints are equally the final segment.

## When Drought Comes, Where Does the Water Come From

The 2021 water shortage made "how much water is left in the reservoir" a nationally familiar image, but experts remind us that reservoir storage alone cannot equal whether a region will face shortage. Rainfall, river flow, inflow volume, available supply days, and dispatch among different water-use targets all affect water-condition assessment.[^7]

During the sustained 2023 southern Taiwan drought, PTS's "Our Island" recorded Zengwen Reservoir storage dropping to about 11.3% and Nanhua Reservoir to 38.45%. Kaohsiung sought alternatives through pressure reduction, reclaimed water, subflow water, and drought-relief wells. These measures did not "conjure" water, but reallocated water originally outside the municipal tap-water system through engineering and management.[^6]

Reclaimed water especially illustrates this shift. Research notes that Taiwan faced an average annual water demand shortfall of about 530.6 million cubic meters from 2011 to 2019; the government therefore treats reclaimed water as an important new industrial source, setting a 2031 target of 1.32 million cubic meters per day. The Fengshan Reclaimed Water Plant already supplies treated water to steel processes; the Yongkang Plant serves the semiconductor and panel industries.[^5]

Here lies a less intuitive result: during shortage, what gets redesigned first is not necessarily the household faucet, but the source of industrial water. The city must sustain both life and industry. Supply policy therefore asks not only "where is there water" but "which kind of water should go where." For households this may mean pressure and outage duration; for industry, whether reclaimed water can stably meet process needs. Two demands share one island's hydrological conditions; supply engineering is therefore simultaneously industrial policy and livelihood policy.

## Where Water Comes From Also Decides Who Must Protect It

The supply upstream is not an abstract "reservoir catchment," but a watershed with farmland, factories, settlements, and roads. A 2026 Reporter investigation overlaid 245 surface-water intakes and protection zones with pollution penalty data, finding nearly one-quarter of intakes lacked designated tap-water quality-and-quantity protection zones upstream. Over the past five years, at least 381 factories upstream of intakes had been fined for relevant environmental regulations.[^8]

This does not mean every fined factory has already polluted drinking water, nor that treatment plants lack capacity. It points to a harder problem: if raw water is polluted, the city must invest more in monitoring, patrol, chemicals, and treatment equipment. If upstream residents face land-use restrictions due to protection zones, who bears the conservation cost?

The Reporter's investigation also notes that tap-water quality-and-quantity protection zones and drinking-water source quality protection zones are managed under different systems, with differing control scope and intensity. When a river flows from upstream to an intake, administrative boundaries and regulatory divisions do not disappear with the water; governance therefore needs clearer responsibility and compensation design.[^8]

## The Water That Leaks, and the Invisible Cost

PTS reporting citing Taiwan Water data notes that Taiwan's 2022 tap-water leakage rate was 13.10%, with about 420 million tons lost before reaching users — equivalent to the storage of two Shimen Reservoirs. The report also notes that reducing leakage below 10% would require very substantial public investment in pipe replacement.[^6]

Leakage is not simply "the water company being careless." Old pipelines run under roads, bridges, dense residential areas, and other underground utilities; replacement encounters traffic, funding, construction space, and outage coordination. The less visible the pipeline, the less daily political attention it receives. Yet every burst pipe, pressure reduction, and outage suddenly returns it to the center of life.

Taiwan Water lists leakage reduction, backup pipeline construction, strengthened cross-system dispatch, and plant modernization as supply-management priorities. The company also states a 99.94% water-quality compliance rate and 100% health-affecting substance compliance. These figures explain plant-outlet management results, but cannot vouch for household water towers, pipes, and filters. Between the public system and the private end lies a segment users must maintain themselves.[^4]

## After the Earthquake, How Does Water Return

Supply resilience is tested not only in drought. Taiwan Water's English-language official materials record that the 2024 Hualien earthquake damaged multiple supply facilities, affecting over 120,000 households. Taiwan Water activated its emergency response center and repair plan, restoring island-wide supply within 86 hours. This number will quickly vanish from the news, but for households without water, 86 hours is a period of rearranging drinking, washing, caregiving, and work.[^9]

Therefore backup pipelines, disaster-relief groundwater wells, emergency survival water stations, and cross-regional dispatch are not standby lists unrelated to daily supply. Their value appears only when built and tested in normal times, so they function when disaster strikes. The water system's reliability rests on putting worst-case scenarios into routine budgets and maintenance plans.

📝 Curator's note: True resilience is not being good at rescue after disaster, but someone willing to pay for invisible backup before disaster.

## The Public Nature of a Single Drop

Taiwan Water's 50th-anniversary English message describes supply coverage rising from under 50% to over 95%, listing pipeline extension, cross-regional dispatch, plant modernization, and leakage improvement as next-phase work. This English page unexpectedly states tap water clearly: it provides not only water, but a society's way of connecting public welfare and economic development to every household.[^9]

But public nature does not mean forever free, nor identical service everywhere. Water experts have argued that basic domestic water should consider equity, while industrial water should design conservation incentives. Dry-season dispatch should also incorporate ecological environment and third-party losses into rules. Water pricing, protection-zone compensation, industrial water, agricultural water, and household demand all ultimately meet in the same supply system.[^7]

So what is truly worth remembering when you turn the faucet is not "Taiwan finally has water," but that this action still requires a society's sustained contribution. The 1885 wells, the 1907 Xindian Creek headworks, the 1974 island-wide integrated network, the 2021 drought dispatch, and today's reclaimed water and pipeline renewal are all the same thing across eras: turning unstable nature into a system for shared life.

Next time water flows from the faucet, you need not think of it as a miracle. Think of it as a public contract re-signed daily: the upstream willing to protect, engineers willing to maintain, government willing to disclose, industry willing to conserve, and water users willing to know where this drop comes from — and on whose land it may have exacted a price.
