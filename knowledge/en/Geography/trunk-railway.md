> **30-Second Summary:** On April 20, 1908, the trans-island railway from Keelung to Tago (Kaohsiung) was completed; on October 24 of that year, the Governor-General of Taiwan held a completion ceremony in Taichung Park. This line not only delivered rice, sugar, and timber faster to the ports but also led to agricultural income growth in some cities connected by rail. It is not merely a story of modernization, but a line that simultaneously amplified imperial control, local opportunity, and social disparity.

![Building from the 1908 Taiwan Trans-Island Railway Completion Ceremony](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fc/Ceremony_building_of_opening_of_the_J%C5%ABkan_Railway_Line_in_Taiwan.png)
_Figure: Building from the 1908 Taiwan Trans-Island Railway Completion Ceremony; provided by Wikimedia Commons, in the public domain._

In 1887, Liu Ming-jen established the General Bureau of Railways for Taiwan at Taodangcheng (Taipei). On April 20, 1908, the trans-island line from Keelung to Tago (Kaohsiung) was fully completed. The completion ceremony was held by the Governor-General of Taiwan in Taichung Park on October 24. This railway appears as a feat of transportation engineering, but it is actually three things at once: connecting the island's north and south, transporting agricultural products to ports, and inscribing the pace of colonial government control over land and population into the landscape.[^1] [^2]

The most counterintuitive aspect is this: the trans-island railway was originally designed for imperial export, yet it also made it easier for some Taiwanese farmers to access markets, even increasing agricultural income. It is not a single narrative of "colonization bringing modernization," nor is it a single story of "railways only serving plunder." It is a line that expanded both control and opportunity simultaneously.

## What Liu Ming-jen Left Was Not a Completed Trans-Island Line

Liu Ming-jen is often placed on the first page of Taiwan's railway history, but what he left was an unfinished starting point. During the Qing Dynasty, construction began in 1887 for the rail line from Taipei to Keelung, completing about 28 kilometers of the Taipei–Keelung line in 1891. Plans to extend toward Taoyuan and Hsinchu stalled after Liu Ming-jen resigned.[^3]

The difficulties of this early railway were not just financial. The route had too steep a gradient, relied on foreign engineers for technology, and operators also had to deal with the fact that the existing line was unsuitable for long-term operation. After Japan took over Taiwan in 1895, they initially treated the railway as military transport before switching it to general transport managed by civil affairs agencies.[^2] [^4]

Therefore, "Taiwan's first railway" and "Taiwan's trans-island railway" are not the same thing. The former is a short segment from Liu Ming-jen's era; the latter is the north-south trunk line redesigned and constructed in sections by the Japanese colonial government after 1899 using national finances.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: When viewing Liu Ming-jen's short line alongside the 1908 trans-island line, one realizes that "the first railway" and "establishing a complete rail system" are actually two different things; the former is a starting point, while the latter was a test of whether the nation could maintain, operate, and distribute speed over the long term.

## From Private Company to State Project

In the early period of Japanese rule, the trans-island railway was not initially built solely by the government. In 1896, the Japanese side planned to establish the Taiwan Railway Company (臺灣鐵道會社) to renovate existing railways and build a line from Hsinchu to Tago, even preparing to offer land and interest subsidies. However, the company ultimately failed to be formally established; private capital could not handle the scale of the project.[^4]

Kodo Shinpei, who arrived in Taiwan in 1898, determined that the railway needed to coordinate with the port construction in Keelung to generate transportation revenue. This initial investment was something a private company could not bear alone. The Japanese Diet subsequently passed the "Taiwan Enterprise Public Bond Law," of which 28.8 million yen out of a total of 35 million yen was allocated to the trans-island railway.[^4]

This is an important turning point: the trans-island railway did not emerge naturally from the market; it was a project driven by the colonial state using public bonds, bureaucracy, and military and economic objectives. In 1899, the Temporary Taiwan Railway Construction Department (臨時臺灣鐵道敷設部) was established. In November of that year, the Railway Department of the Governor-General of Taiwan was formed, integrating construction, operation, maintenance, and private railways into a single management system.[^4]

The organizational design of the Railway Department also reveals the nature of this project. It was not just responsible for laying tracks; it also handled train transport, line maintenance, and private railways used by sugar, forestry, and mining industries. Thus, the trans-island line was not an isolated passenger line but a transportation network connected to ports, production areas, factories, and branch lines.

## "Accelerated Extension" and the Mountain Cost

In May 1899, Hasegawa Kinjai led the project forward from both north and south. The route adopted a strategy of opening sections as soon as they were completed, allowing the railway to generate operating income while under construction. Official records organized this policy as "Accelerated Extensionism."[^4]

But speed was not free. The mountain line between Sanyi and Fengyuan had to pass through the hilly and fault-ridden terrain of Miaoli and Taichung. Sections that later became part of the Old Mountain Line included bridges and tunnels. The National Cultural Heritage website records that the Central Taiwan Earthquake in 1935 severely damaged the bridges and tunnels from Shixifen to Taichung, causing the railway to halt for maintenance for three years until it resumed operation in 1938.[^5]

One tunnel took five years and over 4.2 million yen, accounting for about one-sixth of the entire trans-island project budget. This figure pulls "modernization" from an abstract noun back down to the rock face: every kilometer required materials, labor, and risk, with the pace of construction determined by imperial finance and transportation needs.[^6]

On April 20, 1908, the trans-island railway was fully completed. On October 24, the official completion ceremony was held in Taichung Park. This ceremony was not merely a celebration of transport; it was a public ritual by the colonial government to demonstrate that it "could govern the entire island." Officials invited royalty, civil servants, and various sectors to attend, packaging the railway's completion as proof of Taiwan entering a new era.[^2] [^4]

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The official ceremony leaves behind the image of completion, but what is left in the mountains is the time spent on construction, earthquakes, and repair; if one only looks at the day of completion, the longest wait in the project is cut out.

## How a Line Changed Distance

Before the trans-island railway, movement across Taiwan's north and south was constrained by roads, rivers, and seasons. After the railway was completed, it took about 14 hours to travel from north to south. This speed redefined "inter-island travel" at the time, making the train the primary tool for inter-regional transit.[^7]

Transportation did not just change for passengers. The railway moved rice, sugar, timber, and minerals from inland areas to ports, connecting them to the shipping network of the Japanese Empire. English research analyzing statistics from the Governor-General of Taiwan indicates that the location and configuration of trans-island stations primarily served the export demand of agricultural products moving to ports.[^6]

This design also explains why the railway quickly connected with sugar, forestry, and mining branch lines. National archives maps from the 1930s show not only state trunk lines but also private commercial lines, corporate dedicated lines, and light railways powered by human or animal labor. The density of the rail network reflects not just tourism activity, but how industries were linked to transportation nodes.[^8]

### Three Time Points: Seeing How Rail Became a Network

| Year  | Event                                                                   | What This Number Shows                                                                          |
| :---- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1908  | Trans-island line fully completed; North-South journey approx. 14 hours | The north-south distance was compressed into a manageable schedule for the first time[^2] [^7]. |
| 1922  | Zhunan to Wangtian coastal line fully opened                            | The pattern of west mountain and sea trunk lines took shape[^2].                                |
| 1930s | Wanshin Line carried over 3.64 million passengers in one year           | Private branch lines could also become daily urban transport[^2].                               |

What is easiest to overlook in this table is the year 1922. The trans-island line did not stop growing after its completion in 1908. The coastal lines provided alternative paths to the mountain lines, transforming the railway from a single north-south main trunk into a network that could be diversified, connect production areas, and link to ports.[^2]

It is also worth noting that the railways of the 1930s did not only serve long-distance travelers. The Wanshin Line, running from Wanhua to Xindian, despite being only 10.4 kilometers, carried over 3.64 million passengers in 1930. This number brought "railway" back from a state project to a mode of transport used daily by urban residents.[^2]

![Map of Taiwan Railways in 1950](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c3/1950_map_of_Taiwan_railway.jpg)
_Figure: Map published by the Taiwan Railway Administration in 1950; provided by Wikimedia Commons, in the public domain._

This 1950 map lays out another turning point on paper: the trans-island line was no longer just a product of colonial government efforts but was reorganized by the post-war Taiwan Railway Administration into a spatial imagination of public transport. The map did not erase war damage, nor did it explain how much benefit each place received. It merely drew "which places were connected."[^12]

However, export orientation does not mean that local areas had no benefits. Economic history research from the LSE compared statistics from 1905 to 1918 between cities connected by rail and those that were not, finding that irrigated land income in cities connected by rail increased by an average of about 60,000 yen. The railway reduced transportation costs and expanded markets, also giving some farmers more incentive to invest in irrigation and crop improvement.[^6]

The research also reminds us that benefits were not equal. Cities with increasing population density might see a decrease in irrigated land income. Land competition and urbanization redistributed the benefits brought by the railway. Thus, the trans-island line was not a line where "everyone benefited," but one that amplified differences even faster.[^6]

## The Mountain Line Was Not Background, But the Railway Itself

During the earthquake in the Zhinkuo–Taichung area in 1935, the trans-island mountain line's tracks twisted, tunnel walls cracked, and stations and bridges collapsed, halting trains for three years. This disaster showed that the railway is not a straight line drawn on a map, but an engineering work that ages along with earthquakes, gradients, and rock strata.[^5]

The ruins left by Sheng Hsing Station and the Yutengping (Longteng) Bridge show how the trans-island line traversed mountainous regions, and how natural disasters rewrote a main trunk line. They are not relics detached from transport history, but tangible traces of railway engineering.[^5]

These remains also changed the use of the railway. Sections originally used to transport coal, timber, and fruit became historical display and tourist routes after being shut down. The same tunnels and bridges transformed from transportation infrastructure into containers of local memory.[^5]

The difficulty in preserving rail heritage is precisely about showing how engineering worked, rather than just turning it into nostalgic scenery. Preserving tracks, piers, and stations is also preserving a technical language that once governed movement.[^5] This is why the trans-island line is still worth re-reading.

It left not a single answer, but a path that can be repeatedly questioned.

## Railways Also Created Lifestyles Beyond Stations

After the completion of the trans-island line, the Railway Department began operating more than just trains. It introduced discount tickets, combined operations with seaside resorts and travel activities, and connected trains to hotels, restaurants, buses, and tours. In the 1930s, the railway began selling the idea of a "circular tour," even though a truly complete circular line did not exist at that time.[^8]

A case from the national archives in 1933 illustrates this well: students from Taichung Normal School first took the train to Yilan, then traveled through Suao, Taroko, Hualien, Taitung, and down south by car, walking, and rail to complete a circular trip. It wasn't until the Nan-Hui Railway was completed in 1991 that people could truly tour the island entirely by rail.[^8]

The railway also brought class differences. Exhibition materials from the National Taiwan Museum of History indicate that train tickets during the Japanese era were not cheap for most Taiwanese; first-class passengers were mainly foreigners and the upper classes of the Taiwanese-Japanese community. Ordinary people more often traveled for festivals, seaside resorts, or short trips.[^7]

Thus, while the train shortened the island's distance, it also made "who could move, and how they moved" a new social difference. Modern transportation is never just about speed; it also includes fares, stations, and who was placed alongside the route.

## War, Repair, and the Second Life of a Line

At the end of World War II, Taiwan's railways were bombed, damaging bridges, culverts, and tracks. After Japan left Taiwan, the railway system operated in a barely functional state for a time. Only after 1949 did new personnel, materials, and American aid equipment gradually arrive, allowing the railway to re-establish itself as a major mode of transport in post-war Taiwan.[^3] [^9]

The Taipei Locomotive Works offers another perspective. It maintained steam locomotives during the Japanese era, switched to diesel-electric locomotives in the 1960s, and repaired electric locomotives after electrification in 1979. The Preparatory Office of the National Railway Museum views the repair history of this works as a microcosm of Taiwan's railway development.[^9]

This also means that the "trans-island railway" should not stop at 1908. The life of the railway is not in the completion ceremony, but in every change of track, bridge repair, shift to electric power, relocation of the works, and the daily routine of passengers using it as a journey home after the war.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The same line can transport agricultural products to ports, or carry migrant workers back home; when the purpose of transport changes, the tracks retain the memory of who they served and what they prioritized.

## Two Modernities Left on the Tracks

What is most worth remembering about the trans-island railway is not the oversimplified conclusion that "Japan built Taiwan," but rather asking: Who decided the route? Who paid the cost? Who gained speed? Who was left outside the station?

There is no single answer. The colonial government used the railway to improve administrative, military, and export efficiency. The project integrated land, industry, and population into tighter control. The same network also reduced transportation costs for some localities, reconnecting agricultural production with urban markets. These results conflict yet coexist.[^6] [^8]

Today, when riding the western trunk line, the cities outside the window seem to have pushed away the colonial era. However, the tunnels of the Old Mountain Line, the lake pavilion in Taichung Park, the railway department ruins outside Beimen, and the operating stations all remind us: modernization is not an endpoint, but a series of choices laid upon the old tracks.

In 1908, the official celebration marked the connection with a sense of "seamlessness." For later generations, that line certainly made the island easier to reach. But it also left another question: when one line connects all places to the same market, does a locality gain freedom, or only gains the speed of being transported away?
