> **30-Second Overview:** Today the Wenhu Line is the familiar Brown Line to Taipei residents, but its predecessor, the Muzha Line, endured fires, tire blowouts, pier cap cracks, and a crisis of trust before opening. This article traces the story from 1996, Taiwan's first metro line, tracking how a foreign system was re-understood and maintained by a Taiwan team after the original manufacturer withdrew — ultimately becoming the infrastructure that carries people across the city every day. The focus is not the glorious label of "first metro," but how a public system passed on the knowledge born of failure to the next train.

In 1993, two fires broke out in succession during testing of the Taipei Metro Muzha Line. In 1995, trains suffered two tire blowouts after striking foreign objects. Only on March 28, 1996, did this 10.9-kilometer line with 12 stations officially become Taiwan's first commercially operated metro.[^1] [^2] [^3]

At the time, Taipei residents were not waiting for a shiny new transit vehicle, but for an answer they finally dared to trust. The tone in newspapers and magazines was direct: the Muzha Line's opening date was delayed again and again, and the public's question shifted from "When will it open?" to "Even if it opens, would you dare ride it?"[^4]

This is what the Wenhu Line most deserves to be remembered for. It was not a modern metro flawless from planning to operation, but a public work that survived only by relearning after integration failures and trust collapse.

## The Wenhu Line Did Not Begin as a Single Line

Today the Wenhu Line comprises the Wenshan segment (the former Muzha Line) and the later Neihu segment, integrated into one. This article returns first to the Muzha Line, the earliest to open, because the Wenhu Line's reliability and urban significance both begin at that unstable starting point. According to Taipei City Department of Rapid Transit Systems route data, the original Muzha Line departed from Taipei Zoo Station, passed through Wanfang Community and the Fuzhou Mountain area, entered tunnel, then connected via Heping East Road and Fuxing North/South Roads, terminating at Zhongshan Junior High School Station. The original line was 10.9 kilometers long with 12 stations and one maintenance depot.[^1]

It adopted a medium-capacity, fully automated rubber-tire system. This choice had urban reasoning: Taipei needed a line to serve the Muzha and Wenshan areas without the scale of a traditional heavy-capacity metro. Yet smaller cars and more complex automatic control did not simplify integration.[^1] [^5] [^11]

The Wenhu Line's full-line name resulted from the junction of the Muzha and Neihu Lines. Metro data indicates the Neihu Line departs from the tail track of Zhongshan Junior High School Station, runs along Fuxing North Road, past Songshan Airport, Dazhi, and Neihu, finally entering the Nangang Software Park area. This segment is approximately 14.8 kilometers with 12 stations and the Neihu Depot. The Neihu and Muzha Depots together handle stabling, inspection, maintenance, repair, and testing for Wenhu Line trains.[^11]

The Neihu Line was not an immediate extension after the Muzha Line opened. Metro historical records show that after tire fires during Muzha Line testing, the Taipei City Council demanded a re-evaluation of the Neihu Line's system choice. After roughly eight years of analysis and voting, the decision remained an elevated medium-capacity system. This delay illustrates that the Wenhu Line's shape is not merely an engineer's drawing, but a polyline pulled taut by accidents, the council, and urban needs.[^11]

A metro is not just placing trains on tracks. Civil works, signaling, power supply, rolling stock, braking, and the Operations Control Center must all understand each other at the same moment. The Wenhu Line's story repeatedly returns to this seemingly engineer-centric problem: every subsystem can run alone, but that does not mean they will run normally when connected.

## The Fires Were Not a Single Broken Part

Issue 50 of _Metro Technology_, published by the Taipei Rapid Transit Corporation, preserves a more detailed explanation than news headlines. The Muzha Line's VAL 256 system was originally designed for single-car or married-pair (two-car) operation. Due to capacity needs, Taipei adopted a four-car consist — two married pairs — for the first time.[^5]

The problem lay in unsynchronized propulsion and braking signals between the two married pairs. When one pair's braking action fell out of sync with the other, brake calipers could clamp onto brake pads, generating heat that burned the rubber tires. This explanation does not blame a single bolt; it points to integration failure among consist configuration, control logic, and braking mechanism.[^5]

After the first fire, the true cause was not immediately eliminated. A second fire within half a year inflicted not mere inconvenience on citizens, but the question: "Does anyone actually understand this system?"[^5]

Such doubt leaves traces in a city. _Taiwan Panorama_ wrote in retrospect that pier cap cracks, train fires, derailments, and blowouts followed one another — the Muzha Line's testing mishaps nearly formed a chronicle of their own.[^4] A metro not yet in revenue service had first learned how to make headlines.

## Pier Cap Cracks and Engineering Interfaces

Beyond rolling stock accidents, the Muzha Line had civil works issues. _Metro Technology_ records that pier cap cracks related to design and construction conditions. To preserve aesthetics, beam and column dimensions were reduced, causing congested rebar that prevented even concrete consolidation; later, expert review mandated steel plate jacketing for reinforcement.[^5]

This appears as a separate story from the fires, yet both point to the same core: the hardest part of large public works is not finding a nominally cutting-edge system, but making different disciplines share a single responsibility on site.

Retrospectives on pre-opening pier reinforcement also pull this responsibility back to structural safety. Engineer Chen Chen-chuan (陳振川) treated pier cap cracks, appraisal, and reinforcement as engineering challenges that had to be faced before opening. This is a columnist's engineering perspective, not a Control Yuan investigation conclusion, but it reminds readers that safety never resides only in the train controller.[^8]

Civil delays also pushed the problem into contracts. _Metro Technology_ records that delayed handover of Muzha Line civil facilities caused Matra to mobilize late and claim cost losses. Subsequent arbitration cited over-fragmented contract packages, complex interfaces, integration difficulty, and contract execution difficulties as key background.[^5]

The Control Yuan's 2010 deliberation and 2016 announced corrective case then reviewed the Wenhu Line electromechanical system in more institutional language. The case determined that the electromechanical system was the Wenhu Line's core, yet the Metro Bureau listed it as a subcontract, failed to vet vendor qualifications before award, and did not require a technical service proposal. The Control Yuan also found post-award performance and inspection insufficiently rigorous, causing frequent accidents after revenue service began.[^6]

The time gap matters. The Control Yuan's corrective case is not 1990s breaking news, but a formal institutional review years later. Placing both together reveals the Muzha Line's two memories: contemporaries saw accidents and delays; later investigations asked why procurement, interfaces, and performance allowed accidents to recur.

## Before Opening, Trust Reached the Terminus First

On February 27, 1996, the Muzha Line began free trial rides. _Taiwan Panorama_ records over 1.8 million passenger-trips accumulated during the four-week trial, with only one or two minor glitches and no major incidents.[^4]

This free trial was not merely a promotional event; it resembled a stress test with citizen participation. The engineering team needed to observe how trains, stations, and crowds operated together; citizens used their own bodies to decide whether to entrust their daily commute to it.

After official revenue service began on March 28, the Muzha Line became the Taipei Metro's first commercial line. It linked Taipei Zoo, Muzha, Wanfang, and the city center, and for the first time turned the metro from a construction site into urban daily life.[^1]

The opening thus had two speeds. One was the train advancing on elevated track; the other was citizens slowly changing "I wouldn't dare ride" into "I can ride tomorrow." Public trust does not auto-generate from a ribbon-cutting; it must accumulate through every on-time arrival.

On July 4, 2009, the Neihu Line officially joined operations, and the Muzha and Neihu Lines were collectively named the Wenshan-Neihu Line, later designated BR Wenhu Line under the route numbering system. This date was not merely an extension ribbon-cutting; it let the first metro serving the Wenshan area pierce through the airport, Dazhi, and Neihu, becoming a continuous line spanning Taipei's eastern living sphere.[^1] [^11]

## After Matra Withdrew, Who Would Maintain This System

Two months after opening, a new crisis emerged. _United Daily News_ Time Machine retrospective reports that Matra withdrew without warning over contract disputes and barred subcontractors from selling parts to the Taipei Rapid Transit Corporation, causing a major system crash. The report also records then-Taipei Mayor Chen Shui-bian declaring: "If Matra won't pull, we'll pull ourselves."[^2]

This line endures not just for its rhyme, but because it turned an abstract contract crisis into a political promise citizens could understand: if the OEM stops support, can Taipei keep the trains running on its own?

The Taipei City Government's 2026 retrospective on the Muzha Line's 30-year history notes that when Matra technical personnel withdrew, they took critical maintenance manuals and software passwords. TRTC therefore had to rapidly build operations and maintenance capability without OEM technical support.[^3]

Some distance must be kept here. The 2026 official retrospective is later institutional memory, not a complete original record from 1996. But it aligns with _United Daily News_'s event retrospective and _Metro Technology_'s description of technical learning, showing the post-withdrawal problem was not just a batch of parts, but who held the knowledge.[^2] [^3] [^5]

_Metro Technology_ terms these crises "event-driven project learning," placing fire causes, arbitration, and pier cap reinforcement in the same technical construction narrative. This framing is more useful than "accidents kept happening," because it asks what replicable work method each failure ultimately left behind.[^9]

TRTC later developed replacement components through R&D, enhanced monitoring, improved hardware, and collaborated with domestic manufacturers to develop parts and consumables. Officials describe this experience as breaking the OEM monopoly and laying the foundation for domestic maintenance spares production.[^3]

From an engineering view, this was a forced technology transfer. From a city view, it was the lesson that a public service must not only know how to use, but must be able to understand and maintain.

## The Metro Began Changing More Than Commutes

After the Muzha Line opened, the metro's impact quickly exceeded transport itself. Academic research using Muzha Line station areas as subjects analyzed land use changes in commerce, housing, and public facilities. The research treats stations as nodes of urban spatial redistribution, not mere boarding points.[^10]

_Taiwan Panorama_'s contemporary reporting also captured this shift. Tour groups from central and southern Taiwan arrived at metro stations with flags to "witness" the advanced EMUs; crowds appeared at Muzha Zoo and along-line commercial districts. For first-time riders, the metro was not a daily commute tool, but a new Taipei landscape worth seeing.[^4]

Years later, PTS marked the Muzha Line's 30th anniversary, describing it as a line that gradually built operations and maintenance capability from "the toughest battle." The report also reminded that low fares, electricity costs, personnel costs, and rising operating costs remain TRTC's current challenges.[^7]

This keeps the Muzha Line from settling into a pretty success story. Its early question was whether it could open safely; the later question is whether it can serve long-term at reasonable cost. The former tested engineering integration; the latter tests public service finance and governance.

## The Engineering Ethics the Wenhu Line Leaves Behind

The Wenhu Line is most often reduced to "what became of Taiwan's first metro." This label is not wrong, but too light. It obscures the pre-opening cracks, the testing fires, society's mockery of safety, and the scramble to reclaim knowledge after contract rupture.

If one only remembers March 28, 1996, the metro appears as a finished product. Only by placing the 1993 fires, 1995 blowouts, 1996 trial rides and withdrawal, and later audits and technical reviews on the same timeline does one see it is actually a learning process.[^2] [^4] [^5] [^6]

This is also the Wenhu Line's most practical reminder to engineers today: system reliability is not declared in slide decks, but forged when failures occur — whether the team can find causes, preserve knowledge, fix interfaces, and let the next train depart on schedule.

Thirty years on, the Wenhu Line is no longer the Muzha Line citizens once doubted. Yet the city daily uses the capability it left behind, and still bears the cost of large systems. True success is not never erring, but never hiding errors — so they finally become a capability anyone can take over.
