> **30-Second Overview:**
> Located at the summit of Jade Mountain's North Peak at an altitude of 3,858 meters in Xinyi Township, Nantou County, this station is not only Taiwan's highest-altitude artificial landmark and administrative agency, but also one of the few alpine meteorological observation stations in Northeast Asia. Established in 1943 during the Japanese colonial period, it has withstood over eight decades of severe tests including wartime fire, lightning strikes, blizzards, and extreme weather. Generations of meteorological observers and support staff, operating on a "three-person team, one-month rotation" basis, have guarded this scientific outpost above the clouds amidst extreme isolation and the threat of altitude sickness. When modern automated instruments fail due to ice freezing or lightning strikes, the sentinels here still use their hands and hot water to de-ice the equipment, securing Taiwan's first-hand meteorological pulse.

On October 1, 1943, as the rumbling artillery of the Pacific War still echoed across East Asia, the Japanese colonial government of Taiwan completed the "Shinhotaka Observatory" [^1] [^2] [^5] at the summit of Jade Mountain's North Peak, 3,858 meters above sea level. This wooden structure, isolated on the cliff face, was initially built for military and high-altitude aircraft meteorological intelligence [^5]. After the war, the Republic of China government took over and reorganized it into the Central Meteorological Bureau's Jade Mountain Meteorological Station [^1] [^3]. Eighty years later, the plains below are filled with skyscrapers and ubiquitous digital networks, yet this white dome and meteorological station at the summit of Jade Mountain's North Peak still maintain their most primitive and solitary rhythm: several observers take turns standing guard, day after day measuring wind speed, pressure, and rainfall above the clouds, becoming Taiwan's first line of defense against extreme climate [^8] [^14].

## The Island Above the Clouds and the Test of Survival for Modern Primitives

For most people, Jade Mountain's North Peak is a magnificent spectacle passed by when hikers challenge Taiwan's highest peak; but for the observers and support staff stationed there, it is the highest-altitude, most harshly conditioned office and residence in Taiwan [^7] [^14]. The meteorological station typically maintains a three-person staff—one observer and two support workers. Each stays on the mountain for one month before descending for a month of leave, with three shifts rotating [^14].

At an altitude of 3,858 meters, the air is thin, and pressure is only half that of the plains. Altitude sickness and headaches are the first tests for new personnel. However, more suffocating than hypoxia is loneliness. There are no convenience stores here, no readily available fresh water sources; drinking water relies entirely on collecting rainwater and melting snow from the roof for filtration; bathing and cooking require calculating every drop of water and gas [^13]. Whenever winter blizzards strike, freezing and collapsing the trails, the meteorological station becomes isolated from the world. Observers might as well be playing tug-of-war with death on the cliffs just to commute or go out to check instruments [^10].

In terms of supply logistics, early operations relied entirely on human porters. Monthly shifts and resupply missions were extreme challenges of physical strength and willpower. Porters had to carry dozens of kilograms of rice, gas cylinders, vegetables, and instrument parts, starting from the Tataka trailhead and climbing upward along steep scree slopes and wind-exposed areas. In recent years, although some heavy supplies and emergency parts are airlifted by helicopter, restricted by the恶劣 and variable landing currents on Jade Mountain's North Peak, helicopters often cannot land smoothly. A significant proportion of daily consumables still must be maintained by human effort [^1].

## The Scientific Frontline Battling Lightning: Manual Watch in the Snow and Wind

Jade Mountain's North Peak is not only high but also Taiwan's famous "lightning zone." Due to the protruding peak and special terrain, summer afternoon thunderstorms and convection are vigorous, often making the meteorological station a target for lightning strikes [^11].

![Scenery around Jade Mountain's North Peak ridge and the alpine meteorological station](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/16/%E7%8E%89%E5%B1%B1%E4%B8%BB%E5%B3%B0_07.jpg)
_Surrounding scenery of Jade Mountain's North Peak. Photo: Dragons70c / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0 [^4]._

Senior Observer Technician Hsieh Hsin-tien recalled in an interview that during his decades of service at the station, he experienced countless thrilling thunderstorms [^10] [^11]. The most severe incident occurred in April 2003, when a powerful lightning strike hit the meteorological station directly. The massive boom instantly shattered indoor equipment, causing a total electrical paralysis, leaving blackened marks on the walls [^11]. However, after the lightning strike, the observer's first instinct was not to retreat, but to immediately check the instruments, manually record data, and attempt to transmit real-time meteorological data back to the Taipei headquarters via backup communication. To them, the interruption of meteorological data could mean that countless people below would not be able to prevent disasters in time. This sense of mission far outweighed the fear for their own safety.

Besides lightning, winter ice and snow are another protracted battle. When temperatures drop below freezing, strong cold air masses carrying moisture will wrap the anemometers, rain gauges, and antennas outside the meteorological station in thick rime ice and freezing layers. At this time, observers must wear heavy cold-proof clothing and risk falling off the cliff at any moment, carrying kettles of hot water and tools outdoors to chip away and melt the ice layer on the instruments bit by bit. This alpine life, jokingly called "modern primitives," is full of hardships and dangers unimaginable to outsiders [^7] [^12].

To relieve the long and highly tense alpine life, the observers developed a unique survival aesthetics and human touch at the summit of the North Peak. The station carries the humorous nickname "3858 CAFE." Occasional visits from hikers or superiors allow them to drink a cup of hot tea in this highest, almost isolated corner of Taiwan [^9] [^10].

## Historical and Institutional Evolution: From Military Outpost to National Meteorological Hub

The establishment of the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station is not only an extension of scientific technology but also a microcosm of Taiwan's modern political and social changes. During the Japanese colonial period, the Governor-General's Office selected Jade Mountain's North Peak as the site for the observatory to control the high-altitude atmospheric circulation across Taiwan and East Asia. At that time, construction materials and precision instruments were carried up the mountain step by step by hundreds of Indigenous Taiwanese porters and Japanese technicians. This arduous engineering laid the cornerstone of Taiwan's alpine meteorological observation.

After the war, the Nationalist Government took over the meteorological facilities, renaming them and re-incorporating them under the jurisdiction of the Central Meteorological Bureau. As Taiwan's economy took off and technology developed, the mission of the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station evolved from early simple manual visual observation to a comprehensive hub including automatic meteorological sensing, satellite cloud image reception, alpine environment monitoring, and earthquake reporting. However, technological upgrades have not replaced human value. Precisely because the climate on Jade Mountain's North Peak is too extreme—strong winds often reaching typhoon-force levels or above, winter snow accumulating to feet deep—any automated instrument is prone to failure when facing hail, lightning, or blizzards. The presence of observers is to ensure the continuity and accuracy of the national meteorological database through manual repair and visual calibration when technology fails [^11] [^13].

## Unsung Heroes Behind the Meteorological Data: Solitude, Dedication, and Generational Heritage

In the work logs of the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station, every page is densely recorded with temperature, pressure, relative humidity, wind direction, and wind speed. These seemingly dry data are indispensable key bases for Taiwan to prevent natural disasters such as typhoons, heavy rain, and cold waves. Whenever the Central Meteorological Agency issues marine or land typhoon warnings, the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station, located at the first landing point of the typhoon or the direct impact point of the peripheral circulation, becomes the most frontline "whistleblower" in Taiwan.

However, the outside world often only sees the result of whether the weather forecast is accurate, but few can appreciate the cost required to maintain this data. Observers on the mountain must face harsh physiological limits. Living in high-altitude areas for a long time not only places extreme burden on the heart and lungs but also significantly reduces sleep quality due to low pressure. But the deeper test is the psychological pressure of isolation. In the era when communication equipment was not as developed as today, the connection between the meteorological station and the plains below relied only on radio; when heavy snow sealed the mountain and helicopters could not take off or land, personnel could not descend for their shifts as scheduled, having to rely on limited rations to survive on the summit for several more weeks.

Therefore, every staff member of the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station has left an indelible mark in the history of Taiwan's meteorological development. They are not heroes under the spotlight, but silent sentinels who built a windbreak forest for this island with their flesh and blood.

## Modernization and Future Challenges of Alpine Meteorological Observation: Guarding Taiwan Amidst Global Change

Entering the 21st century, global climate change has intensified, and extreme weather events occur frequently, making the role of alpine meteorological observation stations more critical than ever. The Jade Mountain Meteorological Station not only continues to provide ground meteorological elements but has also gradually expanded its participation in international atmospheric and environmental change research networks. Through long-term monitoring of temperature, rainfall, and atmospheric composition, scientists can precisely analyze the specific rate at which Taiwan's alpine ecosystem is impacted by warming.

Facing the future, the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station is continuously introducing higher weather-resistant digital automatic sensing and satellite communication equipment to reduce the risk of observers frequently going out for maintenance in extreme weather. On the other hand, the Meteorological Agency is actively promoting the preservation of alpine meteorological history and science popularization education, allowing the public to see through documentaries and special reports how this scientific fortress hidden above the 3,858-meter clouds guards the safety of the people across Taiwan. This long-term observation also ensures that alpine precipitation data is no longer just work records within the meteorological station, but becomes basic data for understanding Jade Mountain's hydrology and mountain environment [^1].

## Why Observation Data Cannot Be Interrupted: The Public Nature of a Mountain Station

The importance of the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station lies not only in its location near Taiwan's highest peak, but in the fact that it fixes an observation height that does not exist on the plains into Taiwan's entire meteorological observation network. The Meteorological Agency lists the Jade Mountain Station as one of its affiliated stations; the station page records its location, altitude, and institutional history. The Taiwan Science and Education Museum places it in the historical context of observation stations in central and southern Taiwan, pointing out that it is simultaneously Taiwan's highest building and administrative agency [^1] [^2]. In other words, this is not for hikers to have one more scenic spot, but for meteorological data to continuously accumulate at an extreme height and extreme weather position.

This height also changes the meaning of "routine work." On the plains, checking sensors might just mean opening the server room door; on the North Peak, checking instruments means first judging wind, snow, visibility, and ground ice conditions, then deciding whether it is safe to leave the station. When ice and snow cover the instruments, observers cannot just wait for the weather to recover on its own; they must handle equipment according to on-site conditions and cross-check manual observations with instrument readings [^2] [^8]. These actions have no dramatic endpoint, only the next observation moment arriving on time; the value of the meteorological station lies precisely in this continuity that is not easily seen.

The work of high-altitude stations thus requires both professional training and life skills. Station staff not only read meteorological elements but also understand the alpine environment, maintain communication, handle supplies and equipment, and adjust daily arrangements when supply and shift changes are obstructed. Reports from _Taiwan Panorama_ and _United Daily News_ focus on these details: how stationed personnel face ice and snow, lightning, supply constraints, and long periods away from home, rather than writing Jade Mountain only as a "successful challenge" summit [^7] [^9] [^10]. Observation numbers and life experiences meet in the same station building; the former enters the forecasting system, while the latter determines whether data can be produced on time.

## After Automation, Humans Are Still On Site

Modern meteorological observation indeed uses a large number of automatic sensors and remote transmission, but "automatic" does not mean "no maintenance required." Wind and snow change the state of exposed instrument components; lightning may damage power and communication equipment; extreme environments also mean sensor results require manual interpretation. The history of the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station is not about manual observation being eliminated by technology, but about the gradual division of labor between manual and automated systems: instruments are responsible for stable recording, while station staff are responsible for confirmation, repair, and supplementing on-site judgment during anomalies [^1] [^2].

This is why the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station should not be understood merely as a "highest building." The building keeps people and equipment at the summit; the data connects the summit with forecasting, research, and disaster prevention work on the plains; without either, this hub would be reduced to a mere geographical record. From the 1943 Shinhotaka Observatory, to the post-war Jade Mountain Observatory, to today's meteorological station, name changes reflect administrative system changes, while continuous observation leaves a scientific context spanning institutions [^1] [^3] [^5].

Therefore, what station staff guard is not an abstract "first place," but a public service that must be completed anew every day. People below do not need to know at what time every de-icing event occurred, nor see what weather each shift change encountered; they only need to receive informed information before typhoons, cold waves, or heavy rains arrive. This information can be trusted only if someone completes the observation work where data is hardest to obtain [^6] [^8]. Therefore, what is most worth remembering about the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station is not the title of "highest," but how it transforms difficult on-site work into public data usable by everyone: the wind and snow at the summit eventually become a forecast, a warning, or an early避险 decision on the plains, returning abstract meteorological data truly to everyone's daily life and public safety.

## How a Mountain Station Becomes a Node for Long-Term Data

There is a time scale in meteorological station work that is easily overlooked: a single observation can only answer the weather at a specific moment; continuous accumulation of observations over many years is what has the opportunity to answer seasonal differences, warming/cooling changes, and precipitation distribution. The Jade Mountain Station's inclusion in the meteorological observation network means that high-altitude data is not scattered hiking anecdotes, but records produced, preserved, and comparable with other stations according to institutional rules [^1] [^2]. This comparison requires stable station locations, fixed observation procedures, and someone to leave notes when equipment anomalies occur, rather than treating incomplete data as complete results.

The height of Jade Mountain's North Peak gives it special observation value, but also makes the cost of obtaining data far higher than on the plains. The Meteorological Agency's official data provides station and institutional positioning; historical special articles supplement the background of station construction and the alpine environment; looking at both types of materials together allows us to understand that the Jade Mountain Station is not merely a commemorative building, but a continuously operating public infrastructure [^1] [^3] [^5]. Its "height" is not the end of the story, but the starting point for why a station must be here and why maintenance work is particularly difficult.

From the perspective of data quality, manual observation still has a function that automatic equipment cannot fully replace: when readings are inconsistent with on-site weather, station staff can record rime ice, freezing, lightning strikes, or equipment status, leaving context for subsequent interpretation. Interviews in _United Daily News Online_ and _Taiwan Panorama_ make this context concrete in the daily details of shift changes, supply, maintenance, and waiting for weather permission [^8] [^9]. These are not fields on a meteorological data sheet, but important background for judging how data is produced and when it might be interrupted.

## From Observatory to Observation Network: The Journey of a Data Point

Placing the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station back into Taiwan's meteorological history reveals that it is not an isolated summit facility. The Taiwan Science and Education Museum organizes the construction history of the meteorological observation network, placing the Jade Mountain Station in the context of the gradual formation of central and southern observatories; the Meteorological Agency's official data places current station duties, institutional affiliation, and observation functions within the same administrative system [^1] [^2]. The station building thus has two identities simultaneously: it is a workplace for the alpine site, and a node in the national observation network at a high-altitude position.

Once an observation data point leaves Jade Mountain's North Peak, it does not lose its on-site background just by entering the digital system. It still needs to know the station's height, instrument status, weather at the time of observation, and whether the data was affected by freezing, lightning, or communication interruption. These conditions indicate that the credibility of meteorological data comes not only from the numbers themselves, but also from whether the procedure for generating the numbers can be traced. Official station pages, meteorological historical materials, and interviews with stationed personnel each provide evidence at different levels; together they constitute a more complete record of the Jade Mountain Station [^1] [^2] [^8].

This multi-level data chain also reminds readers not to misunderstand "automatic observation" as "unmanned observation." Automatic equipment can shorten transmission time, but cannot automatically replace frozen parts, judge equipment status after lightning strikes, or rearrange life when supply is delayed. The maintenance done by on-site personnel is not, on the surface, forecast content, but directly affects whether data can continue to appear. The repeated mentions of de-icing, maintenance, shift changes, and supply in interviews are precisely the actual costs of this system [^7] [^9] [^10].

From a preservation perspective, the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station is also worth understanding as a "historical site still in use." The name and system of the observatory established in 1943 later changed, but the station's functions continued to be inherited in different eras. This makes it not just a building displaying the past, but a place that still interacts with public forecasting, disaster prevention decisions, and climate research every day [^3] [^5]. The weather warnings seen by readers on the plains and the observation, maintenance, and records in the station building on the summit actually belong to the same public service chain.

## Conclusion: Eternal Watch in the Snow and Wind

From the wooden bungalow of the Japanese colonial period to today's solid modernized observation station, the Jade Mountain Meteorological Station has witnessed the development trajectory of Taiwan's modern meteorological science from nothing. Behind every uploaded number of temperature, pressure, and wind direction are the footprints of countless observers stepping through sub-zero low temperatures, strong winds, blizzards, and solitude. When people on the plains close their doors and windows on typhoon days and rely on weather forecasts to spend time safely, few remember that above the clouds at 3,858 meters, several "modern primitives" wrapped in heavy clothing are silently de-icing next to the frozen anemometers with kettles of hot water [^7] [^12]. The Jade Mountain Meteorological Station is not just a cold collection point for scientific instruments; it is a spiritual coordinate on Taiwan's land, reminding people to maintain reverence before nature and to guard their duties amidst extremes and solitude.
