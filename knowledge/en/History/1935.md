> **30 Second Summary:** At 6:02 AM on April 21, 1935, a major earthquake struck near Guan Dao Mountain. Official statistics in the _Showa Decade Taiwan Earthquake Journal_ reported 3,279 deaths, 11,976 injuries, and 17,927 destroyed homes. Yang Chao-chia of Shinsui noted "Shinsui Wiped Out" on a business card, while Huang Wang-cheng recorded his family fleeing near Shenjing in his diary. After the disaster, the government established a reconstruction committee to repair railways, redraw streets, and install seismic monitoring equipment. The history of this disaster is not just found in broken bridges and collapsed houses, but also in who could define safety, who had to relocate, and how local residents pieced back their lost lives. This article traces how the disaster became a starting point for governance through one business card, two diaries, three types of public infrastructure, and one post-disaster photograph.[^1] [^2] [^3]

On the morning of April 21, 1935, Huang Wang-cheng was brushing his teeth. The diary did not first compile a disaster summary but left several consecutive actions: the well suddenly shook, his family fled in panic, and another strong tremor came twenty minutes later, sending roof tiles flying; his family dared not return to the house and had to sit near the deep well. Huang Wang-cheng described the sound as "like an ox's roar," recording the earthquake first as a sound, then as a danger from walls, doors, and furniture.[^1] [^2]

On the same morning, local resident Yang Chao-chia witnessed the disaster in his hometown and produced a business card stating "Shinsui Wiped Out," demanding that local officials immediately return to Taichung. He later assisted with investigations, accompanying the governor and officials to inspect Shinsui, Wusheng, Shalu, Sanyi, Houli, and other areas, leaving about two hundred post-disaster images. This business card was not a complete report, yet it made one locality suddenly unignorable on the administrative map.[^1]

## Epicenter, Magnitude, and an Inconsistent Statistical Table

The earthquake occurred at 6:02 AM on April 21, 1935, with its epicenter near Guan Dao Mountain in what was then Hsinchu Prefecture, roughly where Sanyi and Dahu meet in Miaoli County today. Because the main disaster zone spanned southern Hsinchu Prefecture and northern Taichung Prefecture, it is later commonly referred to as the "Hsinchu-Taichung Great Earthquake." Areas such as Neipu Township, Shengang Township, Shinsui Street, and Gongguan Township were particularly severely affected.[^1] [^2] [^3]

Historical earthquake articles from the Central Weather Administration describe it with a moment magnitude of 6.9 and an earthquake moment magnitude of 7.2. The archive page at the Academia Sinica Institute of Taiwan History records it as a moment magnitude of 7.1. This is not a difference that can be arbitrarily chosen. The observation instruments used in 1935, the later recalculated seismic moments, and the scales adopted during different compilations could inherently lead to different results. When discussing disaster impact, this article uses the death and injury figures and building damage numbers from the official earthquake journal; when referring to magnitude, it retains the measurement scope.[^1] [^2]

The _Showa Decade Taiwan Earthquake Journal_ records 3,279 deaths, 11,976 injuries, and a total of 61,685 damaged buildings. The Central Weather Administration article lists 17,927 completely destroyed households and 11,446 partially destroyed ones, estimating agricultural, industrial, and commercial losses at nearly 4.57 million yuan. These figures show that the earthquake was not merely a building collapse event; railways, bridges, roads, telephones, and telegraphs were also damaged.[^2] [^3]

The distribution of damage and the epicenter did not perfectly overlap. Fault lines passed through densely populated Neipu Township and Shengang Township, where traditional buildings like _Tukakcuo_ (earthen houses) had limited seismic resistance, turning ground destruction into mass casualties. The housing, roads, and public facilities in Shinsui, Houli, Fengyuan, and the Miaoli area later became focal points of official investigation and reconstruction plans.[^2] [^3]

## "Shinsui Wiped Out" and How Locals Reported the Disaster

In an era without real-time images or mobile communication, disaster reports had to be sent by foot, written on paper, via railway, or by telegraph. Yang Chao-chia's business card was a form of local reporting. It compressed the condition of Shinsui into four characters before handing it over to officials who were required to return to Taichung. This act was both a plea for help and a demand for the administrative apparatus to acknowledge that Shinsui had become a disaster zone.

The photos of Yang Chao-chia preserved in the Academia Sinica archives cover not only Shinsui but also Wusheng, Shalu, Sanyi, Houli, Shi'gang, Zhuolan, Zhudong, and Zhunan.

The images connected the disaster zone into a long strip, showing that the earthquake crossed roads, railways, fault lines, and administrative boundaries, forming localized crises that occurred simultaneously.[^1]

Huang Wang-cheng's diary provided another form of reporting. He did not write official classifications first; instead, he wrote about brushing his teeth, fleeing to the well, ash falling in the study, and roof tiles flying. Lin Xian-tang's diary recorded members of the Wufeng Lin family falling and being injured, chimneys breaking, roof tiles cracking, and earthen houses collapsing in the township. Both diaries broke down "home" into many parts requiring verification: were people injured, was the roof still there, would the walls fall, and did the family dare to return that night?[^1] [^2]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Official reports excel at turning disasters into comparable fields; diaries retain the chaos before those fields existed. Only when placed together do they prevent victims from being reduced merely to numbers of dead, injured, or collapsed houses.

## The Railway Was Severed, and Rescue Had to Be Rerouted

Not only were buildings damaged during the earthquake. Records in the National Cultural Memory Repository show that ground damage, embankment subsidence, foundation erosion, and bridge damage occurred between Zhunan and Yuanli, directly affecting railway operations. A year after the disaster, the Taichung Earthquake Reconstruction Office of the Railway Department at the Governor's Office published the _Showa Decade Taiwan Earthquake Journal_, followed by the _Showa Ten Year Taiwan Railway Earthquake Journal_, which included railway damage and repair in the disaster records.[^4]

The topography of the Old Mountain Line was even more difficult to manage. The National Museum of Natural Science Electronic News reported that the 1935 earthquake severely damaged 7 tunnels and 3 bridges on the Old Mountain Line. While the coastal line recovered relatively quickly, the Old Mountain Line took about three years to repair. A memorial for disaster reconstruction near Taian Old Station today reminds people that the railway restoration was a long-term engineering effort that caused prolonged transportation disruption.[^7]

The fracture of Yutongping Bridge later became one of the most visible earthquake relics.

Originally part of the longitudinal mountain line project, only bridge piers remained after the earthquake. The Jiji earthquake in 1999 damaged some piers at the same site, layering two seismic memories onto one location. Seeing the Yutongping broken bridge as a scenic spot is seeing a remnant; placing it back into the transportation history of 1935 reveals how the bridge's fracture delayed the reconnection of people, materials, and localities.[^2] [^7]

Railway repair also held political significance. When the coastal line recovered faster while the mountain line was still under construction, transportation inequality became concrete in the speed at which different places received supplies, news, and official inspections. Official reports would record when a section reopened; local memories would remember who returned first, where there were still no trains, and how to reach neighboring townships after roads were cut by fissures.

## The Reconstruction Committee: Restoring to Normal, or Redrawing the Streets?

Shortly after the disaster, the Taiwan Governor's Office established the "Disaster Area Reconstruction Committee." When the National Archives of Taiwan History introduced the _Showa Decade Taiwan Earthquake Journal_, it divided post-disaster work into four parts: earthquake, damage, relief, and reconstruction. Reconstruction included not only repairing collapsed houses but also urban planning, tax reductions, self-reliance, and disaster relief.[^3]

Urban planning literally means improving neighborhoods, but in reality, it involved widening and straightening roads, drainage, public spaces, and building codes. Master's theses studies point out that the Governor's Office planned urban renewal for multiple towns in the central region after the earthquake and observed how these plans affected street structures. This transformation could indeed reduce risks caused by narrow streets and non-seismic houses, but it also redefined land boundaries, road setbacks, and how residents could rebuild their homes.[^5]

The mass collapse of _Tukakcuo_ (earthen houses) during the disaster became a recurring topic of official discussion. It was easy to attribute the problem to "unsafe old houses," but this ignored that the prevalence of earthen houses was related to material availability, family finances, rural life, and local construction methods. When the government prohibited or restricted certain building materials, safety standards clashed with construction costs. Post-disaster planning decided not only to improve seismic resistance in homes but also which ways of life could continue to exist.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** "Reconstruction" is often written as a story of cities becoming prettier, but before the roads were straightened, someone had to give up land; before the houses became more earthquake-resistant, someone had to bear the cost of more expensive materials. Safety has never been an abstract goodwill without cost.

Urban planning also made the governing capacity of the colonial government visible. Through surveying, maps, road plans, and building codes, the disaster area was transformed into a manageable space. This system could mobilize funds and professionals, but it could also compress local choices into engineering standards. Post-disaster construction thus had both protective and controlling aspects; it cannot be glossed over with just the word "modernization."

## After the Earthquake, The Observation System Began to Grow

The 1935 earthquake exposed the deficiencies in Taiwan's seismic observation and disaster reporting. The Central Weather Administration article points out that there were over 60 felt earthquakes within a month of the strong tremor; telecommunications, telephones, railways, bridges, and roads island-wide were damaged. The earthquake process was not complete with one shake; aftershocks, tremors, road blockages, and rumors prolonged the crisis.[^2]

Rumors also appeared locally during the earthquake. The Central Weather Administration records that the Governor's Office required prefectural governments to crack down on rumor-mongers, resulting in 14 cases investigated across Hsinchu and Taichung Prefectures. Some rumors linked the earthquake to the postponement of Mazu processions; some predicted a larger quake before midnight, and even impersonated weather stations to issue notices, leading people to camp outdoors. The official handling of rumors shows that disaster information itself was part of governance. When railways were cut off, telephones failed, and local offices held fragmented pieces of news, who could issue credible information controlled a part of the post-disaster order.[^2]

After the earthquake, the Governor's Office added or strengthened seismic observation equipment and planned seismic research and observation facilities in Hsinchu Prefecture. Central Weather Administration data indicates that the Shinsu Observatory was later established with the focus on island-wide seismic activity and earthquake forecasting, commencing operation in 1938. This path transformed a single earthquake into instruments, observation stations, reports, and professional training.[^2] [^3]

However, the observation system did not immediately equate to mature forecasting ability. People in 1935 still could not know which aftershock would cause greater damage, nor could they transmit live images via modern mobile phones. What the observation stations could do was accumulate data, measure seismic direction, organize epicenters and aftershocks, gradually turning "earth upheaval" into a comparable seismological object.

## Disaster Donations: Music Also Became a Rescue Route

Post-disaster relief was not limited to police, hospitals, and engineering units. Data from the Academia Sinica archives on the "Disaster Donation Concerts" shows that Tsai Pei-hwo initiated touring concerts to aid victims. A special report by the Central Weather Administration records that this event toured 31 locations over 42 days, penetrating rural areas and becoming one of the early Western music tours in Taiwan that reached the countryside.[^6] [^2]

This is easily written as a touching interlude, but it was also a form of social mobilization. Musicians had to travel to different places; local organizations needed to provide venues and promotion; audiences turned a performance into relief resources through donations. The earthquake destabilized railways and roads, yet the donation activities still attempted to connect cities and rural areas along the transportation network.

It also reminds us that post-disaster society was not just composed of victims and the government. Local gentry, social activists, female musicians, schools, and private groups could all participate in relief efforts. These actions were not necessarily outside the framework of the colonial government, but they allowed victims to engage in relief with their own organizations and voices, rather than merely waiting for treasury allocations or official orders. The official earthquake journal also records that hospitals, police officers, and private groups formed relief teams, which handled the injured, transported supplies, and provided temporary shelter, making disaster relief a task spanning administrative and local networks.[^3]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The significance of the donation concerts is not just "soothing hearts with music." It turned cultural activities into a collaboration of transport, fundraising, and local organization, meaning post-disaster publicness did not only emerge from government offices.

## What We Can Still See Today From 1935

Some relics of the 1935 earthquake are striking, such as the Yutongping broken bridge and the memorial near Taian Old Station. Others are less visible, such as a wider street, a section of reconfigured drainage system, a road straightened on a map, or an established seismic observation system. If public memory only retains photos of broken bridges, it forgets that post-disaster reconstruction actually modified more daily spaces.[^4] [^7]

Public history research at UTS views the 1935 earthquake as a case study of disaster sites and public memory. This perspective helps us understand that a site is not merely transparent evidence left by nature. The choice of which bridge to preserve, which village to commemorate, or which photo to display is also a choice about how to narrate the disaster. When an earthquake is placed in monuments, local exhibitions, digital archives, and school textbooks, the past returns to the present in different forms.[^9]

For today's reader, the most important question from 1935 is not "whether we have progressed compared to then." More worth asking is whether, when we discuss disaster prevention, seismic resistance, and reconstruction today, we still treat safety as a standard unilaterally determined by professionals. The urban planning of 1935 made streets more orderly, but it did not necessarily allow all residents equal participation. The observation system of 1935 increased knowledge, but it could not compensate the locality for the cost of relocation and lost homes.

On the morning of April 21, 1935, Huang Wang-cheng waited by the well for the shaking to stop, while Yang Chao-chia sent Shinsui into the administrative view with a business card. The subsequent statistics, railway repair plans, urban planning projects, and observation stations were systems grown from these chaotic scenes. They allowed Taiwan to better recognize earthquakes and re-integrate local life into the national map. A new road on the map, an observation station in a report, or a section of drainage next to a home are all results of technical choices and power relations.

Therefore, this earthquake did not leave a single conclusion. The statistics from local offices, the repairs by engineering units, the instruments at observation stations, and the diaries of residents each preserved a part of the scene. Reading these materials together shows that "post-disaster" actually lasted for many years. How roads were traveled, how houses were built, how information was transmitted, and how memory was preserved—all were repeatedly decided after the disaster.

Today, walking through Shinsui, Houli, Miaoli, or along the Old Mountain Line, one may not directly see the 1935 epicenter. However, the road width, railway relics, monuments, and archival images still bring that morning back. They remind us that the systems left by a disaster are not automatically fair. Disaster prevention is not just about reinforcing buildings before the next earthquake. Affected people should also participate in deciding what needs to be preserved, what must change, and who bears the cost of the change. For 1935, this question also includes who could be seen in official reports, whose homes were reduced only to statistics, and how local residents continued to articulate what they lost after reconstruction. Only when local archives are cross-referenced with on-site readings and family memories does disaster history not remain solely from an official perspective.
