> **30-Second Overview:** On the afternoon of August 23, 1958, hundreds of artillery pieces simultaneously opened fire on Kinmen. The first wave lasted 85 minutes, dropping over 30,000 shells. After forty-four days of high-intensity artillery warfare, Kinmen did not return to peaceful daily life but entered an intermittent shelling pattern of "shell on odd days, cease on even days" that continued until 1979. Understanding the 823 cannot just look at which side held the island. One must also see how an island of less than 150 square kilometers layered military strategy, resident displacement, and later battlefield preservation into the same living space.

At 5:30 p.m. on August 23, 1958, Greater and Lesser Kinmen, Dadan, and Erdan islands simultaneously came under dense artillery fire. The first wave lasted 85 minutes, with over 30,000 shells. Kinmen Defense Command Deputy Commander Lieutenant General Ji Xingwen and others were killed in action that day. That was not a "crisis" that only happened on a map, but an afternoon when a command post, villages, a hospital, and a port were all interrupted at once.[^1]

_Photo: Taken by Seasurfer, CC BY-SA 3.0. Image from [Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aug.23_Artillery_Battle_Museum_20050707.jpg)._

This conflict later came to be called the "823 Artillery Battle," and in U.S. and international scholarship is also often called the "Second Taiwan Strait Crisis." The difference in names reminds us: the same artillery battle was both a local disaster for Kinmen and a node in the Cold War order that could drag the United States, China, and U.S. allies in together.[^2]

## Not a One-Day Artillery Battle

The name "823" easily misleads people into thinking the war only occurred on August 23. In fact, the first phase was forty-four days of high-intensity shelling. Thereafter shelling shifted to the "shell on odd days, cease on even days" rhythm; sporadic but persistent artillery fire continued until 1979. Some sources treat August 23, 1958 to January 7, 1959 as the complete artillery battle period; some official narratives treat the forty-four days as the main campaign and list the rest as intermittent shelling. These time calibrations are not identical, but they point to the same thing: what Kinmen faced was not a single explosion, but a prolonged state of war.[^2] [^3] [^4]

During the forty-four days of the main artillery battle, the Kinmen island group endured approximately 470,000 shells. Local Kinmen historical records note that the island group of about 148 square kilometers averaged about 4 shells per square meter, with 80 civilian deaths, 85 seriously injured, 136 lightly injured, 2,612 houses completely destroyed, and 2,321 half-destroyed. Numbers are not for manufacturing a war spectacle. They turn "the shelling was fierce" into what an island's houses, bodies, and medical resources actually lost.[^3]

> **Curator's Note:** The most counterintuitive aspect of the 823 Artillery Battle is that "ceasefire" did not equal "stop living in war." Kinmen people did not start enduring war only after the guns fell silent; rather, after the gunfire became regular, they learned to schedule war into each day's timetable. The curatorial difficulty of this memory is to make the regularity itself visible, not just display the most intense first wave of shelling.

## Kinmen Was Not a Blank Square on the Chessboard

Before the artillery battle, Kinmen was already a highly militarized island. When troops stationed there in 1949, the local population was about 37,000. By the eve of the 823 Artillery Battle, the total military-civilian population had increased to about 130,000, with the national defense budget accounting for 70% of annual expenditure. These figures show that war did not suddenly fall on a purely military base, but on a place with villages, farmland, schools, and clan networks.[^5]

Early shelling mainly targeted military objectives on the island; later it intensified the blockade of maritime supply lines, intending to trap Kinmen in materiel and manpower. For the military, the port was a supply line. For residents, the port was simultaneously the possibility of rice, medicine, news, and leaving.[^3] [^4]

Supply thus became the most easily overlooked battlefield of the 823. When shelling cut off roads and ports, the front lines needed ammunition; residents needed food and medicine. U.S. escorted supply ships reaching Kinmen was not just military news; it also determined whether hospitals could continue treating wounded, whether schools and families could get the next batch of supplies. This shared military-civilian supply line shows that Kinmen's military geography was never two separate maps from civilian life.[^3] [^5]

U.S. involvement was also not a simple "ally support" story. The 1954 Sino-American Mutual Defense Treaty explicitly covered defense of Taiwan and Penghu; whether it covered Kinmen and Matsu remained disputed. After the artillery battle erupted, the U.S. Seventh Fleet assisted in escorting supplies, and deployed fighters, missiles, and combat command resources to Taiwan. At the same time, the U.S. government hoped Chiang Kai-shek would consider withdrawing from the offshore islands to avoid being drawn into a war not necessarily controllable.[^2] [^5]

Therefore, Kinmen's fate cannot be described only by the two outcomes of "held" or "lost." Taipei wanted Kinmen to remain a frontline shield; Beijing wanted to test U.S. reactions through shelling and change the Taiwan Strait situation; Washington both supported defense and limited conflict expansion. Kinmen residents lived at the intersection of these three strategic calculations, yet had no power to decide when shells would fall.[^2] [^5]

## Residents Under Shelling: Hide Underground, or Leave Home

War history most often leaves generals, fleets, and communiqués; residents' bodies are easily compressed into "collateral damage." Kinmen records show that about 5,700 residents of Lesser Kinmen, including 1,500 children, once lived in underground spaces during the artillery battle. Kinmen's approximately 44,000 residents had only 3 doctors and 9 nurses; plasma, penicillin, and other antibiotics were all insufficient. These figures present not abstract livelihood hardship, but who could endure the damp cold in underground shelters, who could wait for medicine, and who had no place to return to.[^5]

The artillery battle also prompted resident evacuation to Taiwan. In October 1958, 921 students from Kinmen Senior High School evacuated first. In three batches, a total of 6,509 Kinmen residents arrived in Kaohsiung; some stayed with relatives, others temporarily housed in schools, then distributed to seven counties and cities in central and southern Taiwan for resettlement. This was not a neatly completed relocation plan; many brought only simple luggage, and still had to deal with Kinmen-region restricted currency, work, housing, and family separation.[^5]

"Moving to Taiwan" meant different things to different people. For military authorities, it was evacuation to reduce frontline civilian casualties. For those who left, it might be temporary refuge, or a life turning point of never returning. For those who stayed, it meant continuing daily life between military control and empty houses. Writing only "the government resettled over six thousand" would flatten all these choices and non-choices.[^5]

> **Curator's Note:** An island's war memory should not only ask "who won." It must also ask: who was forced to leave, who stayed to guard empty houses, who years later still remembers the dampness of underground shelters in their own body. If an exhibition only has generals, gun barrels, and maps, resident displacement once again becomes background.

## "Shell on Odd Days, Cease on Even Days": How Shelling Became Daily Routine

In October 1958, the PLA announced abandonment of the maritime blockade, then gradually adopted the "shell on odd days, cease on even days" rhythm: shell on odd-numbered days, ceasefire on even-numbered days. This arrangement is rare in military history, yet on Kinmen it formed an unsettling regularity. Ceasefire days allowed house repairs, supply transport, and field work, but could not guarantee the next odd day would not reopen fire. "Peace" became a time unit guaranteed only until tomorrow.[^3] [^4]

It also entangled war with administration, education, and family life. Schools needed evacuation or adjustment; ports needed to await escorts; residents needed to allocate time between air raid shelters, civil defense teams, and farming. Militarization was not just pillboxes and gun barrels; it included how currency was used, when news could be reported, how villagers accepted training, and whether offshore island populations could move freely.[^5]

After the 1960s, the main Taiwan Strait confrontation gradually shifted from direct military offense-defense to international politics and long-term standoff. Historian Xue Huayuan notes that after the 823 Artillery Battle, military annihilation of the opponent was no longer the only feasible imagination; Taiwan's internal discussions on democracy, rule of law, and "counterattack the mainland" policy were also affected. This is not to say the artillery battle directly created democratization, but that it changed the conditions under which government, society, and the international environment could discuss politics.[^2]

On January 1, 1979, the United States established diplomatic relations with the People's Republic of China, and Taiwan and the United States terminated diplomatic relations. The same day, the Mainland Chinese side issued the "Message to Compatriots in Taiwan," announcing cessation of shelling against Kinmen, Dadan, Erdan, and other islands. Counting from the 1958 high-intensity artillery battle, the state of war spanned twenty years. Counting from residents' memory, it had no neat endpoint declarable by communiqué.[^3] [^4]

## News, Blockade, and Waiting

The 823 also changed how Kinmen people obtained news. In the early artillery battle, news reporting was restricted by military secrecy; the outside world often saw only communiqués, battle briefings, and a few journalists' eyewitness reports. For island residents, news was not an abstract media issue, but a life tool for judging whether family was safe, whether ships could dock, whether children must leave, and where the next meal would come from. When news was delayed or filtered, waiting itself became part of the war.[^2] [^5]

The restoration of maritime supply also cannot be understood only as military success. After escorted ships arrived, the military needed to reallocate ammunition and fodder; medical personnel needed to handle new wounded; residents had to rearrange life between damaged houses and underground spaces. Kinmen's supply line simultaneously bore national defense, medical, and civilian livelihood functions. This is why the 823's logistics history deserves to be written separately. It lets one see that what an artillery battle truly attacks is not just artillery positions, but a society's capacity to maintain daily life.[^3] [^5] [^8]

Student evacuation especially illustrates how war enters families. After 921 Kinmen Senior High School students left the island, school education was scattered across Taiwan; students temporarily gained safety but also left behind original classmates, teachers, and family. This arrangement may have been militarily necessary, but in personal life history it is a suddenly severed youth. Evacuation to Taiwan is not a simple "rescued" story; it simultaneously contains relief, homesickness, readjustment, and the complex emotions of not knowing when return might be possible.[^5]

## Figures and Positions in War Memory

Official commemoration of the 823 often begins with the three fallen deputy commanders Ji Xingwen, Zhao Jiaxiang, and Zhang Jie. Ji Xingwen is repeatedly mentioned due to his historical role in the 1937 Marco Polo Bridge Incident. These figures give the event recognizable faces, and let the state establish collective memory through memorial ceremonies. However, if figure commemoration stops at generals and martyrs, it easily leaves residents, medical staff, teachers, civil defense members, and evacuated families outside the frame.[^4] [^9]

A more complete writing does not weaken soldiers' sacrifice, but places different positions in the same narrative. Commanders bore decisions and responsibility; soldiers bore frontline danger; medical staff bore resource scarcity; residents bore destroyed homes and scattered families; students waited for battle news in strange cities. These experiences differ, yet together constitute the 823. Only by placing them side by side will readers not mistake "military-civilian unity" for a slogan that erases difference.

This is also the practical problem museum exhibitions face. Shells, firearms, uniforms, and maps can quickly explain war's scale, but cannot necessarily explain how a family spent a ceasefire day. An evacuation roster, a doctor's recollection, a student's carried luggage — not as eye-catching as weapons — can let audiences understand how war changed people's time, identity, and relationships. The 823 Battle History Museum, updated in 2025, began incorporating diverse perspectives of residents, widows, and journalists — an attempt to push exhibitions from campaign chronology toward collective memory.[^7]

## From Military Sites to Peace Education

The value of Kinmen's battlefield sites lies not only in their former defensive function, but in how they record society's handling of legacy after war ended. Pillboxes may sit on private land, or involve military sensitivity, public safety, and maintenance costs. Demolition or preservation are not purely technical choices, but judgments on "what deserves to be seen by the next generation." The Shishishan Pillbox controversy is therefore representative. It reminds us that without first completing comprehensive inventory and value assessment, withdrawal policy may erase historical evidence faster than shellfire.[^6]

Preserving battlefield sites does not equal glorifying war. Pillbox thickness can let one imagine defensive engineering, but cannot substitute resident casualty data. Monuments can remind people to remember sacrifice, but cannot alone explain why the two sides reached long-term shelling. Peace education needs to place sites back in historical context, letting audiences simultaneously see military decisions, international intervention, civilian cost, and postwar politics. The Ministry of National Defense's commemorative narrative, local historical materials, and the Battle History Museum's new exhibitions should cross-reference, not replace each other.[^4] [^7] [^9]

If one walks into the 823 Battle History Museum today, what is most worth noticing is not which weapon is largest, but whether the exhibition lets one understand how an island maintained society amid shellfire. This includes who was responsible for delivering water, who cared for the wounded, who repaired roads, who recorded deaths, who put children on ships, and who returned to destroyed villages after ceasefire. When these questions enter the exhibition, Kinmen becomes not just a "living military museum," but a place still learning how to speak of war and peace.[^7] [^10]

## Same War, Why Different Numbers

The 823's casualty and shell numbers cannot be spliced into a single table without explanation. Kinmen local data focuses on civilian casualties and house damage; the Presidential Office commemorative draft combines military and civilian casualties and military facility losses. Historical analysis articles may adopt different battle start-end ranges. Therefore, this article uses 470,000+ shells as the order of magnitude for the main artillery battle, and clearly marks 80 deaths, 2,612 houses completely destroyed, etc. as local civilian statistics, not conflating them with the 618 deaths and 3,228 casualties from combined military-civilian statistics.[^3] [^4] [^5]

Such differences are not errors dismissible with "which one is real." Different denominators, time ranges, and statistical objects change our understanding of the war. The responsibility of verification is not to pick the most spectacular number, but to write the statistical caliber next to the number. This is why the 823 needs local historical materials, official commemorative drafts, and academic analyses to cross-read each other, not rely on a single voice.

## Battlefield Preservation: What Remains Is Not Only Victory Memorial

After the 823 Artillery Battle ended, Kinmen still retains large numbers of military facilities and battlefield landscapes.

![823 Battle History Museum, 2016.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/84/Aug.23_Artillery_Battle_Museum_20160620.jpg)

_Photo: Taken by Wei-Te Wong, CC BY-SA 2.0. Image from [Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Aug.23_Artillery_Battle_Museum_20160620.jpg)._

These facilities are on one hand relics of the national defense system; on the other they may sit on civilian land, hinder land use, or face demolition due to management and safety costs. In 2018, when Kinmen locals discussed Shishishan Pillbox demolition, controversy centered on a sharp question: after military facilities withdraw, who has the right to decide what deserves to stay?[^6]

This is not a problem solved by simply sealing all pillboxes. Preservation requires inventory, grading, handling military sensitivity, and coordination among local government, national defense units, cultural asset authorities, and residents. If battlefield sites are only packaged as tourist attractions, pillboxes become photo backdrops. If only viewed as burdens, the war life Kinmen people long endured gets erased along with land clearance.[^6]

The 823 Battle History Museum, reactivated in 2025, began placing frontline officers and soldiers, residents, officers' and soldiers' widows, and journalists into the same exhibition narrative, using audio-visual and artifacts to present battlefield life, family rupture, and individual trauma. The significance of this exhibition direction lies not in making war more thrilling, but in letting "military victory" no longer be the only visible memory.[^7]

Kinmen National Park's battlefield cultural preservation therefore faces two responsibilities: both to preserve concrete fortifications, tunnels, and pillboxes, and to preserve their relationships with villages, displacement, civil defense, women's waiting, and family separation. Objects without human stories leave only concrete. Stories without touchable sites easily become slogans in the next generation.[^6] [^7]

## Rereading the 823 Today

The 823 Artillery Battle's importance to Taiwan lies not only in its being a 1958 military event. It moved the Taiwan Strait division from a postwar temporary state into a more stable, yet also longer, standoff under the Cold War. It forced U.S. strategic ambiguity on Kinmen-Matsu, Taipei's offshore island policy, and Beijing's Taiwan Strait policy onto the table simultaneously. It also turned Kinmen into a political geography that must be understood through resident life.[^2] [^5]

If the 823 is only written as "the National Army defended Kinmen, repelled the attack," readers get a conclusion but see no cost. If only written as great power games, the 6,509 evacuated residents, children living underground, hospitals lacking medicine, and people waiting for family to return are silenced again.[^5]

A saying closer to Kinmen experience is: shellfire changed the island's strategic position, and also changed every islander's sense of time. When shells fell, Kinmen was a frontline. When shells fell by the day, Kinmen became a system. When shelling stopped, Kinmen again had to decide how to preserve a history unwilling to be romanticized, yet unable to be deleted.

> The last question the 823 leaves is not "who will win next time," but when war is written as national memory, can those who had no choice in war yet were forced to learn to live in war be written back in together.
