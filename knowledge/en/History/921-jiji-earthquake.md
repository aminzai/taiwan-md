> **30-Second Overview:** At 1:47:15.9 AM on September 21, 1999, a magnitude 7.3 earthquake began near Nantou with a depth of only 8 kilometers.[^1] The central region suffered the most severe damage: official park data recorded 2,415 deaths, 11,305 injuries, and 29 missing persons, with 51,000 houses damaged.[^2] However, this article is not just about a disaster; it is about a choice: Taiwan did not level all the traces to rebuild, but left the uplifted sports field, the displaced dam body, and the collapsed school buildings in place, turning them into textbooks for the next generation to understand earthquakes.

## A Line That Suddenly Appeared at Night

On September 21, 1999, at 1:47:15.9 AM Taiwan time, the Central Weather Administration recorded an earthquake with a latitude of 23.85° N, longitude 120.82° E, a depth of 8 kilometers, and a moment magnitude of 7.3.[^1] This was not just a point on a distant instrument. The maximum seismic intensity in Nantou reached 7, while the maximum intensities in Yunlin, Taichung, and Chiayi were 6. The shaking spread across the central plains and mountains; many people heard objects fall before they realized their houses were moving.

A retrospective by _Taiwan Panorama_ referred to this earthquake as the '921 Earthquake' or 'Chi Chi Earthquake,' recording that the shaking lasted for 102 seconds, causing 2,415 deaths, over 11,000 injuries, and damaging or destroying over 80,000 buildings.[^5] The significance of this earthquake was not just its magnitude, but how close the ground deformation was to human scale: it made the fault line no longer just a line on a geological map, but a site that could pass through school campuses, dam bodies, and transportation facilities.

> 📝 Curator's Note: The most unforgettable image of the 921 is not the abstract word "the earth shook," but the ground genuinely resembling a table pushed up from underneath, causing the sports field, dam body, and roads to change height individually.

## Guangfu Junior High School’s Field Was Not Smoothed Over

Guangfu Junior High School in Wufeng, Taichung, was one of the most visible places where this line passed. After the Chaluopo fault crossed the campus, the eastern half of the sports field rose by 2.6 meters, and several school buildings were damaged.[^2] A retrospective by Public Television documented that concrete result: the track was lifted by the fault, and the damaged school buildings and topography were left behind, later becoming part of the 921 Earthquake Educational Park.[^4]

This decision was somewhat counterintuitive. The most natural post-disaster thought is usually to clear dangerous buildings, fill cracks, and restore life to "the way it was." However, the original site of Guangfu Junior High School was not completely erased. In 2000, the Ministry of Education selected the campus as the base for an earthquake museum, officially naming it the "921 Earthquake Educational Park" in 2001. The Fault Preservation Museum opened in 2004, and the entire area operated in 2007.[^2]

The park is not simply a collection of collapsed buildings fenced off. The National Museum of Natural Science explains it across three aspects: history and culture, earth science, and earthquake resistance technology, integrating exhibition halls with the originally damaged school buildings.[^2] Visitors see not a monument detached from its site, but how the fault passed through a school, how the track height changed, and how architects arranged circulation between safe retreat and original site preservation.

What is preserved here is evidence that "something happened," and it is a classroom for understanding "why something happened." The former makes people stop; the latter tells them what to do next time.

## Shiguo Dam: Earthquakes Don't Just Shake Buildings

Downstream of the Dajia River, Shiguo Dam demonstrates another scale of disaster. The Water Resources Agency described it as a vital hub for water resource utilization in the Dajia River basin. After the 921, the fault passed through the dam area; the right bank rose by 2.2 meters and the left bank rose by 9.8 meters. The dam body and dissipation pool cracked, and several spillway gates were damaged, and the water conveyance tunnel was also damaged.[^3]

The consequence was not simply "the dam broke." Dam damage led to reservoir loss; the intake structure was displaced, and the water conveyance tunnel collapsed. Water purification facilities in Fengyuan, main water pipelines, and local distribution pipes were all affected. The Water Resources Agency recorded that this caused a water shortage for about 2 million people in the Taichung area.[^3] An earthquake thus became an interruption of daily life: faucets, factories, hospitals, and schools were all connected by one invisible line.

A retrospective by Public Television noted that Shiguo Dam was repaired in 2000, but some damaged dam bodies were retained as witnesses to the disaster.[^4] This echoes the choice made at Guangfu Junior High School: restoring function does not mean erasing memory; allowing the dam to continue supplying water also allows people to see what it once endured.

> 📝 Curator's Note: The 921 translated "infrastructure" from an engineer's term into a question everyone understands—if the dam cannot store water, can the city live normally tomorrow?

## Reconstruction Is Not Returning to the Starting Point

Initially, disaster victims did not have abstract historical questions; they only had where to sleep that night. In the central affected area, there was a large number of homeless people after the earthquake, and the government implemented resettlement measures such as temporary prefabricated housing, rent subsidies, and preferential purchases of national housing.[^4] But resettlement is only the first layer of reconstruction. Houses can be rebuilt, but community relationships, jobs, caregiving, and psychological security do not automatically return.

The original reconstruction experience page of the 921 Earthquake Reconstruction Foundation contained entry points for the collective work of civil society, communities, and the government after the disaster, covering various aspects such as housing, community building, social welfare, psychological rehabilitation, and relief.[^7] These classifications themselves indicate that reconstruction is not just about repairing buildings one by one, but about re-establishing the relationship between people and place.

Public Television's data also records that the Executive Yuan formed the 921 Earthquake Reconstruction Foundation with civil society organizations. After the foundation was dissolved in July 2008, the remaining funds and real estate were transferred to a relief fund.[^4] This institutional history reminds us that donations are not the end of the disaster narrative. They enter into organization, planning, and long-term responsibility, which must eventually face how to conclude and how to transfer.

The foundation's original website stated that it was established on October 13, 1999, carried out 32 reconstruction projects over eight and a half years, and transferred unused resources to subsequent relief systems.[^7] These figures are the foundation's own narrative of achievements and should be read separately from government statistics or academic research; they allow one to see how a civil reconstruction organization describes its work, but they cannot replace case-by-case verification of project effectiveness.

## Temporary Prefabricated Housing Is Not the End of the Story

Temporary prefabricated housing is often placed in the background of post-disaster photos, as if it were merely a transition before permanent housing appeared. But for those who lost their homes, temporary accommodation was also a bedroom, living room, family meeting room, and a place where they had to decide whether or not to stay. The government provided prefabricated housing, rent subsidies, and preferential national housing to address "first getting somewhere to live." Communities and civil society organizations handled "how to live after settling in."[^4][^7]

This difference is crucial. House repair has engineering progress; human trust does not. Neighbors may be scattered to different resettlement sites, children change schools, and the elderly lose familiar markets and care networks. Within the same village, some people may wish to rebuild on site, while others want to leave the dangerous area. Lumping everyone into the term "disaster victim" makes these choices disappear.

The data page of the 921 Earthquake Reconstruction Foundation divides reconstruction experiences into different entry points such as local participation, rebuilding, relief, and Hanshin experience, and includes articles on community building, psychological rehabilitation, social welfare, and housing.[^7] This data may not represent everyone's feelings, but it reminds later readers that post-disaster recovery is never a single path, nor does it happen automatically after a central command.

Here, the protagonist of reconstruction is not just a safe new house, but also whether a place can generate shared life again. If reconstruction is only viewed in terms of construction volume, one easily forgets that what someone needs is to re-establish neighbors, jobs, schools, medical care, and a night where they can sleep with peace of mind.

## Three Timelines Left by an Earthquake

The 921 has three different timelines. The first is the instrument's time: 1:47:15.9 AM, depth 8 kilometers, magnitude 7.3.[^1] The second is the building's time: schools were damaged in seconds, and dams lost function after the quake, requiring repairs spanning several years.[^2][^3] The third is human time: a family may still discuss that night years later, while the next generation might only hear about it for the first time in class or at an exhibition hall.[^4]

These three timelines do not naturally align. News must report casualties on the day; engineers must interpret cracks afterward; community workers must accompany people through grief with no clear end; and museums must place these different speeds of memory in one space. The existence of the 921 Earthquake Educational Park is precisely what transforms a few tens of seconds of an earthquake into a long-term course that can be walked into, understood, and questioned.[^2]

A retrospective by _Taiwan Panorama_ mentioned that the disaster prompted Taiwan to re-examine building codes, disaster relief systems, and the relationship between economic development and the natural environment.[^5] These words sound grand, but they ultimately fall into details: how a house is designed, how a bridge is reinforced, how a water pipeline is backed up, or whether a school should retain its damaged site.

> 📝 Curator's Note: Disaster turned "later" into an invoice; what reconstruction truly pays for is not just engineering costs, but our willingness to write the experience as rules.

## From Fault Line to Database

The 921 also changed how people understood engineering data. The National Earthquake Engineering Research Center's technical report database preserved the building damage survey of 1999 in reports cataloged by report number, author, and outcome.[^6] The Bridge Damage Archive organized bridge materials, fall prevention, seismic isolation, pier reinforcement, and numerical simulations into searchable research entries.[^8]

For the general reader, these pages may not bring immediate emotion like a museum. But it is this calm classification that allows subsequent discussions to proceed without starting from scratch. Which structure failed first, which type of reinforcement is worth adopting, and which route might block rescue—all require records for comparison.

Data also has its boundaries. Technical reports excel at describing structures but cannot answer alone how long someone waited in temporary housing; official statistics are good at organizing totals but cannot put every name who lost a relative back into a complete context.[^6][^7] Good post-disaster knowledge is not choosing one side—engineering or human—but allowing both sides to calibrate each other.

Therefore, preserving the site and preserving the files are two aspects of the same thing. The uplifted track at Guangfu Junior High School shows ground deformation; technical reports show how to analyze damage; the damaged dam body at Shiguo allows understanding of the water supply crisis; and hydrological data fixes the displacement of 2.2 meters on the right bank and 9.8 meters on the left.[^3] These records complement each other and also limit each other: earthquake reports cannot tell us how a family lost its home, and oral memory cannot single-handedly replace structural assessment. Only by placing both in one article can we simultaneously see the speed of the disaster, the length of the reconstruction, and the weight that the system must bear.

## From Collapse to Resilience, With Many Practices In Between

After the 921, Taiwan's changes did not only happen inside museums. A retrospective by _Taiwan Panorama_ directly pointed out that the earthquake exposed deficiencies in building standards and disaster relief systems, prompting society to re-examine building codes, geological faults, and post-disaster reconstruction.[^5] These changes were not declared complete in one go but were broken down into tasks that could be repeatedly revised: building safety, engineering research, community drills, and government coordination.

The National Earthquake Engineering Research Center's technical report database preserved the official record of the "Comprehensive Disaster Survey of the 921 Chi Chi Great Earthquake—Building Damage Investigation" from 1999, cataloged as NCREE-1999-054.[^6] The engineering report does not tell the victims' stories, but it organizes collapse patterns, structural weaknesses, and repair methods into knowledge that can be used next time. Insurance cannot prevent houses from collapsing, nor can it restore lost lives; it deals with another often overlooked question: when a disaster shatters both a family's dwelling and assets, who bears the cost of starting over?

The Bridge Damage Archive preserved by the National Earthquake Engineering Research Center focused on engineering issues such as bridges, fall prevention, seismic isolation, pier reinforcement, and numerical simulations.[^8] These studies seem far removed from daily life, but they actually determine whether rescue vehicles can pass through, whether supplies can be delivered, and whether a fractured transportation network can be reconnected.

So, the "after" of the 921 is not a smooth curve of progress. New building codes may increase costs, reinforcing old buildings requires resident consent, and community reconstruction may face disparities due to resource allocation and local power. Presenting post-disaster changes as a beautiful success story tends to obscure the areas that still need maintenance.

## Scales for Viewing Disaster

Photos, earthquake reports, and damaged buildings are not the same type of evidence. Photos bring human vision to a certain moment; reports break moments down into time, coordinates, and numbers; and buildings fix the results of force in materials. Only when placed together does it prevent the 921 from being reduced to a shocking but unanswerable disaster image.[^1][^5]

The value of retrospective articles is not just recounting casualties. They also allow us to see how society understood the disaster at the time: which images were repeatedly published, which issues were featured in special reports, and which institutional gaps are still worth reviewing years later.[^5] For those today who did not personally experience the 921, these images are an entry point, not a conclusion; after the entry point, one must return to official documents, site preservation, and local memory.

Images also need careful use. They can bring readers close to the plight of victims, but they cannot treat others' losses as consumable entertainment. Therefore, this article does not treat photos as decoration for casualty figures, but reads them within their context of source, time, and preservation: which institution preserved it, when was it published, and did it record the disaster itself or a later review—these must be explained separately.

## Keeping Memory in Place

In 2024, Public Television re-organized this earthquake with a 25-year retrospective, beginning by reminding younger generations that many people have no direct memory of that night.[^4] This sentence is important because time turns disaster from bodily memory into a date on a textbook. As firsthand witnesses gradually decrease, preserving the original site is not just commemoration; it is allowing later people to still understand what "the ground rose by 2.6 meters" means.[^2]

The track at Guangfu Junior High School, the dam body at Shiguo Dam, and an official earthquake report preserve three different scales. The track is a scale that human height can feel; the dam body is a scale that city water supply can withstand; and the report fixes those few seconds of the morning into verifiable time, coordinates, depth, and magnitude.[^1] Only when placed together does it prevent the 921 from being just a disaster photo.

> 📝 Curator's Note: Commemoration is not polishing the wound, but retaining enough accurate shape so that those who have not experienced it can still approach it.

Taiwan cannot choose whether the next earthquake will come, but it can choose what to leave behind, what to correct, and what to teach the unborn. The most powerful aspect of the 921 Earthquake Educational Park may be precisely that it did not restore the campus into a place where one could not tell it had been wounded.

It allows a fault line to continue passing through memory. This is not an addiction to staring at disaster, but an acknowledgment: true reconstruction is not making everything look like nothing ever happened, but allowing life to continue after knowing that something did happen.

## What Is Still Missing in This Memory?

An article can put official figures, engineering reports, and preserved sites together, but it cannot pretend that it represents the 921 for everyone. The number of deaths can be statistically counted, but how those who lost their homes spent their first winter requires oral history, local archives, and the community's own records.[^4] This is why the reconstruction foundation's data page not only lists housing and engineering but also retains entry points to articles on social welfare, psychological rehabilitation, and community building.[^7]

Similarly, preserving the original site is not the only answer. For those living in dangerous buildings, leaving ruins might be educational; for those who still need to live, safe, affordable housing and stable income are the starting point of reconstruction. Guangfu Junior High School was able to become an educational park because the campus had other functions, and the preservation of the original site was also borne by educational institutions and local society.[^2]

Therefore, the 921 should not be written as an ending where "Taiwan overcame the earthquake." A more accurate way to put it is: after a massive disaster, Taiwan left several sites that can be revisited repeatedly, and established some systems that still require continuous maintenance. When the next earthquake comes, the real test is not whether we remember 1999, but whether those memories have become checks, reinforcements, drills, and mutual care. This is also why the article cannot just write the 921 as "Taiwanese resilience." Resilience is not an innate character, nor is it a virtue that victims should automatically possess; it requires safety standards, accessible information, reliable water and transportation, a community that can be heard, and a system willing to admit shortcomings. Only when official reports, engineering data, media reviews, and civil reconstruction experiences are cross-referenced can readers distinguish what has been repaired and what is still a promise.[^1][^3][^5][^7]
