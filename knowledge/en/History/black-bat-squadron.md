> **30-Second Overview:**
> The Black Bat Squadron (Air Force 34th Squadron) was Taiwan's most mysterious special operations unit during the Cold War. Between 1952 and 1974, in coordination with the US CIA and under the cover of "Western Enterprises," they executed extremely dangerous low-altitude electronic reconnaissance missions. Flying at altitudes below 100 meters in the dead of night, members frequently penetrated PRC airspace, facing the constant threat of terrain collision and interception by Communist forces. Out of 838 missions, 1

11:00 PM on November 6, 1961, over Chenzituan, Dalian, Liaoning Province: a pitch-black P2V-7 reconnaissance aircraft, devoid of any serial numbers, flew low against the surface of the sea and hills. This aircraft belonged to the Republic of China Air Force 34th Squadron, known to the public as the "Black Bat Squadron." The thirteen crew members on board silently monitored electronic signals on their instrument panels, attempting to weave through the gaps in the PRC's radar network. However, this time they could not escape a dense curtain of anti-aircraft fire; the plane crashed in a violent explosion, killing everyone on board. [^1]

### The Art of "Low Altitude" on the Edge of Death

The core tension of the Black Bat Squadron lay in their execution of what was essentially "near-suicidal" intelligence gathering. To evade radar detection, pilots had to maintain ultra-low altitudes of 100 to 200 meters during pitch-black nights. [^2] At such a height, any minor navigational error or sudden hill would result in certain death.

"The Black Bat Squadron wasn't something just anyone could join; intelligence officers observed you covertly and selected you based on your flying skills and various academic criteria," recalled survivor He Zuo-ming (何祚明). [^3] Missions typically departed from Hsinchu Air Base, crossing the Taiwan Strait to enter Fujian or Zhejiang provinces, and even reaching as far as the Northeast and Northwest of China. The aircraft were equipped with precision electronic detection instruments provided by the CIA, with the goal of enticing PRC radars to activate so their frequencies and locations could be recorded.

📝 **Curator's Note:** The Black Bat insignia is deeply symbolic: the black wings represent night missions, the circular ears represent electronic reconnaissance, and the Big Dipper guides their direction through the dark.

### Western Enterprises: Shadow Trades Under the Cold War Shadow

The establishment of the Black Bat Squadron was inseparable from "Western Enterprises." On the surface, it was a private trading company, but in reality, it served as a CIA cover agency in Taiwan, with its office even located within a guesthouse on Dongda Road in Hsinchu. [^4] The United States provided aircraft, equipment, and funding, while Taiwan provided top-tier pilots and lives. This model of "US funding, Taiwan effort" turned Taiwan into a frontline intelligence hub of the Cold War and secured US military and economic aid for Taiwan.

However, this cooperation was fraught with inequality. To obtain electronic parameters, the US side would sometimes require aircraft to deliberately fly into the strongest zones of enemy air defenses. For the US, this was a data collection exercise; for the Taiwanese pilots, it was a high-stakes gamble with life and death. [^5]

### The "Missing" Who Remained Anonymous

For the families of the squadron members, this was a decades-long period of silent agony. Because missions were extremely classified, members could not disclose their destinations to their families before deployment. If a plane crashed, the notification received by families often contained only the word "missing," without even an official obituary. [^6]

Ms. Yang Zhao-sun (楊昭<0xE8><0x93><0x80>), the widow of Lieutenant Colonel Yin Jin-ding (尹金鼎), once received a washbasin made from the wreckage of a crashed aircraft. It was debris from the P2V-7 that crashed in Dalian in 1961, which local residents had collected and hammered into everyday household items. [^7] It was not until 1998 that she was finally able to visit the crash site in Dalian to search for her husband's final traces on that land. Many wives spent half a century guarding empty rooms without knowing if their husbands were alive or dead; this pain of "not seeing them alive, nor finding their bodies" remains one of the deepest scars left by the Cold War on Taiwanese families.

### A Delayed Homecoming and the Historical Jigsaw

During its twenty-plus years of operations, the Black Bat Squadron lost 15 aircraft and 148 personnel. [^8] It was only in the 1990s, as Cross-Struction relations eased, that the ashes of fallen members began to be brought back to Taiwan by their families. In 1992, the ashes of the thirteen heroes who died in Dalian in 1961 finally returned to Hsinchu to be interred in the Air Force Cemetery. [^9]

In 2009, the Hsinchu City Government established the "Black Bat Squadron Memorial Hall" on Dongda Road, where the families of the members once resided. The museum displays letters from the members, medals, and personal photographs, which reconstruct the daily lives of that era more effectively than any aircraft model could.

> "However, I believe that even if there are omissions in the military records, it will not erase the memory of every Black Bat member who once faced life and death to execute missions deep within China," wrote former member Zhu Zhen (朱震) in his oral history. [^10]

📝 **Curator's Note:** Listening to Andy Lau's song "Black Bat Squadron," one might feel that specific loneliness: "to fly over that land, yet unable to leave a name." This song is part of popular culture, but it also serves as a compensation for this silenced history.

The story of the Black Bat Squadron records the sacrifices made under Cold War geopolitics; it is a true record of a group of people in Taiwan's turbulent era who used extreme courage and endured solitude to hold up a protective umbrella for the nation in the dark of night. This courage should not vanish due to secrecy, nor be forgotten due to the changing of eras.
