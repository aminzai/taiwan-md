> **30-second overview:** In 1920, the Chianan Plain began constructing a water conservancy system that bound reservoir, canals, drainage, and crop rotation together; upon completion in 1930, it once supported approximately 150,000 hectares of farmland. Everyone knows the name Yoichi Hatta, but what truly keeps the Grand Canal alive day after day are the people who divide water, maintain canals, plant crops, and decide who gets water first during the dry season.[^1] [^2]

The images present only a few perspectives of the reservoir; they cannot stand in for the entire Chianan Irrigation System. Reservoir, intake, main canals, branch canals, drainage channels, and field zones must be viewed together; otherwise, a continuous water supply system is misread as a single scenic spot. The official tourism page lists Wushantou Reservoir as an attraction, but the cultural asset's value still lies in its connection to farmland, institutions, and local life.[^2] [^10]

## A Reservoir That Began with "Water Shortage"

On September 1, 1920, water conservancy works on the Chianan Plain pushed toward Wushantou. The problem there was concrete: rainfall was uneven, rivers flooded, and coastal soils contained salt. Before reliable irrigation appeared, farmers could only plant dryland crops near limited water sources. Historical data from the Irrigation Agency records the irrigated area at that time as approximately 5,000 hectares, far smaller than the later Chianan Irrigation scope.[^3]

The project's name comes from Chiayi and Tainan. It is not merely a single reservoir, but an entire water conservancy system: Wushantou Reservoir, Zhuoshui River intake, north and south main canals, Zhuo main canal, branch canals, and drainage facilities, interconnected into a network spanning the Yun-Chia-Nan Plain.[^3]

Looking back today, it is easy to compress this history into "Yoichi Hatta came to Taiwan and designed the Chianan Irrigation." Hatta was indeed the core engineer: he graduated from the Department of Civil Engineering at Tokyo Imperial University in 1910 and came to Taiwan, later taking charge of surveying the irrigation potential of the Chianan region. But the project also required technical personnel, laborers, local management, and a system capable of delivering water to the fields.[^4]

📝 **Curator's note: The protagonists of the Grand Canal are not just one name, but a community that must deliver water to the fields of strangers.**

## Sending Water Over Mountains, and Building Risk into the Project

The Wushantou Reservoir dam is 1,273 meters long, 56 meters high, with a capacity of approximately 150 million cubic meters. Construction ran from 1920 to 1930. Ministry of Culture materials specifically note that the semi-hydraulic fill method was used during construction, with one goal being to reduce siltation and damage to the surrounding environment.[^2]

This was not a project that could be completed by blueprints alone. Water had to enter the reservoir from the Zengwen River area, requiring diversion facilities that pierced through the mountain mass. After the reservoir was completed, water then had to travel along dense irrigation and drainage channels to reach farmlands far from the source. The English edition of _Taiwan Panorama_ records that the main works included Wushantou Reservoir and approximately 16,000 kilometers of irrigation and drainage channels, and that the reservoir itself took ten years to complete.[^5]

The project also had costs. Irrigation Agency articles record that the works required railways, engineering offices, soil laboratories, darkrooms, dormitories, schools, and hospitals as supporting facilities. They also note deaths during construction, and that the 1923 Great Kantō earthquake caused budget tightening and layoff pressures. These details remind us that so-called "modernization" was not a straight line of only achievements.[^4]

During layoffs, Yoichi Hatta chose to retain lower-level laborers who would have greater difficulty finding new work; this story later became one reason he is remembered. But it cannot obscure the larger issue: the water conservancy project was advanced under Japan's colonial rice policy, and irrigation, land, and rice and sugar production were tied to state governance from the start.[^4] [^5]

## Water Was Insufficient, So Not Every Field Could Grow Rice

After completion in 1930, the Chianan Irrigation did not turn all farmland into rice paddies simultaneously. Water volume was insufficient to support the entire irrigation district planting rice in the same year, so the Grand Canal adopted a three-year rotational irrigation and crop rotation: different zones planted rice, sugarcane, or mixed crops in different years. English-language materials explain that the approximately 150,000 hectares of the Chianan Plain were divided into three zones, so that rice production would not be concentrated only in the hands of the few farmers with water access.[^5]

This is an easily overlooked counter-intuitive point: **The Chianan Irrigation succeeded not because water was abundant, but because it acknowledged water was insufficient, and designed an order for allocating water.** The Irrigation Agency's official statement records the three-year crop rotation area as 150,000 hectares. The Chianan Management Office's organizational history records that after the integrated operation of Zengwen Reservoir and Wushantou Reservoir in 1974, farming methods changed from one crop in three years to two crops in three years.[^1] [^6]

Rotational irrigation is not an abstract system. It directly decides which year a household plants rice and which year other crops; it also determines how water conservancy personnel schedule water supply. This arrangement may increase overall fairness, yet it also makes farmers currently short of water feel restricted. Articles studying the Chianan Irrigation cultural landscape point out that the three-year rotational irrigation was itself once understood as government oppression of commoners, while simultaneously being viewed as a method for pursuing fairness under water scarcity.[^7]

📝 **Curator's note: Crop rotation is not fine print on an engineering table; it enters household income, crop choices, and the rhythm of an entire year.**

## After the Grand Canal Was Completed, the Hardest Work Had Just Begun

The scale of the water conservancy system has different calibers in different official sources. The Ministry of Culture's English case page describes the current system as "approximately 10,000 kilometers of waterways"; the Tainan City Government's 2021 centennial commemorative piece records waterways extending over 16,000 kilometers; AgriHarvest further breaks down the main, branch, and sub-canals from Yunlin to Tainan as approximately 1,410 kilometers, with nearly 7,400 kilometers of minor irrigation channels. These figures do not necessarily contradict each other; they may reflect different calculation scopes; therefore this article does not write any single number as the sole answer.[^2] [^8] [^9]

AgriHarvest once followed the annual water supply rhythm of the Chianan Irrigation Station. January to May, the first rice crop needs water; after the June harvest, water conservancy personnel race against time to repair channels; plum rains and typhoons replenish the reservoir; the second half of the year then supplies the second rice crop and mixed crops depending on water volume. Every season contains estimation, allocation, inspection, and adjustment.[^9]

Longtian Work Station Chief Chen Yen-hsing says: "Everyone thinks we at Longtian have lots of water, nothing to worry about — not true!"[^9] This sentence pulls the Grand Canal back from tourist attraction to worksite. On the map the reservoir is a blue area; come irrigation season, it becomes a deposit that may be insufficient: last year's rain was low, so this year's rice planting must queue up again.

When water is short, water conservancy personnel face not only calculation problems, but also farmer dissatisfaction and the risk of sluice gates being damaged. In AgriHarvest's reporting, managers describe midnight patrols and work preventing water theft. Here, fairness is not a slogan, but delivering every drop of water to the correct field.[^9]

## A Cultural Landscape Still in Operation

In 2009, Wushantou Reservoir and the Chianan Irrigation Water Conservancy System were registered as a cultural landscape, and listed as a potential World Heritage site for Taiwan. The Ministry of Culture points out that the value of this system lies not only in engineering technology, but in its long-term linking of land, agriculture, and human life.[^2]

But "preserving" a water conservancy system that still supplies water cannot mean stopping time at the Yoichi Hatta era. StoryStudio interviewed cultural asset worker Wang Chun-hsi and water conservancy historian Chen Hung-tu, raising a sharp question: what the Grand Canal truly should preserve — the water canals themselves, or the fact that they still possess function? Chen Hung-tu's answer: "The greatest value of the Grand Canal as a cultural asset is the fact that it is 'still operating now.'"[^7]

This question becomes more urgent after industrial transformation. Agricultural water must compete with domestic, industrial, and tech-sector water. Reservoirs and channels also face aging, maintenance, and extreme climate. StoryStudio's reporting describes the Chianan Irrigation as an ongoing water resource allocation problem, not a finished historical chapter.[^7]

The Chianan Management Office's official organizational history also shows this system did not stop in 1930: 1974 integrated operation of the two reservoirs, 1975 management temporarily taken over by government, 2002 restoration of directly elected management organizations, 2020 reorganization into the Irrigation Agency Chianan Management Office, 2023 renamed with the establishment of the Ministry of Agriculture. The water conservancy facilities remain; the people and institutions managing them continue to change.[^6]

📝 **Curator's note: The hardest part of a cultural landscape to preserve is often not the stone, but the daily function that still allocates benefits and responsibilities.**

## From Reservoir to Field: How Engineering Becomes a Calendar

The spatial scale of the Chianan Irrigation is also a temporal scale. Reservoir water storage is not the endpoint; supply personnel must turn water into an executable schedule: when to open water, which main canal gets priority, which field zones need crop adjustments, when to stop water for channel construction. The annual calendar compiled by AgriHarvest makes this "invisible engineering" concrete: first rice crop concentrated from late winter to spring, summer is the boundary between harvest and maintenance, plum rains and typhoons determine whether the second half of the year has sufficient water volume.[^9]

This calendar also explains why the Chianan Irrigation cannot be represented by reservoir photos alone. The reservoir is the visual center of water storage; what truly converts water into agricultural production are the channels and management personnel scattered across the plain. When water volume decreases, the supply schedule becomes a fallow schedule. When rainfall increases, the supply schedule must be readjusted again. The "present tense" of the engineering is therefore not fixed; it must be recalculated every year based on rain, crops, and institutions.

For farmers, the rotational irrigation system is not merely an administrative table. It changes when to buy seeds, when to hire labor, when to harvest, and it places neighborly relations into the water allocation order. People whose water flows through the same canal do not necessarily have the same land conditions. Therefore, so-called fairness is not just equal distribution, but continuous coordination among different crops, different locations, and different seasons.[^7] [^9]

## Don't Rush to Write the Grand Canal as One Person's Work

Yoichi Hatta's bronze statue squats beside Wushantou Reservoir; this posture easily makes one imagine the project as one person's long examination: an engineer gazes at wasteland, proposes a plan, and finally lets water flow into fields. Irrigation Agency materials indeed record Hatta's design and management philosophy, and also record his arrangements for worker and family dormitories, schools, hospitals, and public facilities.[^4]

However, the worksite had another kind of time less easily commemorated. Workers transported materials between reservoir, tunnels, and channels; water stations had to complete a section of maintenance before Lunar New Year; water gatekeepers patrolled sluice gates at night; farmers changed transplanting and harvest according to the rotation table. These actions have no bronze statue, yet they are necessary conditions for the Grand Canal to operate.[^4] [^9]

Irrigation Agency local materials also preserve memories of "corvée labor": in certain project phases, local residents contributed labor by household, excavating and building embankments by hand. Viewing such local material narratives alongside official engineering history makes the word "construction" heavier: it is both technology, and encompasses who contributed labor, who benefited, and who was required to cooperate.[^1]

So, Yoichi Hatta can be the entrance, but not the terminus. Stopping the story at his biography misses the most Taiwan-specific part of the Chianan Irrigation: water conservancy facilities must be embedded in local society to turn from engineering drawings into an order operated by people every day.

## Three Gazes: Water Supply, Tourism, and Cultural Preservation

Viewing Wushantou Reservoir today, at least three gazes coexist. The first is the tourist's gaze: looking at islands, water surface, dam body, and park scenery. The second is the engineering management gaze: looking at water level, outlet, channel safety, and maintenance cycles. The third is the cultural asset gaze: looking at a social technology formed in the colonial period, adjusted postwar, and still functional today. All three are real, but none alone can tell the whole Chianan Irrigation.[^2] [^7] [^10]

Placing tourism photos in the article is not to beautify the Grand Canal into a scenic postcard, but to let readers first see the shape of water, then ask how water is used. The same lake surface: to tourists it is scenery, to water conservancy personnel it is storage volume, to farmers it may be whether next season's rice can be sown. These different meanings layered on the same water surface are precisely why a cultural landscape is harder to explain than a single monument.

## Water Flows to Today; the Story Has Not Ended

In 2025, the Tainan City Cultural Assets Management Office brought Wushantou Reservoir and the Chianan Irrigation to Tokyo's "Doboku Collection" exhibition, introducing this century-old water vein as a cultural asset. The official article positions it as a representative work of modern water conservancy engineering, and emphasizes that it links land, agriculture, and life.[^10]

Such international display is meaningful, but the Chianan Irrigation's value does not need World Heritage List approval to be established. It more needs to be placed back on the plain: who manages the sluice gates? Is there rain this year? Which zone needs fallow? Are the farmlands still there? If a water canal leaves only photos in a memorial park, it leaves only a past to be looked at.

Yoichi Hatta deserves to be in the story, because he left design, organization, and engineering judgment. Taiwanese technical personnel, laborers, water station staff, and farmers equally deserve to be seen, because they turned design into usable water. More importantly, the Chianan Irrigation's history should not only answer "who built it," but also "how was water allocated, and who bore the cost of allocation."

## Understanding the Scale of a Water Canal

When people discuss the Chianan Irrigation, they often first mention "third in Asia" or "rare in the world," but rankings change with era, engineering classification, and measurement calibers. Compared to stuffing it into a ranking table, a more reliable question is: does this waterway leave traceable function? Does it still send water to farmland? Can the management system explain where water comes from and where it flows to?

This is also why this article lists different sources' kilometer figures side by side, rather than picking one seemingly prettiest number. The Ministry of Culture's 10,000 kilometers, the Tainan City Government's 16,000 kilometers, and AgriHarvest's breakdown of main/branch canals and minor irrigation channels each describe different system layers. The differences between numbers are not errors themselves; the real error is treating different denominators as the same thing.[^2] [^8] [^9]

The same caution should apply to terms like "fertile fields." The Chianan Irrigation indeed changed the Chianan Plain's agricultural production, but it did not suddenly turn a blank land into civilization. Before the project, the plain already had people farming, storing water, building ponds, and adapting to wet and dry seasons. Irrigation Agency Taiwan irrigation history materials show that Taiwan's water conservancy development preceded the Chianan Irrigation by hundreds of years, and local water conservancy facilities were once crowdfunded and managed by civilians.[^3] [^13]

The Chianan Irrigation's importance lies precisely in its linking of existing local water conservancy experience with modern state engineering, machinery, surveying, and administrative organization. This articulation brought enormous productive capacity, and also larger-scale resource allocation responsibility. When we call it a symbol of "modernization," we must simultaneously remember it is a modernization continuously maintained by people and institutions.

International display can let more people see water conservancy engineering, but the true continuation still occurs in the plain's irrigation calendar, field sluice gates, and farmers' choices. If the next generation only remembers one engineer, but does not know how water enters a bowl of rice, this cultural landscape leaves only a name. If they can see the relationship between waterway, land, and food, the Grand Canal will re-become part of life.[^7] [^10]

For city dwellers, this question may first appear as a bowl of rice, a water shortage alert, or a fallow news item. Tracing it to the waterway source reveals the ever-negotiating agricultural infrastructure behind daily life.

The Chianan Irrigation's truly enduring place is not in how high a dam is, but in that it still forces a society to face the same question: when water is insufficient, how do we live together?

This question still has the sound of water today. Readers walking along the waterway can still encounter it.
