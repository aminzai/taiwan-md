> **30 Second Summary:** In 1931, the Chiayi Agricultural School baseball team represented Taiwan at Koshien in Japan, becoming the first Taiwanese team to reach the summer tournament finals and ultimately winning second place. The team included Han Chinese, Indigenous peoples, and Japanese students. This is not just a story of "underdog comeback," but also how students under colonial rule were organized on the same field, making the entire island feel invested in the outcome.[^1] [^2] [^3]

On July 23, 1931, approximately 15,000 spectators filled the Yuanshan Stadium in Taipei. Chiayi Agricultural School defeated Taipei Commercial 11 to 10 in the Taiwanese representative finals, earning a ticket to Koshien in Japan. On the same day, radio broadcasts sent the game's sounds across the island. On August 21, they were defeated by Aichi Commercial in the Koshien finals, finishing as runners-up. This team, which did not win the championship, became known thereafter as "The Glory of Jia-Nong."[^1] [^2]

This contrast must be addressed first: Jia-Nong's most significant game was not a victory, but a near miss—second place. It brought Taiwan onto the national stage of the Japanese Empire and compelled later generations to ask whether what was seen then was Taiwan, Chiayi, or simply a school team that happened to play well.

## A School from Agriculture Found a Common Language on Red Soil

Chiayi Agricultural School was founded in 1919 with the mission of cultivating agricultural talent. The baseball club was established in 1928. Baseball was originally a recreational sport for Japanese people within the colony, and only after 1919 was it incorporated into school physical education, giving Taiwanese students gradual opportunities to engage with the sport. In 1920, the Taiwan Sports Association was founded, leading to more organized local competitions. Based on oral history interviews, baseball's appearance in Taiwan is closely linked to Japanese colonial rule. It first entered schools and the Japanese community, then was used by Taiwanese students for competition and practice, eventually evolving into a sport that no longer belonged solely to the colonizers.[^2] [^3] [^9]

What made Jia-Nong special was not that it "naturally" brought together three ethnic groups. The composition of Han Chinese, Indigenous peoples, and Japanese people mentioned in official exhibitions occurred within the school and competition framework provided by the Japanese colonial system. The players still lived within different social orders. While the field allowed them to be on the same team, it did not eliminate the power disparity inherent in colonialism.[^3] [^4]

This is also where the "republic of three races" narrative is most easily flattened. It can be a beautiful name given by later generations for cooperation, but it cannot be retroactively interpreted as a society that was already equal at the time. The significance of Jia-Nong's story lies in the coexistence of brief joint struggle and systemic inequality outside the field.

The coach of Jia-Nong, Kondō Heitaro (近藤兵太郎), was originally a Koshien coach from Matsuyama Commercial School in Japan. The Chiayi City Archives record that players recalled his training as almost Spartan: daily repetitive practice, polishing basic movements until flawless, and then finding each player's talent. Kondō did not hand down a set of "passionate" slogans to the students; instead, he transformed a team that rarely won into one capable of surviving in island-wide competitions through extremely specific training.[^2] [^5]

> **Curator's Note:** The Jia-Nong spirit is not about "success through sheer effort." It is more like a question: when the rules are not written by you, can you still develop your own style within those rules?

## Wu Ming-jie’s Hands First Secured Taiwan Representation for Jia-Nong

In July 1931, Jia-Nong held its selection games at Yuanshan Stadium in Taipei. Pitcher Wu Ming-jie (吳明捷) threw a complete game against Taichung First Middle School, which official records mark as the first complete game in Taiwanese baseball history. On July 23, Jia-Nong defeated Taipei Commercial 11 to 10 to secure the right to represent Taiwan.[^1] [^2]

That final game was not a clean line of victory. Jia-Nong had to rely on defense, speed, and incremental scoring to claim the last spot against Bei-Shang (Taipei Commercial). Photos preserved by Chiayi University show Luo Bao-nong (羅保農) scoring at home plate, Wu Ming-jie receiving an award, and players returning to Chiayi to pay respects at Jia-Nong Shrine. The photos pulled "representing Taiwan" from an abstract title back into a group of people in uniforms.[^1]

After securing representation, Jia-Nong traveled from Keelung to Japan aboard the Kansen Maru on August 5. One aspect of this journey that is easily overlooked by film narratives is that the players did not depart from an abstract "Taiwan," but sailed from Keelung Port, carrying the expectations of their school, families, and the local society of Chiayi, toward a field defined by Japanese national competition rules.[^1] [^5]

The Koshien tournament opened on August 13, and Jia-Nong subsequently defeated Kanagawa Commercial, Sapporo Commercial, and Ogura Industrial before reaching the finals on August 21. School history photos from National Chiayi University record their procession before the final and their receiving of the runner-up trophy.[^1] [^4]

In the finals, they lost to Aichi Commercial and did not bring the championship banner back to Taiwan. However, "runner-up" was not a consolation prize at the time: this was the first Taiwanese team to reach the Koshien summer tournament finals, and it was the last game played by students from the colony on the Japanese national stage. Indigenous documentation notes that four of Jia-Nong's nine starters were from Taitung, and Luo Bao-nong was a key batter with a batting average of .333 in that series.[^4]

## Koshien Was Not a Neutral Field

Writing Jia-Nong as "Taiwanese defeating the Japanese" is dramatic but inaccurate. Jia-Nong faced Japanese schools in both the Taiwanese qualifiers and at Koshien, and it was named, trained, and represented within the colonial system. The team's Japanese students, coach, and Taiwanese students cannot simply be placed into two opposing camps.[^3] [^5]

Similarly, writing it as "the colonial government successfully fostered tripartite integration" is incomplete. Research in the paper _KANO_ points out that the film made Jia-Nong a subject of postcolonial identity discourse. The cooperative value in the film both provided an imagination of Taiwanese subjectivity and required confronting how to reorder the Japanese colonial experience.[^6]

This contradiction does not need to be smoothed over. Jia-Nong's second place can simultaneously be the result of colonial rule, the effort of Taiwanese students, the moment Indigenous players were conscripted and seen, and the starting point for later generations of Taiwanese people reappropriating an external system into collective memory. History is not a binary choice between "glory" and "oppression." It often ties two things to the same ball.

## After the Team Left, the Story Remained in Taitung and Chiayi

Jia-Nong's influence did not stop when the final whistle blew in 1931. Records from Chiayi City show Chen Gengyuan (陳耕元) returning to Taitung to teach children baseball, and Yang Yuanxiong (楊元雄) also having trained the Hongye Little League team. This line connecting Jia-Nong players and local coaches ensured that the "Jia-Nong spirit" was not just a movie slogan but continued through human teaching.[^2]

This thread also connected Chiayi and Taitung. The Jia-Nong players did not just leave their names in their hometowns; they brought training methods, game memories, and an understanding of the ball to the next generation of children. Later little league teams did not have to replicate 1931 but could borrow an imagination of "local participation" from that team.[^2] [^4]

After Jia-Nong, baseball in Taiwan often played two roles simultaneously: it could be the most ordinary practice at school, or it could be packaged by the government as national glory on the international stage. The National Archives Exhibition places Hongye Little League, Jinlong Little League, and professional baseball on a historical line from "amateur ball" to "professional ball," reminding us that the team's outcome never belonged only to the players; it was also used by different eras to explain "who we are."[^3]

It is not a legacy exclusive to Jia-Nong. Indigenous documentation places the Nengao Team (能高團) from the 1920s before Jia-Nong: this team, composed of Indigenous players, traveled to Japan in 1925, and Luo Daohou (羅道厚) later became the first Taiwanese player to join professional baseball in Japan. Kondō Heitaro sought players in the East along this earlier existing path of Indigenous baseball to form Jia-Nong.[^4]

Therefore, Jia-Nong should not be told as a story where Japanese coaches "discovered" Taiwanese talent. A more accurate way to put it is that different regions and ethnic groups in Taiwan were already playing ball; Jia-Nong brought these dispersed paths into one school team, and Kondō's training propelled them onto Koshien. Who was seen depended on who had the chance to enter that field. Who was remembered also depended on whether later generations chose to write their names back.[^4]

## In 2014, Film Brought the Runner-up Back to the Streets

In 2014, the film _KANO_, directed by Ma Zhixiang (馬志翔) and produced by Wei Te-sheng (魏德聖), was released. The film moved Jia-Nong from alumni associations, local museums, and baseball archives onto the screen of popular culture. With box office revenue exceeding NT$300 million in seven weeks, a statue of Wu Ming-jie was installed at the Yuanshan Fountain in Chiayi City during related premiere events.[^2] [^5] [^6]

The statue is an interesting turning point. In photos from 1931, Wu Ming-jie was the captain and ace pitcher who received awards. In 2014, he became a figure passing by daily near the city's fountain. History was no longer confined to the school archives but placed on the daily traffic routes of citizens.[^1] [^2]

This preservation also carries risks. When a person is turned into a statue, their complex identity can easily be reduced to four words: "never give up." When a mixed-race team is made into an inspirational movie, the specific experiences of colonialism and Indigenous players may recede into the background. Taiwan Insight's analysis reflects this tension, both affirming the film's power to recall baseball and Taiwanese identity, and organizing critics' concerns about the beautification of colonial modernity.[^6]

The film brought Jia-Nong into the memory of a new generation of viewers, but it also changed how people imagined it. The real 1931 did not have every character's dialogue in the movie, nor a clean arc that could organize the colonial experience into "never give up." Researchers therefore remind us that when viewing _KANO_, one must maintain a postcolonial distance: respecting its power to recall memory while discerning how it chooses, compresses, and reorders history.[^6]

The film's depiction of Jia-Nong does not mean that 1931 finally found the only correct version. On the contrary, the film gave more people the opportunity to revisit names that were originally preserved only in alumni photos, local memories, and baseball archives: Wu Ming-jie, Chen Gengyuan, Tuo Hongshan (拓弘山), Luo Bao-nong, Lan Dehe (藍德和), and the other players who were not centered on posters.[^1] [^4]

> **Curator's Note:** A film made Jia-Nong visible again, but true preservation is not turning players into myths; it is allowing their identities, eras, and differences to remain together.

## A Memento Ball Brings 1931 Back to Chiayi

Jia-Nong's history does not exist only in films and texts. For the 100th anniversary of Koshien Stadium in 2024, the Hanshin Koshien Museum held a special exhibition on Taiwan and Chiayi Agricultural School, displaying the "Memento Ball from Jia-Nong to Kanagawa Commercial." In 2026, when the president of Hankyu Railway visited Chiayi, the Chiayi City Government placed this ball into the narrative of urban exchange.[^7]

The weight of a single ball is small, yet it layers several places: the school in Chiayi, the stadium in Japan, the opponent in Kanagawa, and two cities exchanging artifacts and stories a hundred years later. It is more complicated than the four words "The Glory of Jia-Nong," and closer to history, because the ball holds specific opponents, not abstract glory.[^3] [^7]

The National Archives also preserved an official document from 1937 regarding subsidies for repairs at Chiayi City Baseball Field, amounting to 1,752 yen. The field was inaugurated in 1918 and later used by the Chinese Professional Baseball League (CPBL). This document allowed Jia-Nong's story to move beyond "that Koshien game" and return to how cities build fields, repair them, and allow the next game to continue.[^3]

Thus, Jia-Nong's core objects are not just a runner-up trophy. The ticket, photos, memento ball, pitcher statue, and a repaired stadium each preserve different versions of memory. Only by putting them together can we see how Jia-Nong transformed from a game into the public history of Chiayi.

## Why Runner-up Endures More in Taiwan Than Champion

Perhaps what makes Jia-Nong most compelling is that it did not achieve a perfect ending. It did not win the final game at Koshien, nor did it turn colonial limitations into a miracle overnight, nor did "tripartite integration" automatically equal equality. It simply sent a school team from Chiayi to the last game of the national tournament in the summer of 1931. This was not Jia-Nong's only journey: research data shows that Jia-Nong represented Taiwan four times at Koshien in 1931, 1933, 1935, and 1936. The runner-up status of 1931 is the most legendary, but subsequent appearances remind us that Jia-Nong was not a summer story that accidentally stumbled into history, but an institution that continuously treated baseball as part of school life.[^8]

But that "near miss" was enough to change the imagination of an island. Baseball originally entered Taiwan with the Japanese colonial system, yet it was later used by Taiwanese people to record their speed, bodies, localities, and names. After Jia-Nong, Hongye Little League, Jinlong Little League, professional baseball, and overseas players continuously pushed this sport onto new international stages. The National Archives Exhibition calls this line "from amateur ball to professional ball," which truly explains not just a change in name, but a change in the people who used the sport.[^3]

So, Jia-Nong is not merely Taiwan's first "success story" in baseball. It is more like a cracked mirror: on one side reflecting the field arranged by the colonial empire, and on the other reflecting the path run by Taiwanese students there. What readers remember may not be 11 to 10, 19 to 7, or the runner-up trophy, but that harder question—if a stadium was not built for you, how do you bring it back to your city after leaving? The 2026 visit by the Hankyu Railway president to Chiayi and his inspection of Jia-Nong artifacts demonstrates this nearly century-long connection: Koshien is no longer just a distant field from 1931, but a local memory that Chiayi continuously uses to narrate itself.[^7]
