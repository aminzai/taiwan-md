> **30-Second Overview:** The connection between Taiwan and Disney is far deeper and more legendary than many imagine. From the golden age of early animation outsourcing to two missed opportunities with Disneyland, even the fact that _Toy Story 3_ once planned Taiwan as a setting, and the localized image of Stitch riding a scooter—these stories not only witness Taiwan's unique role on the international stage but also showcase a fantastic chapter where Taiwanese culture intertwines with Disney magic.

The connection between Taiwan and Disney is far deeper and more legendary than many imagine. This relationship extends beyond the golden age of animation outsourcing to include IP localization, the export of voice-acting culture, explorations of business models, and even political campaign promises, weaving together a fantastic journey filled with both wonder and regret.

## Taiwan Was Once the "Disney of the East": The Driving Force Behind Classic Animation

From the 1980s to the 1990s, Taiwan was hailed as the "Disney of the East," and this was no empty title. Located in the Xindian District of New Taipei City, "Hung Kwang Cartoon" was the world's largest animation outsourcing factory at the time, handling nearly one-third of the global animation production volume [^1]. During this golden era, Taiwanese animators used their exquisite skills to breathe life into countless Disney classics.

### The Taiwanese Imprint on the Disney Renaissance

Many classic feature films from the Disney Renaissance, such as _The Little Mermaid_, _Aladdin_, _The Lion King_, and even _Mulan_, had their hand-painted celluloid frames meticulously completed by Taiwanese animators, stroke by stroke [^1] [^7]. This was not only a point of pride for Taiwan's animation industry but also allowed Taiwan to leave an indelible mark on global animation history.

> 📝 Curator's Note: When we marvel at the grand scenes in _The Lion King_ in front of our screens, few know that these images actually emerged from the tips of countless Taiwanese animators working day and night in a factory in Xindian. This is a form of invisible cultural export and the best proof of Taiwan's soft power.

### The Taiwanese Soul of _Mulan_

It is worth mentioning that Chang Chen-yi, a primary character designer for the Disney animated film _Mulan_, was a native-born Taiwanese animator. He skillfully integrated Eastern aesthetics into the framework of Disney animation, ensuring that _Mulan_ not only presented a unique Eastern charm visually but also allowed global audiences to appreciate the charm of Chinese culture through a Disney lens [^1] [^9].

## _Tron_: A Traditional Chinese Surprise in the Credits

In 1982, Disney released _Tron_, the first science fiction film in cinema history to heavily utilize computer special effects. During the latter half of this film, much of the light-and-shadow tracing and manual cel coloring work was completed through the hard work of Taiwanese animators from Hung Kwang in Xindian [^1] [^11].

To honor these behind-the-scenes heroes, Disney made an exception by printing the actual names of the Taiwanese animators in "Traditional Chinese" directly within the Hollywood film's end credits. This was extremely rare in the international film industry at the time, serving not only as an affirmation of the professionalism of Taiwanese animators but also becoming a celebrated anecdote in the history of cooperation between Taiwan and Hollywood [^1] [^11].

## Twice Missing the Chance for "Disneyland"

It is not an urban legend that Taiwan twice missed the opportunity to host a Disneyland; it is a historical fact [^2] [^3] and [^4] [^5].

### The First Time: The 1970s Battle for Tokyo and Yen Chang-shou's Efforts

In the 1970s, when Disney was planning its first overseas theme park in Asia, both Taipei and Tokyo were on the final evaluation list. Mr. Yen Chang-shou (嚴長壽), then General Manager of American Express, actively analyzed Taiwan's advantages to Disney, such as lower land prices compared to Japan and a superior geographical location [^5] [^20]. However, due to Taiwan's foreign exchange control policies at the time and undeveloped transportation infrastructure, Tokyo, Japan, ultimately emerged as the winner and became the site of Asia's first Disneyland [^5] [^21].

### The Second Time: The Yue Mei Regret of the 1990s

In the 1990s, as Taiwan's economy took off, the government actively sought to bring Disney to the "Yue Mei Large-scale Recreation Area" (the predecessor to today's Lihpao Resort) in Houli, Taichung. At the time, Disney proposed extremely strict conditions, including rights to surrounding land development, a dedicated connection to the High Speed Rail (HSR), and high equity stakes. However, against the backdrop of Taiwan's relatively conservative regulations and systems, the government was unable to fully meet these conditions. Coupled with Disney's assessment that Taiwan's local tourism population was insufficient, this collaboration also fell through [^2] [^3] [^4] [^5].

> 📝 Curator's Note: Missing out on Disneyland twice may seem like a regret, but it also reflects the political and economic realities of Taiwan during different eras. From foreign exchange controls to regulatory restrictions, these obstacles witnessed the growing pains of Taiwan's social transformation and prompted us to reflect on the balance between large-scale international investment and local development.

## _Toy Story 3_ Almost Set in Taiwan: Buzz Lightyear's Recall Journey

A little-known fact is that in the original script concept for the Pixar animated film _Toy Story 3_, Taiwan was planned as a major setting for the story. In early versions developed by Disney's Circle 7 Animation studio between 2004 and 2006, the plot involved Buzz Lightyear malfunctioning and needing to be sent back to his "place of origin," Taiwan, for repairs [^12] [^13] [^14].

To rescue the recalled Buzz Lightyear, Woody and his fellow toys would have traveled across the ocean to Taiwan to embark on a perilous rescue mission. Although this script was ultimately not adopted due to the restarting of the partnership between Pixar and Disney, this concept unexpectedly revealed Taiwan's important position in the global manufacturing supply chain and its unique potential role in popular culture [^12] [^13] [^15].

## The "Kaohsiung Disney" Campaign: From Hope to Reality Gap

In recent years, local leaders in Taiwan have also proposed campaign promises to bring Disneyland to the region. The most prominent was the "Kaohsiung Disney" concept proposed by former Kaohsiung Mayor Han Kuo-yu during his 2018 campaign. At the time, he promised that if elected, he would actively strive to bring Disneyland to Kaohsiung to drive the development of the tourism industry [^16] [^17].

However, this policy encountered numerous difficulties in actual implementation. In May 2020, Han Kuo-yu admitted that the plan to bring in Disneyland faced "difficulties" and expressed a willingness to withdraw the campaign promise. He explained that the scale of investment for a Disneyland is massive and carries extremely high requirements for surrounding land development and transportation infrastructure, which Kaohsiung's conditions at the time could not fully meet [^16] [^17] [^18] [^19]. This episode once again highlighted the challenges faced by large-scale international theme parks settling in Taiwan, involving not just capital and land, but also complex regulations, infrastructure, and market assessments.

## Long-term Management of Localized Disney IPs and Merchandising

Even without a physical theme park, Disney's management of its IP economy in Taiwan is long-term and profound. Disney maintains dedicated consumer licensing operations in Taiwan, covering diverse categories such as toys, apparel, stationery, food, and beauty products, working long-term with Taiwanese manufacturers to integrate Disney magic into the daily lives of the Taiwanese people [^22].

### Stitch's Imprint of Taiwanese Scooter Culture

One particularly special case is the localized image of Stitch. In Taiwan, licensed merchandise appeared featuring Stitch riding a scooter and wearing a helmet, combining this cute character from outer space with Taiwan's unique scooter culture [^23] [^24]. This type of localization later even became a reference model for Disney's IP adaptation in markets such as Southeast Asia and Latin America, demonstrating the influence of the Taiwanese market on Disney's IP strategy [^22].

### Marvel-Themed Gym: A World-First Cross-Industry Collaboration

In recent years, Disney has also actively engaged in innovative collaborations with local Taiwanese brands. For example, the local fitness brand "Fitness Factory" collaborated with Marvel in July 202able to create the world's first Marvel-themed fitness space [^25] [^26]. This collaboration integrates the heroic spirit of superheroes into a fitness setting, allowing people to experience the charm of the Marvel Universe while exercising, embodying Disney's IP economic strategy of "creating dreams even without a theme park."

## Cultural Contributions of Taiwanese Voice Actors and Dubbing

In addition to the early case of the Taiwanese-language version of _Snow White_, Taiwan's dubbing industry has long made significant contributions to the localization of Chinese content for Disney films and series. Much of the Chinese dubbing (especially the Traditional Chinese versions) for many Disney works is completed by Taiwanese voice actors, allowing Taiwanese audiences to enjoy Disney stories in their most familiar language [^27].

For instance, the well-known singer Yen Yi-ke (閻奕格) performed the Chinese theme song and participated in the dubbing for the live-action Disney film _The Little Mermaid_ [^28] [^29]. Furthermore, the "Lao Shu" (勞贖) meme widely circulated in online communities originated from the Taiwanese Chinese dubbing of the Disney animated film _Ratatouille_; it became an unexpected hit due to its unique pronunciation, becoming part of Taiwan's popular culture and indirectly proving the influence and local characteristics of Taiwanese dubbing [^30].

## The Continuity of Modern Events and Cultural Exchange

Beyond cruise ship visits, Disney's modern activities and cultural exchanges in Taiwan continue unabated. Examples include periodic pop-up stores, themed exhibitions (such as the _Alice in Wonderland_ interactive installation at Beixiang Lake Park), and the launch of Disney+ streaming services in Taiwan, all allowing Taiwanese audiences to access Disney content more instantaneously and diversely [^31]. These activities not only enrich Taiwan's cultural life but also ensure that Disney magic continues to exist in various forms within Taiwanese society.

## The Other Side of Business Models and Regret

As mentioned previously, Disney's theme park partnership models in Asia primarily fall into two categories: the Tokyo model (pure licensing) and the joint venture model (such as the 55/45 split in Hong Kong and Shanghai) [^5]. Had Taiwan successfully introduced a Disneyland back then, the government might have had to bear massive transnational profit and loss pressures. However, the success of Tokyo Disney also proves the feasibility of the pure licensing model, reflecting Disney's business consideration of adopting flexible strategies across different regions.

Taiwan has repeatedly missed opportunities with Disneyland due to factors such as regulations, land, and population; this history serves as a case study worth reflecting upon for Taiwan when developing its tourism industry and attracting large-scale international investment. This has also prompted Taiwan, in the development of its IP economy, to shift toward a strategy that focuses more on localized cooperation and diverse experiences.

## Conclusion

These trivia points regarding Disney and Taiwan not only witness the golden era when Taiwan used its superb "craftsmanship soft power" to support half of Hollywood, but also outline Taiwan's unique trajectory in global cultural exchange. From animation outsourcing to theme park evaluations, from the unfulfilled dream of _Toy Story 3_ to the localized image of Stitch, and from the deep cultivation of dubbing culture to the flourishing modern IP economy—the story of Taiwan and Disney is a fantastic journey filled with wonder and regret, yet deeply interconnected. Even without a physical theme park, Disney's magic continues to profoundly influence Taiwanese culture and life in various forms.
