> **30-second overview:** In 1982, Orchid Island began receiving low-level radioactive waste shipped from across Taiwan; by 1996 the storage site was full, yet there was no end in sight to its "temporary storage." The Tao people have demanded relocation since 1988, and a 2018 official truth investigation reopened the entire process. This article does not declare either side "safe" or "dangerous." Instead, it asks: if a homeland is chosen as a storage site without the residents' knowledge, can later monitoring data alone answer the question of justice?

## 1982: Yellow Drums Arrived Before the Truth

The Orchid Island low-level radioactive waste storage site was not a structure that appeared overnight. The Nuclear Safety Commission's English timeline page records that in 1972, the Atomic Energy Council convened scholars, nuclear research institutions, and Taipower to assess abandoned mines, highlands, uninhabited islands, and outer islands; at the time, "future ocean dumping" was still listed among the disposal technologies considered for low-level radioactive waste. In late 1975, the Executive Yuan approved the construction plan; work on the first phase began in 1978.[^1]

This history matters not to retroactively portray today's Orchid Island storage site as an engineering doomed from the start, but to remind us: it was born in an era of rapid nuclear expansion, when ocean dumping had not yet become taboo. Site selection criteria cited topographical isolation, sparse population, and convenient maritime transport. The very same criteria later became the starting point for Tao people questioning "who is being treated as someone who can be isolated."[^1]

In 1982, the storage site officially began receiving radioactive waste. Nuclear Safety Commission records show the site contained 23 storage trenches with a designed capacity of 133,728 drums. In July 1990, management responsibilities were transferred from the Atomic Energy Council to Taipower.[^2] Taipower's own feature article notes the site reached full capacity in 1996 and has not accepted new low-level radioactive waste since.[^3]

"No longer accepting new waste" is easily misheard as "the problem has ended." But a storage site's timeline does not stop on the day the last drum is hauled in. The drums remain on the island, the trenches still require maintenance, and land lease agreements, environmental monitoring, and the selection of a final disposal site all still require someone to take responsibility.

## What the Tao People See Is Not a Facility, but a Rewritten Homeland

To the Tao people, Orchid Island is not an empty plot of land measurable by population density. A Taiwan Insight research article points out that Tao livelihoods are closely intertwined with the sea, rivers, groundwater, farmland, and gathering activities. The risks brought by nuclear waste extend beyond dosage and regulations to include psychological and cultural impacts on traditional beliefs and physical well-being.[^4]

Public Television Service's "Our Island" report describes this lived scale more concretely: the sea is the father in a child's eyes, the taro fields are the mother's embrace. Taro is not merely a crop but part of daily diet and ceremonial life.[^5] This is not romanticizing the island but explaining why "whether the surrounding environment falls below standards" and "whether the homeland is suitable for storing nuclear waste" have never been the same question in Tao experience.

In February 1998, hundreds of Tao people living in Taipei gathered outside Taipower's headquarters demanding the removal of the storage site, with their protest framed as "expelling the evil spirit of Orchid Island." Taiwan Insight records that Taipower had pledged to remove the storage site by the end of 2002, but the failure to select a final disposal site caused the relocation to be delayed.[^4] Public Television Service documents that from 1988 to 1991, Orchid Island held consecutive annual "expelling the evil spirit" anti-nuclear activities on February 20.[^5]

Resistance did not begin from an abstract "anti-nuclear stance" but accumulated from experiences of being told, being kept in the dark, and being promised results that never came. Those who did not know their homeland would be filled with what it was later had to spend decades learning about radionuclides, storage trenches, and regulations in order to demand that the state make good on its promises.

## One Storage Site, Two Languages of "Safety"

The Nuclear Safety Commission's description of the storage site is highly specific: waste is first solidified, then placed in steel drums, which are then inserted into concrete trenches. The trenches are equipped with leachate collection systems, which are subsequently processed by a concentrator. The overseeing authority describes this design as a multi-barrier system and notes that since 1996 (Minguo 85), zero-emission measures have been implemented.[^6]

Taipower's published environmental monitoring page states that annual monitoring includes air, seawater, drinking water, river water, groundwater, agricultural and fishery products, soil, and coastal sand. Taipower's conclusion is that all yearly environmental samples fall within safe ranges, and that parallel monitoring also falls within background radiation fluctuations.[^7] These data are an indispensable half of understanding the official regulatory approach.

The other half lets readers see how the word "safety" is used within controversy. A 2012 Public Television Service report notes that between 1982 and 1996, Orchid Island received over 97,000 drums of nuclear waste stored in 23 open-air trenches. The report also noted that the sorting project discovered widespread corrosion and damage to the drums.[^5] The same report also presented differing interpretations by Taipower, the Atomic Energy Council, and scholars regarding cesium-137 and cobalt-60 sampling results and applicable standards. This is not treating an old report as the final arbiter but preserving the controversy in its original form: the same batch of environmental data may yield different public meanings depending on sampling location, comparison standards, and risk understanding.

A 2011 Taipei Times editorial also mentioned soil sampling, cesium-137, cobalt-60, and disputes over different standards, but it was commentary, not original research.[^8] Therefore, what can be stated with rigor is not "Orchid Island has been confirmed polluted" or "Orchid Island has no problems at all," but rather: relevant monitoring once sparked public controversy, and the authorities and critics disagreed on standards, sampling locations, and data interpretation. This way of writing is slower, but it is closer to the facts than pre-judging for the reader.

## 2018: The State Finally Asks "How Was This Decided?"

On August 1, 2016, the President spoke on behalf of the government to apologize to Taiwan's Indigenous peoples and pledged to investigate the decision-making process behind the storage of nuclear waste on Orchid Island. The Executive Yuan's Orchid Island Nuclear Waste Storage Site Truth Investigation Team later completed the "Report on the Truth Investigation of the Orchid Island Nuclear Waste Storage Site," published by the Council of Indigenous Peoples in September 2018, totaling 746 pages.[^9]

The importance of this report lies not only in its thickness but in its shifting the question from "what are today's monitoring values?" back to "what were the original site assessments, planning approvals, Indigenous consultations, land expropriation, and decision-making processes?" The book's table of contents lists these chapters, also indicating that the report was positioned as a reference for future reconciliation and equitable negotiations with Indigenous peoples.[^9]

Taiwan Insight's interpretation of the report points out that official documents do not prove that the placement decision obtained the Tao people's informed consent. The article also categorizes the controversy into three dimensions: distributive injustice, recognition injustice, and procedural exclusion.[^4] In other words, the truth investigation did not reduce nuclear waste to an object that could be handled solely through engineering means. It pulled a stretch of concrete trench back into the history of land, ethnicity, and state power.

## Compensation Can Be Calculated in Money, but Not Necessarily in Homeland

On November 29, 2019, Taiwan Insight records that Tao anti-nuclear activists rejected compensation in place of relocation outside the Executive Yuan. They called for the establishment of a communication platform, sought to bring nuclear waste disposal and compensation into legal frameworks, and demanded that funds be redirected toward relocation.[^4] This choice should not be interpreted as "the residents do not need compensation" but understood as: when the core of an issue is who gets to decide a homeland, money may not substitute for consent and relocation.

Here lies a familiar yet often fragmented contradiction in Taiwanese society: the state can use engineering language to explain how trenches prevent seepage, use monitoring data to explain how the environment falls below limits, and use feedback payments to explain what local communities have gained. But the question the residents might ask is another one entirely — why wasn't this decision something we could have participated in from the beginning?

Placed back into Taiwan's nuclear energy history, Orchid Island is not merely a geographical question of "where nuclear waste is stored." It reveals how energy interests connect to land governance and shows that Indigenous informed consent is not a procedural checkbox at the end of a policy brief but a prerequisite for deciding whether a place can be entrusted with the risks of the entire nation.

## The Yellow Drums Remain, and Time Has Not Closed the Case for Either Side

As of the Nuclear Safety Commission's English regulatory page updated in December 2024, the Orchid Island storage site is still listed as a low-level radioactive waste control target. The page also preserves the timeline of site selection in the 1970s, receipt of waste in 1982, and transfer to Taipower in 1990.[^2] Taipower, in its 2022 feature article, explains that the storage site is still managed by the nuclear backend unit and lists safety maintenance and ecological restoration as concurrent tasks.[^3]

These official pages do not provide a completed date for the relocation issue. They tell us how the facility operates, how it is monitored, and how it is maintained. The truth investigation report and the history of Tao resistance ask why, within the existing data, there is no evidence of obtaining Tao informed consent, and why later promises did not conclude in 2002.

The reason Orchid Island's yellow drums have become an object in Taiwan's history is not because they look particularly striking but because they compress over forty years of time into a single concrete place: one island, one group of residents, 23 trenches, and a future still not jointly agreed upon. A true ending will not be merely moving the drums from one coordinate on a map to another. It must also allow those who were previously excluded to become the people who decide the next coordinate.
