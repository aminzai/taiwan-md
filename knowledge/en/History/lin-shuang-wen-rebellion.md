> **30-Second Overview:** In late 1786, a local conflict in Daliyi, Changhua, escalated into one of the largest popular uprisings in Taiwan during the Qing period. The actions of Lin Shuangwen, Zhuang Datian, and Jinniang forced the Qing court to transfer troops across provinces and reorganize policies on crossing ports, garrison troops, and fortifications after the war. This article does not frame the event merely as "Qing troops quelling a rebellion," but instead asks how a war changed the way the empire governed Taiwan.

In late 1786, Lin Shuangwen of Daliyi, Changhua, was drawn into a local conflict that he may not have originally instigated. By January 1787, the conflict had captured the county seat of Changhua and shifted the Qing Empire’s military attention to the western half of Taiwan. [^1] In the spring of 1788, Lin Shuangwen was captured and transported to Beijing in a prisoner cart. [^2]

This history is most easily written as "Qing troops quelling a popular uprising," but what is truly worth asking is another matter: Why did an island, viewed by the empire as a frontier and long unwilling to invest significant governance costs, force the Qing court to cross the sea with troops for over a year, ultimately changing policies on ports, garrison troops, and city defenses? [^3]

> **Counter-Intuitive Core Sentence:** The outcome of the Lin Shuangwen Rebellion seemed to restore the status quo, but in reality, it made it impossible for the Qing Empire to govern Taiwan in the old way.

## From Banning Secret Societies to Local Loss of Control

Lin Shuangwen was originally from Pinghe County, Zhangzhou, Fujian. He crossed to Taiwan with his father in his youth and reclaimed land in Daliyi, Changhua. StoryStudio compiled his confessions after his arrest, noting that he joined the Tiandihui (Heaven and Earth Society) in the eighth month of the 51st year of the Qianlong Emperor’s reign, initially to look out for one another within local society. Later, as local authorities cracked down on secret societies, yamen runners took the opportunity to extort them, and the homes of Lin Shuangwen’s associates, such as Lin Pan, were burned by soldiers. The conflict shifted from association and crackdown to armed resistance against officials. [^4]

Here, one cannot simplify the "Tiandihui" into a rebel organization that suddenly appeared. Qing-era Taiwan was a frontier settlement society; the administrative capacity of local officials, ethnic relations, and land interests were intertwined. When public power expanded arrests under the guise of crackdowns, previously scattered local grievances found a common enemy. Research abstracts collected by the Airiti Library also list the government’s overly aggressive arrest methods as a key reason for the escalation of the event. [^5]

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> Lin Shuangwen was not at the center of the historical stage from the beginning. He was pushed to the forefront after local order failed. Putting this sequence back in place, the event is no longer a story of a heroic figure suddenly summoning the masses, but rather one where local conflicts first lost the institutions that could contain them, forcing individuals to become banners.

In the eleventh month of the 51st year of the Qianlong Emperor’s reign, Lin Shuangwen raised the flag to resist officials. In early 1787, the rebels captured Changhua and killed Sun Jingsui, the Prefect of Taiwan. Responders in the Northern Route then entered Zhuqian (modern Hsinchu), while the Southern Route saw Zhuang Datian gather followers, creating a separate front in the Fengshan area. The National Taiwan Museum of Literature’s historical theme exhibition connects these city assaults and responses, showing that the event was not a riot in a single village, but a rapidly connected regional war. [^1]

## Why a City Held Out for So Long

Lin Shuangwen’s forces besieged the city of Zhuluo (modern Chiayi) for a long time. English reports indicate that Zhuluo was besieged again for about six months after falling, and the relief army sent by the Qing court made limited progress, forced to shift to defense. This detail is important because it shows that the war did not end immediately upon the Qing army’s arrival in Taiwan; rather, local city defenses, food supplies, relief troops, and terrain jointly determined the course of the battle. [^3]

![The "Relief of Zhuluo" from the *Paintings of the Pacification of Taiwan*, depicting the battlefield and city terrain where Qing troops broke the siege. Image is a Wikimedia Commons public domain file, using direct hotlink.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/1787_to_1788_Lifting_the_Siege_at_Zhuluo.jpg)

Zhuluo was later renamed Jiayi, usually recorded as the result of the Qianlong Emperor rewarding the residents who defended the city. This place name is still in use today, but it also leaves another layer of questions: When a city is given a new name for assisting the official defense, how are the survival choices of local residents rewritten into the empire’s story of loyalty? [^4]

The Qing court dispatched naval and land admirals and the governor-general to cross the sea and supervise the army, yet still could not quickly end the war. It was only when Fu’kang’an led elite troops collected from across provinces to arrive in Taiwan that the situation gradually reversed. Different sources have inconsistent records of troop organization and numbers; therefore, this article does not treat any single number as the only answer, but only confirms the fact pointed to by all sources: The Qing court had to mobilize cross-provincial military forces and spend over a year to regain control of Taiwan. [^3] [^5]

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> The significance of Zhuluo lies not just in "holding out." It showed the empire that Taiwan’s cities, ports, and local society must be governed together, not just by a single ban. The subsequent policies on ports, garrison troops, and city defenses were the result of this city pushing the weight of local society back onto the empire.

## The Archives Contain More Than Just Lin Shuangwen

Research from the National Palace Museum shifts the gaze from leaders to military advisors, strategists, and grassroots figures. The article notes that Qing archives, besides recording the war, also preserve many people who played pivotal roles in the event. These figures may not remain in later textbooks, but they allow us to see how a popular uprising operated through intelligence, confessions, local networks, and military division of labor. [^6]

The most tense character among them is Jinniang, a Fan (indigenous) woman from Shangshuanshui She in Fengshan. Based on her confessions after being escorted, StoryStudio describes how she was invited by Zhuang Datian’s camp to be a female military advisor due to her practices of drawing talismans for healing and spirit worship. She was not a passive "indigenous female representative" appearing in the event, but entered the war with her own techniques, beliefs, and local relationships. [^4]

Jinniang’s story simultaneously reminds us that archives do not equal transparent truth. Her confessions were official texts formed after her arrest; the narrative contains self-justification and limitations left by the interrogation system. Being able to read her does not mean we have fully heard her. This is the distance that must be maintained when the Palace Museum research traces small figures from a large number of memorials and archives. [^6]

Research published by the Institute of Taiwan History, Academia Sinica, discusses how the Qing court produced war images through the _Paintings of the Pacification of Taiwan_. The research points out that these images were not merely battlefield records; they were also produced under the order of the Qianlong Emperor to propagate the Qing court’s military achievements. The indigenous people of Taiwan, cities, and terrain in the images were all placed within the perspective of imperial victory. [^7]

## How a Painting Turned War into Victory

![*Paintings of the Pacification of Taiwan (7): Capturing the Rebel Chief Lin Shuangwen*, 1793, painted by Yao Wenhuan et al., National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts. Image is a Wikimedia Commons public domain file, using direct hotlink.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Capture_of_the_rebel_chief_Lin_Shuangwen.jpg)

![The *Fangliao Battle* scene from the *Paintings of the Pacification of Taiwan*, arranging mountains, fortresses, and troops within a single field of view. Image is a Wikimedia Commons public domain file, using direct hotlink.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/1787_Battle_at_Fangliao.jpg)

The National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts, through Google Arts & Culture, states that the _Paintings of the Pacification of Taiwan_ were originally sketched by Fu’kang’an under the Emperor’s order, then handed over to court painters to redraw into twelve battle paintings, completed in 1793 and submitted for imperial review. This set of images arranged battlefield geography, architecture, plants, people, and troop dynamics within a single field of view, transforming the war into evidence of victory that the Emperor could observe. [^8]

The captured Lin Shuangwen was not the only protagonist. The larger picture consists of mountain ranges, ambushes, horses, flags, and the soldiers surrounding him. This composition places individual failure within the narrative of the restoration of imperial order, making "capture alive" the period at the end of the entire war. However, the more complete the image, the more one must ask what it did not paint: burned houses, city residents lacking food, forced participants in the societies, and those nameless dead. [^7] [^8]

The National Cultural Memory Bank’s _On the Remains of the Lin Shuangwen Event_ describes the event as a large-scale, widely influential anti-official uprising in Taiwan during the Qianlong era, also pointing out that excessive crackdowns by local officials were one factor in the escalation of the situation. Such local cultural data can supplement memories outside the battlefield, but terms like "revolution" or "anti-Qing" within them should still be cross-checked with Palace Museum, academic papers, and museum data, and cannot be taken directly as the sole explanation. [^9]

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> What the empire leaves behind the most is often how it describes victory. When reading Taiwan history, one must also shift the gaze to the people at the edges of the victory paintings. The war paintings fill the terrain and troop strength, but they do not leave equally clear positions for burned houses, food-starved city residents, and nameless dead.

## After the Event, Taiwan Was Reorganized

Lin Shuangwen’s capture did not mean governance returned to the state before 1786. Research collected by Airiti organizes the changes after the event into three items. The Qing court opened formal crossing ports in northern Taiwan, implemented the garrison troop system, and relaxed city fortification policies. These three measures respectively touched upon transportation, military mobilization, and city defense, showing that the Qing court began to view Taiwan as a strategic space requiring long-term management. [^5]

The Palace Museum article also points out that after the event, the Qing court readjusted crossing ports, the Fan (indigenous) boundary, and the Fan garrison system, and actively built cities. More notably, the event caused a massive increase in memorials, imperial edicts, and military archives concerning Taiwan. In other words, the Lin Shuangwen event not only changed Taiwan’s institutions, but also changed the density with which Taiwan was recorded by the empire. [^6]

These policies were not all stories of progress. The garrison troop system incorporated indigenous ethnic groups into military and governance arrangements, while simultaneously forming new classes and population pressures. Opening city fortifications allowed cities to be more defensible, but also indicated that Taiwan was placed under tighter military control. Policies can simultaneously bring security and constraint; they cannot be summarized by the single word "construction." [^5]

## Why It Is Still Worth Re-reading Today

The Lin Shuangwen event and the February 28 Incident, the White Terror, or democratization are not the same kind of historical experience; they should not be forced into a straight line for the sake of continuity. But it raises a question that repeatedly appears in Taiwan history: When central power treats local areas as frontiers that need to be managed, how does local society respond? And when the center is forced to increase governance capacity, who do the new institutions protect, and who do they restrict?

Lin Shuangwen himself was finally escorted to Beijing, while Jinniang and other prisoners left fragments in confessions and archives. The Qing court wrote this war as one of the Ten Great Campaigns; the local area left city walls, steles, and place names; later researchers retrieved those suppressed voices from the gaps in the archives. This is not simply opposing "official" and "folk," but acknowledging that the same event has multiple sets of memories coexisting. [^2] [^4]

Therefore, the significance of the Lin Shuangwen event lies not only in it being one of the largest popular uprisings of the Qing period. It is more like a stress test, revealing that the Qing Empire’s original governance methods were no longer sufficient for Taiwan. After the event was quelled, ports, garrison troops, city defenses, and archives all changed. The island did not thereby gain freedom, but was forced to become clearer within the empire’s calculations.
