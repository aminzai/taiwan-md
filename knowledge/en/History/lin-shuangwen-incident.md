> **30-second overview:** In late 1786, a local dispute at Dalihui in Changhua escalated into one of the largest rebellions in Qing-governed Taiwan. The actions of Lin Shuangwen, Zhuang Ditian, and the Indigenous woman Jinniang forced the Qing court to transfer troops across provinces and, after the fighting, to reorganize Taiwan's landing ports, the military settlement system, and city fortifications. This article does not reduce the event to a simple "army pacifies rebels" narrative, but instead asks how a war changed the empire's approach to governing Taiwan.

In late 1786, Lin Shuangwen of Dalihui in Changhua was drawn into a local conflict that was not necessarily initiated by him. By January 1787, the conflict had captured Changhua County City and drawn the Qing Empire's military attention to western Taiwan.[^1] In the spring of 1788, Lin Shuangwen was arrested and transported to Beijing by prison convoy.[^2]

This history is most easily written as "the army pacified the rebellion," but the more important question is this: why could a frontier island that the empire regarded as peripheral and long avoided investing heavily in governance, force the Qing court to transfer troops across the sea for more than a year, and ultimately change policies regarding ports, military settlements, and city defenses?[^3]

> **Counter-intuitive core sentence:** The outcome of the Lin Shuangwen rebellion appeared to restore the status quo, but in reality, the Qing Empire could never again govern Taiwan in the same way.

## From Suppressing Societies to Local Loss of Control

Lin Shuangwen was originally from Pinghe in Zhangzhou, Fujian, and moved to Taiwan with his father at an early age, settling in Dalihui in Changhua. StoryStudio compiled his confession after arrest, noting that in the eighth month of the 51st year of the Qianlong era (1786), he joined the Tiandi Society, originally for mutual support within local society. Later, local officials cracked down on the society, and the clerks took advantage of the situation to extort money. When Lin Shuangwen's companion Lin Pan's house was burned down by soldiers and officers, the conflict shifted from society organization and suppression to armed resistance against the government.[^4]

Here, the "Tiandi Society" should not be simplified into a suddenly emerging rebellious organization. In Qing-era Taiwan, society was a society of migration and settlement. The administrative capacity of local officials, ethnic relationships, and land interests were intertwined. When state power expanded its search and arrest under the guise of suppression, the originally scattered local grievances found a common enemy. The abstract summary collected by the Huajie Online Library also listed the government's overly aggressive arrest methods as a key reason for the escalation of the incident.[^5]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> Lin Shuangwen was not on the center stage of history from the beginning. He was pushed to the forefront after local order collapsed. Putting this sequence back in place, the incident is no longer a story of a hero suddenly rallying the masses, but rather a story of how local conflicts first lost the system that could contain them, and then people were forced to become symbols.

In the 11th month of the 51st year of the Qianlong era (1786), Lin Shuangwen raised his banner in resistance to the government. In early 1787, the rebels captured Changhua, killing Taiwan Prefect Sun Jinggui. Along the northern route, supporters attacked into Zhuqiao, while along the southern route, Zhuang Ditian gathered followers, and the battles around Fengshan formed another front. The National Taiwan Museum of Literature's historical theme exhibition connected these sieges and responses, showing that the incident was not a single village's uprising, but a rapidly connected regional war.[^1]

## Why Did a City Hold Out So Long

Lin Shuangwen's troops besieged Zhuoluo for a long time. English reports pointed out that after Zhuoluo fell, it was besieged for about six months, and the reinforcement troops sent by the Qing court made limited progress, eventually switching to defense. This detail is important because it shows that the war did not end immediately when the Qing army arrived in Taiwan, but was determined by the city's defenses, food supply, reinforcements, and terrain together.[^3]

![The map of lifting the siege at Zhuoluo in "The Campaign to Pacify Taiwan," depicting the battlefield and city terrain where the Qing army broke through the siege. Image is a public domain file from Wikimedia Commons, used directly from the hotlink.[^10]](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/1787_to_1788_Lifting_the_Siege_at_Zhuluo.jpg)

Zhuoluo was later renamed Jiayi, usually recorded as the result of the Qianlong Emperor's praise for the city's residents who defended the city. The place name is still in use today, but it also leaves another question: when a city is renamed because it helped the government defend the city, how were the survival choices of local residents rewritten into the story of imperial loyalty?[^4]

The Qing court successively sent naval and provincial commanders to lead troops across the sea, but still could not quickly end the fighting. It was not until Feng Xiaoxing led elite troops gathered across provinces to arrive in Taiwan that the situation gradually reversed. Different sources have inconsistent records regarding the composition and number of troops, so this article does not treat a single number as the only answer, but only confirms the facts that all sources point to: the Qing court had to mobilize cross-provincial military power and spend more than a year to regain control of Taiwan.[^3] [^5]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> The significance of Zhuoluo is not only in "holding out." It made the empire see that Taiwan's cities, ports, and local society must be governed together, and cannot be controlled by a single order. The later port, military settlement, and city defense policies are all the result of this city pushing the weight of local society back to the empire.

## The Archives Are Not Just About Lin Shuangwen

The research of the National Palace Museum shifted its focus from the leader to the military advisors, strategists, and grassroots figures. The article pointed out that in addition to recording military affairs, the Qing archives also left many people who played a pivotal role in the incident. These figures may not be recorded in later textbooks, but they allow us to see how a rebellion operates through intelligence, confessions, local networks, and military division of labor.[^6]

Among them, the most powerful figure is Jinniang, an Indigenous woman from Danshui on Fengshan. Based on the confession left after her arrest, StoryStudio described how she was asked to be a female military advisor by Zhuang Ditian's camp due to her skills in drawing talismans for healing and conducting religious ceremonies. She was not a passive "Indigenous woman representative" who appeared in the incident, but entered the war with her own skills, beliefs, and local relationships.[^4]

Jinniang's story also reminds us that archives do not equal transparent truth. Her confession was formed into an official text after her arrest, with her own defenses in the narrative, as well as the limitations left by the interrogation system. The fact that we can read about her does not mean we have fully heard her. This is also the distance that the National Palace Museum's research must maintain when searching for small figures from a large number of memorials and archives.[^6]

The research published by the Institute of Taiwan History, Academia Sinica, discusses how the Qing court created war images from "The Campaign to Pacify Taiwan." The research pointed out that these images are not only battlefield records, but were also created under the order of the Qianlong Emperor to publicize the Qing court's military achievements. The Taiwan Indigenous people, cities, and terrain in the picture are all placed within the perspective of imperial victory.[^7]

## How a Painting Turned the War into Victory

![The map "The Campaign to Pacify Taiwan (VII): Capturing the Rebel Chief Lin Shuangwen," 1793, drawn by Yao Wenhao, collected by the National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts. Image is a public domain file from Wikimedia Commons, used directly from the hotlink.[^12]](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Capture_of_the_rebel_chief_Lin_Shuangwen.jpg)

![The map of the battle at Fangliao in "The Campaign to Pacify Taiwan," showing the mountains, forts, and army arrangements in the same field of view. Image is a public domain file from Wikimedia Commons, used directly from the hotlink.[^11]](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/1787_Battle_at_Fangliao.jpg)

The National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts' description provided through Google Arts & Culture pointed out that "The Campaign to Pacify Taiwan" was originally drafted by Feng Xiaoxing according to the emperor's order, and then handed over to court painters to redraw into twelve battle maps, completed in 1793 and presented for imperial viewing. This set of images placed the battlefield geography, architecture, plants, people, and military dynamics in the same field of view, and turned the battle into evidence of victory that the emperor could observe.[^8]

The captured Lin Shuangwen in the picture is not the only protagonist. The bigger picture is the mountain, ambush, horse, flag, and the soldiers surrounding him. This composition places the personal failure within the narrative of the restoration of imperial order, making "capture" the ending of the entire war. However, the more complete the image, the more we need to ask what it did not paint: the burned houses, the hungry city residents, the assembled followers who were forced to participate, and those who died without leaving their names.[^7] [^8]

The "Discussion on the Relics of the Lin Shuangwen Incident" preserved by the National Digital Library of Culture describes the incident as a large-scale anti-government movement in Taiwan during the Qianlong era, and also points out that the excessive actions of local officials in investigating the case is one of the factors that caused the incident to escalate. Such local cultural data can supplement the memory outside the battlefield, but the words "revolution" or "anti-Qing" should be verified with the Palace Museum, academic papers, and museum data, and cannot be used directly as the only interpretation.[^9]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> What the empire left behind the most is often how it describes victory. When reading Taiwan's history, we should also move our focus to the people on the edge of the victory map. The battle maps draw the terrain and military strength very well, but they will not give the burned houses, hungry city residents, and unnamed dead the same clear position.

## After the Incident, Taiwan Was Rearranged

Lin Shuangwen's arrest does not mean that governance returned to the state before 1786. Huajie's collected research summarizes the changes after the incident into three points. The Qing court opened up the official landing ports in northern Taiwan, implemented the military settlement system, and relaxed the city fortification policy. These three measures touched on transportation, military mobilization, and urban defense, showing that the Qing court began to regard Taiwan as a strategic space that needed long-term management.[^5]

The Palace Museum's article also pointed out that after the incident, the Qing court adjusted the landing ports, the barbarian boundaries, and the military settlement system, and actively built cities. More noteworthy is that the incident caused a large number of memorials, imperial edicts, and military archives related to Taiwan to increase. In other words, the Lin Shuangwen incident not only changed Taiwan's institutions, but also changed the density of Taiwan being recorded by the empire.[^6]

These policies are not all progress stories. The military settlement system incorporated Indigenous groups into military and governance arrangements, and also formed new class structures and population pressures. Opening up city fortifications made cities more defensive, but also meant that Taiwan was placed under tighter military control. Policies can bring both security and constraints, and cannot be summarized with the word "development" alone.[^5]

## Why It's Still Worth Rereading Today

The Lin Shuangwen incident is not the same historical experience as the February 28th Incident, the White Terror, or democratization, and should not be forced into a linear narrative for the sake of continuity. But it raises a repeatedly appearing question in Taiwan's history: when the central authority treats the local area as a frontier that needs to be managed, how will the local society respond? And when the central government is forced to increase its governance capacity, what do the newly added institutions protect and restrict?

Lin Shuangwen was finally sent to Beijing, and Jinniang and others left traces in confessions and archives. The Qing court wrote the war as a complete military achievement, the local area left city walls, stelae, and place names, and later researchers picked up the suppressed voices from the gaps in the archives. This is not to simply oppose "official" and "folk," but to recognize that the same incident has multiple memories coexisting.[^2] [^4]

Therefore, the importance of the Lin Shuangwen incident is not only that it was one of the largest rebellions in the Qing era. It is more like a stress test, measuring that the Qing Empire's original way of governing Taiwan was no longer sufficient. After the incident was pacified, ports, military settlements, city defenses, and archives all changed. The island did not gain freedom, but was forced to become clearer in the empire's calculations.
