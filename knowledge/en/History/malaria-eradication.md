# Taiwan's Malaria Eradication: Lian Rachih and a Public Health Project That Encompassed Mosquitoes

In November 1946, the Chaozhou Malaria Research Center was established. Fifteen-year-old Lian Rachih, who had entered the Insect Research Room of the Institute of Tropical Medicine at Taipei Imperial University, later became the core figure in Taiwan's anti-malaria work. On December 4, 1965, Taiwan obtained the World Health Organization's malaria eradication registration certificate. [^1] [^2] [^3]

These three dates are easily arranged into a heroic biography: a young person meets a good teacher, studies mosquitoes, and leads a team to eliminate disease. But Taiwan's malaria eradication is actually more like a machinery of institutions. It connects microscopes, anti-malarial drugs, DDT sprayers, local health stations, the military, household registration, and people's homes, and finally turns a disease from a domestic endemic into a public health risk that must be prevented from being imported. [^1] [^8] [^9]

> **30-second overview:** During the Japanese colonial period, Taiwan was a malaria-endemic area. After the war, there was a restructuring of the medical system and a shortage of resources. In 1946, the Chaozhou Research Center connected the Rockefeller Foundation, Taiwan's local researchers, and the local epidemic prevention network. In 1948, it was reorganized into the Taiwan Province Malaria Research Institute. Indoor DDT spraying, anti-malarial drugs, mosquito vector research, and case surveillance continued for about twenty years. Taiwan obtained the WHO eradication certification in 1965. The contrast in this story is that eradication did not make mosquitoes disappear, but instead left behind a set of national capabilities that must be continuously monitored. [^1] [^7] [^8]

## Taiwan Was Not Always Free of Malaria

Malaria is not a disease unrelated to Taiwan on the tropical map. The historical exhibition of the Ministry of Health and Welfare's Centers for Disease Control records that during the Japanese colonial period, the number of deaths from malaria in Taiwan reached as high as ten thousand people per year. The National Human Rights Memory and Reconciliation Center pointed out that the Taiwan Governor's Office had established a malaria prevention office, which promoted diagnosis, treatment, and environmental cleanliness through the neighborhood responsibility system, and once suppressed the epidemic. [^1] [^8]

This experience during the Japanese colonial period cannot be simplified as "Japan did it first, and then did it after the war." The neighborhood responsibility system provided an administrative path for entering villages, but it also put epidemic prevention in the control structure of colonial governance. After the war broke out, the quinine produced in Taiwan was prioritized for transport to the South Pacific battlefields. Medical facilities were destroyed by bombing, the sanitary environment deteriorated, and malaria spread again. [^8]

The problems after the war were therefore not just about the lack of a special medicine. Researcher Tsai Tzu-yi sorted out the anti-malaria history from 1945 to 1965 and pointed out that at that time, it was necessary to deal with research institutions, drug supply, health personnel, case statistics, and public cooperation at the same time. The anti-malaria work later combined the administrative foundation left over from the Japanese colonial period, Western public health technology, and Taiwan's local environmental conditions, and gradually formed a runnable plan. [^9]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> Taiwan's malaria history is not a story of foreign medicine pressing a stop button on local diseases. What really changed the situation was a set of technologies translated into actions that local health workers, researchers, and residents could do every day.

## Chaozhou: Why Was the Research Center Located Here

In 1946, the Central Institute of Health sent experts to survey Taiwan, believing that the local anti-malaria technology was backward. Mr. Hua Sheng, the representative of the Rockefeller Foundation in China, and Central Institute of Health researcher Hsu Shih-chu came to Taiwan in November of the same year to find a base that could be used to manage anti-malaria affairs nearby. In the end, the research center was set up in Chaozhou, where the epidemic was most severe. [^8]

Chaozhou is located in the area with the highest disease density and the most need for research, so the research center could not just do experiments away from the local area. The research center initially used the space of the Chaozhou Malaria Prevention Office from the Japanese colonial period, and later moved into the former Chaozhou Shinto Shrine provided by the Kaohsiung County Government. In 1947, the research center also set up field research stations in Wufeng, Taichung, and Keelung, extending the research from one building to different geographical environments. [^8]

The work of the research center was not just to identify the malaria parasite on paper. It studied the ecology of malaria mosquitoes, trained anti-malaria personnel, tested drugs such as chloroquine and sulfadoxine, and introduced DDT residual indoor spraying, outdoor larvae treatment, and automatic flushing. Chaozhou thus became a public health base that put entomology, drug testing, health engineering, and administrative training in the same space. [^8]

On April 1, 1948, the Malaria Research Center was merged into the Health Bureau of the Taiwan Provincial Government and reorganized into the Taiwan Province Malaria Research Institute. The Rockefeller Foundation later withdrew personnel and equipment due to the Chinese political situation, and the original international aid was interrupted. The institute did not disappear because of this. The China Rural Reconstruction Association has been providing funding since 1949 to keep the anti-malaria business going. [^8]

This change altered the way the results were attributed. The results of Taiwan's public health cannot only be written as "U.S. aid brings success," nor can it be reversed as "completed by the locals themselves." External funding brought in instruments, drugs, training, and international standards. Local institutions turned these resources into work that could pass through villages, homes, and seasonal changes. Without one of them, the research center might only be left with a short history of aid. [^8] [^9]

## What Lian Rachih Learned First Was Typing, Not Eliminating Malaria

Lian Rachih was born in Dadaocheng in 1927. At the age of 15, due to the need for family economic conditions, he was recommended to work in the Insect Research Room of the Institute of Tropical Medicine at Taipei Imperial University, mainly responsible for English typing and document processing. The Science Park records that his supervisor, Professor Mori Senzaburo, required him to stop typing when there were visitors and focus on listening to the research discussion. [^2] [^3]

This scene is more illustrative of his starting point than the "genius young man." He did not receive complete academic training first, but listened to the discussion of mosquito classification, tropical diseases, and epidemic prevention in the office, and then practiced microscope observation by himself. The acting director, Mori Shigemitsu, let him identify the larvae of malaria mosquitoes. The first time was ten pieces, the second time was ten pieces, and the results were both correct. Later, he identified the Luconia mosquito when sampling in the pond, and gradually determined that he wanted to spend his life on mosquito research. [^3] [^4]

There is an easily overlooked place in Lian Rachih's story. He was later known as the "mosquito doctor," not because he stood at the center of the research system from the beginning, but because a boy who was originally only responsible for English typing gradually gained research qualifications through listening, practicing, sampling, and repeated comparison. This is a story of personal ability, but also a story of institutions occasionally letting people in marginal positions approach the core of knowledge. [^2] [^3]

After he entered the Taiwan Province Malaria Research Institute, he worked in the field all over Taiwan, participated in malaria eradication, and also studied Japanese encephalitis, dengue fever, and scrub typhus. In 1958, he was funded by WHO to study at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, where he learned mosquito susceptibility analysis techniques for insecticides. This expertise was later not only used to identify mosquitoes, but also to determine whether anti-mosquito measures were still effective. [^1] [^3] [^4]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> What is most worth remembering about Lian Rachih is not only how many kinds of mosquitoes he found for Taiwan, but also his ability to turn "seeing a mosquito" into "knowing how a community should prevent epidemics."

## The Power of DDT Lies in Its Ability to Enter Every House

### How Epidemic Prevention Becomes the Rhythm of Local Work

The truly difficult part of malaria prevention is often not the one-time distribution of medicine, but rather making the work happen repeatedly according to the seasons and geographical conditions. Researchers must know when the rainy season begins, health workers must record the villages where cases appear, spraying teams must return to the same batch of houses to confirm coverage, and local residents must accept inspections, take medicine, and receive health education in their daily lives. There is no single picture that can represent all the results of this work. It depends on many people doing small things for a long enough time.

The value provided by the Chaozhou Research Center is precisely to turn these scattered actions into teachable, comparable, and traceable processes. Mosquito specimens can be sent to the laboratory, cases can be entered into statistical tables, drug trials can be fed back to the scene, and indoor spraying can also be performed by trained personnel. When the number of cases in a village decreases, researchers still cannot immediately declare success, because the decrease may be related to the season, population movement, or inadequate diagnosis. The eradication plan must separate "no cases seen" from "no local transmission."

This also explains why anti-malaria work has trained a group of technically skilled people who are familiar with the local area. They know which roads are difficult to walk after the rain, which type of wall is not easy to keep the medicine, and which village's residents need to be explained in different languages or ways. The official plan provides standards, and local workers fill in the environmental differences, so eradication has a chance to turn the goals on paper into a daily executable rhythm.

One of the key tools for Taiwan's anti-malaria work after the war is DDT. The CDC records that the anti-malaria plan was carried out through the health units of various counties and cities, the National Army, and the cooperation of the people, and carried out a large-scale indoor chemical prevention. This is not spraying a distant, invisible field, but letting epidemic prevention workers enter the house, and leaving medicine on the wall that can continue to work. [^1]

This method is effective precisely because it connects the habits of mosquito vectors with the structure of housing. The Chaozhou Research Base confirmed that the Asian malaria mosquito is the main vector, and it prefers to live indoors. Therefore, the indoor wall becomes the intervention point of epidemic prevention, and the residents' houses also become the space that the state's health policy can measure, deal with, and review. [^1] [^8]

![Close-up of a malaria mosquito sucking blood, showing that vector research is facing a specific and tiny biological target](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/ae/Anopheles_albimanus_mosquito.jpg?utm_source=commons.wikimedia.org&utm_campaign=index&utm_content=original)

_Figure: Anopheles albimanus mosquito close-up. Image source: U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention Public Health Image Library, released by the U.S. federal government work, public domain. Picture file page: [^11]_

But effectiveness does not mean there is no cost. DDT represents the public health's trust in chemical prevention at that time, and also incorporates residents into a collective action that must be coordinated. Research points out that after the war, anti-malaria is not only done by legal coercion, but also uses publicity, education, and flexible communication to improve participation. The back of this sentence is that the government must know who lives where, which house has been sprayed, and where there are still cases, so that epidemic prevention can form a coverage rate. [^9]

Therefore, malaria eradication is both a medical project and a governance project. It makes the mosquito vectors from a kind of insect in the natural environment into a public object that needs to be classified, monitored, estimated, and dealt with. To the people at that time, this may be a spraying. To the institution, it is a practice of connecting the family space with the state's statistics and health network. [^1] [^9]

## 1965: There Are Two Ways to Remember the Date of Eradication

The WHO's malaria-free certification list lists Taiwan among the areas certified in November 1965. The historical exhibition of the Taiwan CDC records that Taiwan obtained the WHO's malaria eradication registration certificate on December 4, 1965. The two dates are not errors that can be chosen at will in the article, but rather the "certification month of the international list" and the "date of the certificate" two kinds of institutional time. [^1] [^6]

This achievement is usually written as "Taiwan became the first malaria-free area in the world." The Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the articles of the Normal University all use this statement, and place it in the context of national health, social development, and economic development. For post-war Taiwan, the eradication of malaria indeed means that a long-term, cross-departmental public health capability has been formed. [^2] [^5] [^6]

But "eradication" does not mean that Taiwan will never have mosquitoes again, nor does it mean that all work related to malaria has ended. In 2015, the Ministry of Health and Welfare data pointed out that Taiwan can still find Asian malaria mosquitoes, and the average number of imported cases per year is about 10 to 30. The CDC therefore continues to carry out vector surveys, imported case testing, patient treatment tracking, and anti-malarial drug reserves. [^1] [^7]

> "Eradication" is most easily misunderstood as a period. For public health, it is more like a threshold: after crossing it, society has the resources to shift its efforts from comprehensive elimination to maintaining non-relapse.

## The Chaozhou Research Institute Is Also in the Shadow of the White Terror

The history of the Malaria Research Institute cannot be isolated as a purely bright scientific history. The National Human Rights Memory and Reconciliation Center records that in 1948, the Chaozhou Branch used the Chaozhou Wude Temple, which was repaired after the war, as the office space. In 1952, after the institute's headquarters and branches merged, it became the headquarters of the Taiwan Malaria Eradication Program. In 1969, the headquarters moved to Nangang, Taipei. The original building was later given to Chaozhou High School. [^8]

The same human rights memory also records that Ye Shengji was arrested by the military police at the Chaozhou Branch on May 29, 1950, and was later sentenced and shot in November of the same year. Ye Shengji was originally a doctor who graduated from the College of Medicine at National Taiwan University. He visited the Chaozhou Branch to observe the work of DDT spraying and collecting malaria mosquitoes, and left a positive impression on the research equipment there. [^8]

This overlap is not to say that anti-malaria work is equivalent to the White Terror, nor is it to negate the public health work with a political case. It reminds us that research institutions have never existed in a political vacuum. The Chaozhou Branch can be both a place where young researchers see American instruments and learn vector science, and a place where the state security apparatus arrests people. Putting both memories on the same map prevents "modernization" from being written as a shadowless indoor photo. [^8]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> A research institute may allow people to see mosquitoes under a microscope, and may also allow people to see soldiers outside the door. Taiwan's public health history is important not because it can be put into a purely progressive story, but because it must be read together with the contemporary state power.

## After Eradication, Taiwan Took the Experience Out to Sea

Taiwan's malaria history did not stop in 1965. Lian Rachih later entered the U.S. Naval Medical Research Institute to continue medical insect research, and also participated in anti-malaria work in Bolivia and Saint Pierre and Miquelon. The data of the Normal University and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs records that he participated in the anti-malaria plan in Saint Pierre at the age of 80 after 2003, and Taiwan turned the local eradication experience into a part of public health diplomacy. [^2] [^5]

The official data uses different years for the incidence rate in Saint Pierre. The Ministry of Foreign Affairs article records that the incidence rate dropped from 50% in 2003 to 4% in 2012 and 1.01% in 2015. The Science Park describes the infection rate in another plan's statistical caliber, from 40% to below 1%. These numbers cannot be mixed into a single precise decline curve. The article can only indicate the source's statement. [^3] [^5]

This difference is actually very meaningful. Anti-malaria work is not a set of machines that can be copied intact after leaving the local area. Mosquito vectors, housing, rainfall, medical accessibility, drug supply, and administrative capabilities are all different. What Taiwan exports is not only a spraying method, but also research, training, monitoring, and the method of translating technology into local work. [^1] [^3] [^5]

## The Responsibility of a Mosquito: Monitoring After Eradication

The most worth re-reading part of Taiwan's malaria eradication is that it contains three scales at the same time. The first is the personal scale of Lian Rachih identifying larvae under the microscope. The second is the institutional scale of the Chaozhou Research Institute, local health units, and epidemic prevention workers passing through the house. The third is the cross-border scale formed by WHO certification, international aid, and public health diplomacy. No single scale alone is enough to explain the result of 1965. [^1] [^2] [^8] [^9]

It also contains three times at the same time. The prevention office of the Japanese colonial period provided the history of administration and health. After the war, U.S. aid and research centers brought knowledge, drugs, and equipment into the country. After eradication, the CDC system took "no local cases" as a state that must be continuously maintained. This is not a linear progression from backward to advanced, but a process of continuous reorganization, continuation, and repair. [^7] [^8] [^9]

Today, when talking about malaria, the easiest thing to say is "Taiwan has already eradicated." But if this sentence is not connected to the research base in Chaozhou in 1946, Lian Rachih's field sampling, the DDT on the residents' walls, the two dates in 1965, and the Asian malaria mosquitoes that still exist after eradication, it will compress a institutional engineering into a medal. [^1] [^3] [^7] [^6]

Taiwan once built a set of capabilities that could suppress diseases to the point of being invisible, and could identify them before they came back. This capability is closer to the true meaning of eradication than "mosquitoes disappear." This capability has the traces of foreign aid, the hands of local researchers, the power of national governance, and the intervention of the authoritarian era on the body and space. The focus of preservation is not the word "miracle," but how this capability was formed and why it must continue to be checked. [^5] [^7] [^8]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> The last object left by malaria eradication is not the certificate, but a mosquito collection tube that has not been put away. It reminds us that the most reliable victory of public health has never been to declare that things will end forever, but to know where to check again tomorrow.
