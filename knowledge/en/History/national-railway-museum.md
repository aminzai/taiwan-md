> **30-Second Overview:** The Taipei Locomotive Works, established in 1930 and opened in 1935, once handled the maintenance, assembly, and upkeep of Taiwan's rail vehicles. After 2012, the trains moved out, but this nearly 17-hectare industrial site did not disappear. It was designated a National Historic Site in 2015 and opened its first phase as the National Railway Museum in 2025. What is preserved here is not just old trains, but how the factory buildings, tools, bathhouses, and labor techniques collectively preserve the memory of moving an island.

In 1930, the Taipei Railway Factory began construction in the Songshan area, officially opening in 1935. It was not a building designed for audiences, but a factory that existed to ensure trains returned to the tracks on time. At the time, the site centered on maintenance, assembly, upkeep, and repair, eventually becoming one of Taiwan's largest and oldest railway vehicle repair factories still in existence.[^1]

After 2012, the Taipei Locomotive Works gradually exited its original mission; official English historical pages state the relocation was completed at the end of 2013. Researchers, however, interpret this move within the context of Taiwan Railway's modernization and the commissioning of the Fu-o Vehicle Base.[^2] The trains left behind a nearly 17-hectare industrial site, along with decades of wear, oil stains, tracks, machinery, and living facilities.[^3]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** In a train museum, the locomotive is the easiest thing to see; the people who made the locomotive run are the easiest to forget. The Taipei Locomotive Works reverses this viewing order: first look at the factory, then understand the train.

## Not Just Putting Trains in Display Cases

During its time as a railway factory, it was not preserved for "preservation." Its mission was to dismantle vehicles, find faults, manufacture parts, reassemble them, and send the trains back into regular service. A 2021 museum exhibition planning study pointed out that the Taipei Locomotive Works was not originally built for a museum; therefore, much of the data, objects, and documents were not centrally preserved, and the exhibited vehicles are scattered across Taiwan.[^4]

This difference dictates the presentation method of the National Railway Museum. It does not just display "which train once ran in Taiwan"; it also helps people understand how a train was maintained, what kind of spring was forged, how workers specialized amidst high heat and noise, and how a factory connected to the city's energy, transportation, and labor systems. A 2020 study on industrial heritage analyzed the forging and metallurgy shop as a case study, placing machinery, parts manufacturing processes, worker workspaces, and exhibition planning within the same analysis.[^5]

### The Architecture of 1935 Solved Problems for Workers First

The architectural language of the Taipei Locomotive Works does not persuade through decoration. National Cultural Heritage records indicate that the buildings and auxiliary facilities on the site generally followed a 1930s plan, with construction methods and spatial configuration primarily focused on function. The tall factory buildings, tracks, and workspaces were all designed to allow vehicles to enter, be dismantled, moved, and reassembled.[^1]

A report on the site by _Taiwan Guanghua_ in 2017 recorded specific dimensions before refurbishment: the entire area was approximately 17 hectares, with a main assembly factory measuring 167 meters long, 20.4 meters high, and 23.8 meters wide. The large-span steel trusses and high windows allowed for natural light and ventilation in the vast spaces. These dimensions were not architectural flourishes but necessary conditions for train maintenance work.[^6]

Even the floor had a working logic. The factory floors were paved with large pieces of cypress wood, with asphalt filling the gaps to absorb oil stains, reduce vibration, and mitigate damage from falling heavy parts. High-pressure steam generated by boilers was used not only to drive machinery but also for heating the workers' bathhouse. Industrial efficiency and labor life were not separate stories; they once shared the same piping system.[^6]

## From Machine Bureau to Railway Factory

The Taipei Locomotive Works was not the starting point of Taiwan's railway industry. Records from the Railway Department Park at the National Taiwan Museum trace its origins back to the Taipei Machine Bureau during Liu Mingchuan’s era in 1884. That machine bureau, located on the Tamsui Riverbank, had smelting and forging facilities, which later changed uses through military factories and railway factories. In 1900, the Japanese Army Ministry transferred related facilities to the Railway Department and renamed it Taipei Railway Factory.[^7]

In 1909, the Taipei Railway Factory expanded with vehicle repair and painting workshops. In 1934, the original factory moved to Songshan, forming the direct predecessor of today's Taipei Locomotive Works. This trajectory gives this industrial heritage multiple layers of time—from Qing Dynasty Westernization, Japanese colonial railway modernization, post-war Taiwan Railway systems, to contemporary cultural preservation.[^7]

Therefore, the Taipei Locomotive Works cannot be explained by a single regime or a single industry. Its walls, machinery, and tracks witnessed how Taiwan converted foreign equipment, technology, and systems into daily operations on the island. It also witnessed how different eras renamed, took over, and used the same factory.

## The Era of the Train is Written in the Maintenance Methods

The official history page of the National Railway Museum links the maintenance history of the Taipei Locomotive Works with the development history of Taiwan Railways: during the Japanese colonial period, steam locomotives were mainly maintained; after the 1960s, diesel locomotives became prevalent. After Taiwan Railways was electrified in 1979, it faced the maintenance demands of various types of electric locomotives. Starting in 1997, the factory also undertook repairs on some steam locomotives to restore them to operation.[^2]

This was not merely a generational change in equipment. Every power conversion required a rearrangement of worker skills, machinery, parts supply, and inspection systems. A 2020 study noted that the forging shop once had production lines for making helical springs and leaf springs. After British-made electric locomotives were introduced in 1978, Taiwan Railways gradually switched to air springs, but early trains still required maintenance of helical springs.[^5]

The research also identified the steam hammers, heating, and forming equipment in the forging area, as well as material storage, finished product inspection, and worker rest areas. These objects mean that "technological progress" is not just an abstract upward line: when a new train technology arrived, old machinery did not necessarily disappear immediately; it might continue to serve the still-operating _Juguang_ (莒光號) or freight trains.[^5]

## Workers' Lives in a Factory

The preservation value of the Taipei Locomotive Works also includes welfare facilities and working memory. The 2015 designation as a National Historic Site specifically noted that the site preserved not only buildings, landscapes, and machinery but also intangible cultural assets such as casting, forging, machine operation, and vehicle maintenance, along with welfare facilities related to the workers.[^1]

Field reports from 2017 documented that workers inside the factory created humorous "TRA robots" out of parts. The diesel-electric locomotive workshop and the internal combustion engine workshop also retained murals created by workers. A retired supervisor, Chen Wu-chang (陳武昌), recalled that a single steam locomotive had thousands of parts, and workers could manufacture each one. This capability was not just a skill listed in a job title but knowledge shared by an entire working community.[^6]

The Taipei Locomotive Works was historically difficult for the general public to access. Researchers point out that before its relocation, the factory was an important repair base for Taiwan Railways; guided tours only began via online reservation in 2018. A railway-themed lecture planned by the Ministry of Culture in 2019 was also part of preparing this transforming museum.[^8]

## The Integrity That Almost Was Dismantled

On March 15, 2015, the Cultural Heritage and Historic Buildings Review Committee of the Ministry of Culture resolved to designate the Taipei Locomotive Works as a National Historic Site, not because one building was particularly beautiful, but to preserve the overall appearance of the railway industrial heritage in the entire area. The official announcement stated that the architectural history, transportation development, economic industry history, labor culture, and industrial heritage value of the Taipei Locomotive Works were interconnected, possessing irreplaceable site significance.[^9]

The importance of this decision lies in its refusal to cut up industrial heritage into a main building and several movable exhibits. The workers' bathhouse, material testing laboratory, technician training school, grand hall, general office, and diesel-electric workshop must be understood together with the tracks, factory scale, and workflow. If any part is missing, what remains may just be an exteriorly complete shell that has lost its working context.

However, preservation does not mean restoring it to some undisputed "original state." Research on Taiwan Railway cultural heritage preservation points out that many railway vehicles and objects were dismantled in the past due to insufficient regulatory recognition and conservation awareness. The museumization of the Taipei Locomotive Works required collaboration among the Ministry of Culture, Taiwan Railways, researchers, civil groups, former employees, and visitors.[^8]

## In 2025, the Factory Begins Welcoming Visitors

On July 31, 2025, the first phase of the National Railway Museum officially opened. The Ministry of Culture stated that this project took nearly nine years of historic site restoration and preparation, with the first phase opening six historical buildings: the diesel-electric workshop, general office, technician training school, material testing laboratory, grand hall, and bathhouse.[^3]

The exhibitions deliberately do not just talk about vehicles. "Power. Trains—Diesel-Electric Workshop" deals with machinery and vehicles. "Movement and Emotion—Culture and Memory on the Rails" places literature, film, songs, and fine art into railway history. "Vaporous Era—Workers' Bathhouse" preserves the workers' lives within the museum's narrative. The first phase also features 24 static preserved vehicles and plans for dynamic ride experiences on holidays.[^3]

This concept of a "living museum" is not complete just by running trains. Museum opening materials from the Ministry of Culture mention that the park uses its original tracks for vehicle displays, allowing the collection to move between different exhibition themes. A passage spanning 335 meters north and south through the park was also opened, making the museum not just a closed area entered by paying visitors, but a public thoroughfare connecting to city avenues, Keelung Road, and surrounding neighborhoods.[^3]

A news report from CNA in English described this opening as a place where "the trains still run and memories continue to move." This phrasing captures a key point: the museum preserves not a stopped past, but provides opportunities for the past to walk, be viewed, and be questioned within contemporary cities.[^10]

## "Living" Also Means Incomplete

The National Railway Museum currently operates in phases; official visitor information clearly lists areas undergoing construction and those that are not yet open. This is not merely a warning to visitors, but part of the museum's state: an industrial site nearly a century old and once continuously operational cannot be polished into a flawless finished product on opening day.[^11]

A 2021 exhibition planning study also reminded that the transformation process of the Taipei Locomotive Works still faces issues with scattered data, objects, landscapes, and vehicles. Research, surveying, interviewing former employees, restoring buildings, collecting objects, defining functional areas, and arranging exhibitions are all tasks that must advance simultaneously.[^4]

Therefore, the challenge for the museum is not just preserving "what can be seen." It also involves preserving invisible technology, processing institutional memories from different eras, explaining which parts are still under research, acknowledging which objects have been lost, and letting visitors know how a factory was preserved amidst the speed of railway modernization.

## What the City Gains Is Not Just a Nostalgic Site

A specialized report in 2017 described the Taipei Locomotive Works as one of Taiwan's earliest "scientific industrial parks" because it concentrated equipment, manufacturing processes, precision, and time management in one place. The value of this description is not to give it a grand title, but to remind us: modernization in Taiwan did not only happen in offices or industrial zones; it also happened when workers used tools to make individual parts into trains fit for the road.[^6]

From the machine bureau on the Tamsui Riverbank, to the railway factory during the Japanese colonial period, from the post-war repair base of Taiwan Railways, to the National Railway Museum opening in 2025—this line has no single starting point and no endpoint that can resolve all issues. It records how Taiwan received technology, adjusted systems, trained workers, and turned a set of modern tools originally not belonging to the island into its own daily life.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The most moving part of the Taipei Locomotive Works is not that it finally became a museum, but that it was never forced to forget that it was once a factory. When visitors walk in, they do not see the end of the railway; they see the work that still demands our understanding.

The National Railway Museum should therefore be viewed as an ongoing public project, not a completed nostalgic site. It places trains, buildings, technology, labor, and the city into one space, forcing us to ask a very practical question: after a factory stops producing trains, can it still produce understanding?

The answer is not on an exhibition plaque. The answer lies in the large-span truss of the assembly factory, the machinery left behind in the forging shop, the steam from the bathhouse, the memories of old employees, and every vehicle that still moves along the old tracks.
