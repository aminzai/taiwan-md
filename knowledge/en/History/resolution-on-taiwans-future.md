# Taiwan's Future Resolution: Two Characters, "Currently," Held Up for Twenty-Seven Years

> **30-second overview:** On May 8, 1999, the Democratic Progressive Party passed the Resolution on Taiwan's Future in Kaohsiung, completing a strategic shift with the sentence "Taiwan, of course, is currently called the Republic of China under the constitution, but is not subordinate to the People's Republic of China." The two characters "currently" added by Lin I-hsiung before "state name" made hardliners feel that "there's a chance to change it in the future," and middle voters felt that "the DPP has accepted the status quo." This document opened the door for the first peaceful transfer of power in 2000, and also became the foundation of cross-strait discourse in Taiwan for the next twenty-seven years, and to this day no one dares to clarify its ambiguity.

In the spring of 1999, Kuo Ching-yang received a mission: to write a new cross-strait position paper for the Democratic Progressive Party. Chen Shui-bian, through the then-party secretary-general You Xi-kun, pushed for the direction to use "Republic of China" to replace the "Taiwan Republic" in the Taiwan independence party platform, in order to win over middle voters.[^1]

Kuo Ching-yang wrote "Republic of China" as the state name. Lin I-hsiung picked up the draft and added two characters before "state name": "currently."

"The Chen faction was very unhappy."[^2]

"Currently" implies that the state name may change in the future, weakening the commitment to middle voters. But Lin I-hsiung insisted: without these two characters, it would mean the DPP officially accepts the Republic of China as the permanent state name, and hardliners would not agree. He believed the cost of insisting on the name of the Republic of China was very realistic: the overseas missions might be forced to close, "it's more important for the country to go out."[^3]

In the end, the resolution was passed with Lin I-hsiung's version. The two characters soothed both sides, and also buried an ambiguity in Taiwan's politics that no one has been willing to clarify to this day.

## Eight Years of Turnaround

The starting point of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future was in 1991. On October 13 of that year, the DPP passed the Taiwan independence party platform, advocating "establishing a Taiwan Republic with sovereignty, independence and autonomy."[^4] The drafter was Lin I-hsiung. The platform included a caveat: the state-building position "should be decided by all residents of Taiwan through a citizen vote," but the core signal was unambiguous: the DPP wanted to establish a state.

Four years later, on September 14, 1995, in Washington, D.C. After spending twenty-five years in political prisons, Su Hsien-dung, as chairman of the DPP, said to the international audience a sentence that later became the starting point of the turnaround: "If the DPP comes to power, it does not need to and will not declare Taiwan's independence, because Taiwan has been independent for half a century."[^5] This was a reassurance to the international community and a thunderous blow to the hardliners within the party. After several rounds of debate within the party, this sentence eventually became the consensus of the entire party, planting the seed for the Resolution on Taiwan's Future four years later.

Su Hsien-dung's argument can be traced back even earlier to 1980. He put forward "Taiwan's independence under the Republic of China model" in prison: Taiwan has already been independent, and its current name is the Republic of China. This logic turned "independence" from a future tense into a present tense, requiring no declaration, only recognition.[^6]

What really forced the turnaround was the ballot box. In March 1996, the DPP nominated "father of Taiwan independence" Peng Ming-min to run for president, with a vote share of 21.13%, the largest defeat in a nationwide election since the party's founding.[^7] American political scientist Shelley Rigger's analysis was blunt: voters felt that supporting independence was too dangerous. After Peng Ming-min's defeat, he withdrew from politics and founded the "State-Building Association." The party paid a heavy price on the issue of position: it lost its own presidential candidate.

The post-election reflection was intense. In May 1996, more than 100 young party members signed a petition to publish the "New Generation Platform," written by Chou Yi-cheng, criticizing the party's "rhetorical independence" and "formalistic culture," and advocating that the Taiwan independence movement should be based on the national identity of more than 20 million people.[^8] This platform directly challenged the methodology of the Taiwan independence party platform: the direction of state-building was not wrong, but slogans would not make it happen.

External pressure was also escalating. On June 30, 1998, U.S. President Clinton visited mainland China and announced the "Three No's" policy at a joint press conference with Jiang Zemin in Shanghai: not supporting Taiwan's independence, not supporting "one country, two systems" or two Chinas, and not supporting Taiwan's participation in international organizations with statehood as the membership qualification.[^9] In February of the same year, a major debate on Chinese policy erupted within the DPP: Hsu Hsin-liang advocated "boldly going west" and actively engaging in exchanges, while Qiu Yi-ren and the New Tide faction insisted on "strong foundation, gradual progress" to control risks, with the compromise being "strong foundation, going west."[^10] At the end of the year, the mayor of Taipei, Chen Shui-bian, failed to win re-election, losing to the Kuomintang's Ma Ying-jeou. Although Su Hsien-dung won Kaohsiung city for the DPP that year, the defeat in Taipei shook the entire party: even Chen Shui-bian, with extremely high governance satisfaction, could not hold on to the capital. What could win the presidential election? A consensus spread within the party: the position must be fundamentally adjusted.

All the pressures converging from all directions pointed to the same direction: the road of independence and state-building is not feasible, and pragmatic transformation cannot be delayed any longer.

> **📝 Curator's note:** The story of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future is often narrated as "the victory of pragmatism." But the people who pushed for the position turnaround were all the original definers of the position. The person who said "no need to declare independence" was Su Hsien-dung, who had been imprisoned for Taiwan's independence for the longest time; Lin I-hsiung, who drafted the Taiwan independence party platform, added two characters to a document continuing the same spirit eight years later, turning "state-building" into "recognizing the status quo." Those who pushed for the turnaround were all the people who had traveled the farthest on that road.

## The Ambiguity Written by Three People

Chen Chung-hsin proposed the key compromise solution: not modifying the Taiwan independence party platform, but passing a new resolution. Chen Chung-hsin, whose pen name is Hang Zhi, was the former chief editor of the "Beautiful Island" magazine, and was one of the earliest intellectuals to use text to intervene in political movements. His solution precisely responded to the political reality within the party: the Taiwan independence party platform could not be touched, because touching it would mean declaring war on the deep green; but if you don't get out of the framework of the Taiwan independence party platform, you can't win the 2000 presidential election. Using a new document to bypass the old document, letting the two coexist without negating each other, this itself was the first step of the ambiguity strategy.

The three people in the drafting group represented three different forces. Kuo Ching-yang was the main drafter, standing on the side of Chen Shui-bian's pragmatic election strategy; Chen Chung-hsin coordinated the various parties, and was a literatus intellectual who had walked from the chief editor of "Beautiful Island" magazine to the outside world; Lin I-hsiung guarded the bottom line of the position, representing the theoretical insistence of the New Tide faction. Lin I-hsiung said: "(I) played the role of preventing the resolution from leaning too much toward Chen and You's middle-of-the-road line."[^12]

On May 8, 1999, at the second plenary session of the eighth National Party Representatives Conference of the DPP, in Kaohsiung, the Resolution on Taiwan's Future was passed.[^13]

The core paragraph of the document was written very precisely:

> **✦** "Taiwan is a sovereign and independent state, its territorial jurisdiction extends only to Taiwan, Penghu, Kinmen, Matsu and their affiliated islands, as well as the territorial waters and contiguous waters in accordance with international law. Taiwan, of course, is currently called the Republic of China under the constitution, but is not subordinate to the People's Republic of China. Any change in the current state of independence must be decided by all residents of Taiwan through a citizen vote."

The preamble of the resolution reviewed the process of Taiwan's democratization, pointing out that "through the arduous struggle of the Democratic Progressive Party and the people for many years, the Kuomintang was forced to abandon martial law and one-party rule, and accept democratic reforms," making Taiwan "de facto an independent democratic state."[^27] The seven propositions covered the complete framework of national positioning: Taiwan is a sovereign and independent state (first point), not belonging to the People's Republic of China (second point), should participate in the international community and join the United Nations (third point), should abandon the one-China principle (fourth point), should complete the legalization of referendums (fifth point), the political parties should establish a consensus on foreign policy (sixth point), and the cross-strait should establish a peaceful framework through dialogue (seventh point).

The seventh point was the most subtle. A document that advocates Taiwan's non-subordination to China, the last article was written as "through comprehensive dialogue, seeking deep mutual understanding and economic and trade benefits, and establishing a peaceful framework." This ending made the resolution not entirely an anti-war declaration, and also reserved space for cross-strait exchanges in the future.

Looking at the 1991 Taiwan independence party platform, the difference was clear. The Taiwan independence party platform wanted to "establish a Taiwan Republic with sovereignty, independence and autonomy," which was about changing the current situation. The Resolution on Taiwan's Future said "Taiwan is a sovereign and independent state," which was about declaring that the current situation already existed. From doing one thing, it turned into recognizing that one thing had already been done. The state name changed from "Taiwan Republic" to "currently called the Republic of China under the constitution." The function of the referendum was reversed: from the starter of state-building, it became the insurance of protecting the status quo.

The reason why the ambiguity could work was that everyone read what they wanted to see. The hardliners saw "currently" and felt that there was a chance to change the state name in the future, and the dream of state-building was still alive. The middle voters saw "currently called the Republic of China under the constitution" and felt that the DPP had finally become pragmatic, and they didn't have to worry about cross-strait war when voting. The Kuomintang saw "Taiwan is not subordinate to the People's Republic of China" and criticized this was just a change of packaging of Taiwan independence. Beijing agreed with the Kuomintang's assessment and determined this was "de facto Taiwan independence."[^14]

Every party's interpretation had textual support. No party was completely wrong. This was exactly the intention of the drafters.

## The Door Opened

On March 18, 2000, Chen Shui-bian was elected president with 39.3% of the vote, achieving the first peaceful transfer of power.[^15]

Chen Shui-bian ran on the "new middle-of-the-road line": deliberately ambiguous on foreign diplomacy and national defense, focusing on cross-party cooperation and anti-corruption reforms, making the focus of the campaign issues shift from the Taiwan independence and unification issue to governance capabilities. The Resolution on Taiwan's Future provided a stable foundation for this strategy — the party's cross-strait position had already been made clear, and the candidate did not have to answer "do you want to declare independence" every day.

The role of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future in the campaign could be summed up in one sentence: it made middle voters no longer afraid. The equation "the DPP comes to power = declares Taiwan's independence = cross-strait war" was broken by the Resolution on Taiwan's Future. Since the DPP itself said that Taiwan has already been independent, the state name is called the Republic of China, and changes to the current situation require a referendum, then voting for the DPP is just changing the ruling party, which has nothing to do with the country's direction.

When Chen Shui-bian took office, he announced the "Four No's and One No": no declaration of independence, no change of state name, no push for a two-state theory in the constitution, no push for a referendum on independence and unification, and no issue of abolishing the national unification council and the national unification association.[^16] These five commitments were simultaneously directed at Washington and Beijing, and were a carefully calibrated political guarantee. These commitments sounded like concessions, but within the logic of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future, they were the inevitable extension of the status quo: since Taiwan has already been independent and changes to the status quo require a referendum, then "not declaring independence" is just stating that a thing that doesn't need to happen won't happen. The precision of the wording was already preset in the drafting stage.

In 2001, the DPP tried to formally elevate the status of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future, making it superior to the Taiwan independence party platform in terms of effectiveness.[^17] The attempt was unsuccessful. The Taiwan independence party platform was not abolished, not frozen, and not modified. It was simply covered by another document.

## The Foundation No One Dares to Touch

The Resolution on Taiwan's Future has lived for twenty-seven years, longer than any subsequent discourse. The reason it could last so long was very simple: every attempt to surpass it or retreat from it failed.

In September 2007, at the end of Chen Shui-bian's second term, the DPP passed the "Normal State Resolution," trying to go one step further: the state name was officially changed to Taiwan, a new constitution was formulated, and Taiwan was joined the United Nations under the name of "Taiwan."[^18] The Resolution on Taiwan's Future recognized de facto independence under the hat of the Republic of China, and the Normal State Resolution wanted to take off the hat and move toward legal independence. The moderate faction within the party believed this was a risky move by Chen Shui-bian to divert the controversy of governance. In the 2008 election, the DPP suffered a devastating defeat, with Su Hsien-dung's vote share at 41.55%, and the Normal State Resolution was shelved.

In 2014, the main drafter of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future, Kuo Ching-yang, and more than 40 party representatives signed a proposal to freeze the Taiwan independence party platform. The reason was written bluntly: the DPP's presidents and candidates "have already acted to accept the Republic of China," and the Taiwan independence party platform "is equivalent to opposing the Republic of China and seeking to change the status quo, causing unnecessary misunderstanding in the international community."[^19] The party chair, Tsai Ing-wen, ordered it to be sent to the central executive meeting for discussion, and there was no follow-up after that.

Going forward is not possible, and retreating is not possible. The Resolution on Taiwan's Future stayed in place, becoming the only remaining position.

In February 2024, Wu Chun-shuo, director of the DPP's Chinese Affairs Department, said in an online lecture the party's open secret: the Taiwan independence party platform "has become a historical document" and is practically replaced by the Resolution on Taiwan's Future.[^20] At the end of 2023, Zhuo Rong-tai, who was the executive director of Lai Ching-te's campaign headquarters, was even more direct: "The Resolution on Taiwan's Future has clearly told the world that Taiwan is a sovereign and independent country, and its name is the Republic of China. This is the DPP's only pragmatic position, and there is no issue with not freezing the Taiwan independence party platform."[^21]

Tsai Ing-wen, during her eight years in office, developed the "Four Principles" based on the Resolution on Taiwan's Future: resolutely uphold the non-subordination of the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China, resolutely uphold the principle that sovereignty cannot be invaded and annexed, resolutely uphold that the future of the Republic of China in Taiwan must be determined by all 23 million people in Taiwan, and resolutely uphold the democratic constitutional system of freedom and democracy.[^28] Lai Ching-te was even more direct in the 2024 election: "Chen Shui-bian was elected president based on the Resolution on Taiwan's Future, and did not declare Taiwan's independence while in office; Tsai Ing-wen also did not declare Taiwan's independence. I will also not declare Taiwan's independence when I am elected president."[^29] A document from 1999 became the common basis cited by three DPP presidents.

The Taiwan independence party platform was not frozen, because freezing it requires a formal vote, and a formal vote means the party must answer a question it cannot answer: does the DPP still advocate Taiwan's independence? The Resolution on Taiwan's Future was also not formally replaced the Taiwan independence party platform, because "replacement" itself is a clear action, equivalent to acknowledging that the previous thirty years of state-building efforts were wrong. The party chose to do nothing, letting the ambiguity continue to operate.

The three drafters went their separate ways twenty-seven years later. Kuo Ching-yang withdrew from the DPP in 2023 and turned to political commentary on television, with a dramatic reversal of position that surprised his former comrades.[^22] Lin I-hsiung resigned from the legislature in 2006 due to Chen Shui-bian's corruption case, but remained in the party, continuing to comment on current affairs with the precision of the wording of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future, becoming the most unsparing internal critic within the party.[^23] Chen Chung-hsin quietly withdrew from the political arena after leaving his post as deputy secretary-general of the National Security Council. The three of them wrote the same document at the same table in 1999, and twenty-seven years later, the document they wrote was still the foundation of Taiwan's cross-strait discourse.

1. **October 1991** — The DPP passed the Taiwan independence party platform, advocating "establishing a Taiwan Republic," drafted by Lin I-hsiung
2. **September 1995** — Su Hsien-dung declared in Washington that "no need to and will not declare Taiwan's independence"
3. **March 1996** — Peng Ming-min failed with 21.13% of the vote, and the pressure for position transformation surged
4. **May 1999** — The Resolution on Taiwan's Future was passed in Kaohsiung, and "currently called the Republic of China under the constitution" became the new consensus
5. **March 2000** — Chen Shui-bian was elected president, achieving the first peaceful transfer of power
6. **September 2007** — The Normal State Resolution was passed, attempting to move toward legal independence, and was shelved after the devastating defeat in the next election
7. **May 2026** — Lai Ching-te defined the "two major meanings of Taiwan independence" based on the Resolution on Taiwan's Future, and Lin I-hsiung commented "very appropriate"

## "Very Appropriate"

May 17, 2026. The background was the international pressure after the Trump-Xi meeting that month: Trump said he hoped Taiwan would not move toward independence, and Beijing reiterated the one-China principle. Lai Ching-te's response strategy was to redefine "Taiwan independence" using the language of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future: Taiwan independence is not about declaring anything, Taiwan independence is the status quo — Taiwan does not belong to the People's Republic of China, and the Republic of China and the People's Republic of China are not subordinate to each other. This logic can be traced back to Su Hsien-dung's words in Washington in 1995. The basis cited by Lai Ching-te was the 1999 Resolution on Taiwan's Future and Tsai Ing-wen's "Four Principles" in 2021, emphasizing that these "are the national policies currently promoted by the DPP government."[^24]

Kuomintang's Jiang Wan-an asked: does the DPP want to delete the Taiwan independence party platform? DPP legislator Shen Bo-yang replied: "Now it's the Resolution on Taiwan's Future, Taiwan's future is determined by all 23 million people in Taiwan... He's not a lawyer, is he?"[^25]

On the same day, the presidential office's statement returned to the wording of "the Republic of China is a sovereign and independent democratic country." Lin I-hsiung wrote three characters on Facebook: "very appropriate." He said this made people "breathe a sigh of relief," and believed the government "finally got rid of the errors since 2020."[^26]

Twenty-seven years ago, Lin I-hsiung added two characters "currently" before the state name. Twenty-seven years later, he was still measuring every word of the government with the precision of these two characters.

Taiwan's 20-year-olds take it for granted that "Taiwan has always been independent." The Resolution on Taiwan's Future will not appear in their textbooks. They receive a packaged conclusion, and the struggles, compromises, and word battles behind it have long been flattened by twenty-seven years of time. They don't know that this common sense comes from a party representatives' meeting in Kaohsiung in 1999, from the wording that three people repeatedly pondered at the same table, from a character that was added: "currently." The greatest success of the Resolution on Taiwan's Future was making itself unnecessary to be remembered.
