> **30-Second Overview:** In April 1954, the Shimen Reservoir Design Committee was formed, bringing together water scarcity in the Taoyuan plain, post-war governmental financial difficulties, and American aid technology onto one engineering blueprint. On June 14, 1964, the reservoir was completed, becoming Taiwan's first multi-functional reservoir. It supplied irrigation and public water, but it also involved debates among engineers, contracts with foreign consultants, the relocation of residents from flooded areas, and the national decision of who would receive the water. This article treats the reservoir as a historical site where engineering, administration, and local life intersected.[^1] [^2] [^3]

In April 2017, a photographer stood along the Ring Road, aiming his camera at the Shimen Dam. The embankment of earth and rock rose from the valley, with the water spreading out behind the dam, and the road winding along the mountainside. What modern viewers see is a stable public facility; what they often fail to see is that it was once the subject of decades of debate: whether to build the dam, where the money came from, who would design it, how high it should be, and who would have to leave after the reservoir was filled.[^4]

What is counterintuitive about the Shimen Reservoir is that its completion in 1964 did not bring an end to the project. The reservoir began supplying irrigation, power generation, flood control, and public water; the engineering benefits entered daily life, while the land of the flooded areas, resettlement, and the environmental cost of the concrete dam remained local issues.[^1] [^3]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** This article does not portray the Shimen Reservoir merely as a dam completed in 1964. Instead, it traces the history of hydraulic modernization through design, borrowing, construction, resettlement, and archival review. Readers should pay attention to both the engineering benefits and the land costs, avoiding summarizing this entire period with a single narrative of success or failure.

## The Water of Taoyuan: Starting from the Thousand Ponds

The Taoyuan plain is not a flat plain naturally traversed by stable rivers. The National Archives Administration points out that much of the Taoyuan terrain belongs to higher plains, and early inhabitants developed many _pí-táng_ (small ponds) to store irrigation water, leading to the appellation "Town of Thousand Ponds." These ponds divided rainfall into small bodies of water, but the supply was still insufficient to support all agricultural land.[^1]

During the Japanese colonial period, official diversion from the Shimen Gorge built the Taoyuan Great Canal to expand irrigated area. The Construction History of the Water Resources Agency traces earlier concepts back to 1902, when civil engineer Teh Ch'ang-hsiung surveyed the Shimen area and proposed a reservoir concept. This concept did not immediately materialize. In 1928, the Taoyuan Great Canal was completed; the following year, Ya-tian and Yi mentioned the Shimen Great Weir in the "Showa Water Conservancy Project," bringing Shimen back into the purview of the Governor's Office.[^2]

This history is important because the Shimen Reservoir was not a sudden post-war "modernization miracle." It followed the hydrological surveys from the Japanese colonial era, the Taoyuan Great Canal, and the ideal of basin-wide management, while also continuing the experience of using _pí-táng_ to manage plain water scarcity from even earlier times. War and budget constraints halted the dam concepts from the Japanese period, but the problem did not disappear.

The Taoyuan Great Canal could only supply about 60% of the area's water needs. During droughts, farmers sometimes disputed over water diversion. The reservoir was re-proposed not just because engineers wanted to build a massive structure, but because the small ponds and canals could no longer independently meet the increasing agricultural and population demands.[^1]

## Starting from a Design Committee Roster

In April 1954, Director Chen Cheng of the Executive Yuan instructed the establishment of the Shimen Reservoir Design Committee. Members came from the Ministry of Economic Affairs, the Provincial Construction Bureau of Taiwan Province, the Water Resources Agency of Taiwan Province, the Taiwan Electric Power Company, and the China Rural Reconstruction Council. Professor Hsu Shih-tai of National Taiwan University served as the chief engineer, and foreign experts such as Savanci, Haimen, and Kondeng were hired to participate in the research. In May of the following year, the Design Committee submitted the "Shimen Reservoir Project Final Plan Report."[^1] [^2]

This roster reveals one thing: the reservoir was not a project of a single agency from the start. It involved agriculture, power generation, public finance, local water resources, and international aid simultaneously. The 1954 Design Committee had to deal with issues such as dam site selection, hydrology, engineering costs, subsequent repayment, construction equipment, and how technical consultants would be brought in.[^1]

The financial situation of post-war Taiwan made it difficult to support large hydraulic projects independently. Water Resources Agency records state that the total cost of the Shimen Reservoir was equivalent to approximately NT$3.39 billion, of which American aid loans accounted for about NT$1.902 billion (56.1%), non-American aid loans were about NT$1.097 billion (32.4%), and government funding amounted to about NT$0.39 billion (11.5%). The American aid funds had specific uses, which had to be spent on items such as American engineering consultants, permanent equipment, and construction machinery. This also indicates that the technical choices for the Shimen Reservoir were influenced by loan conditions; the project accounts could not be entirely separated from international politics.[^2]

American aid also brought new professional relationships. The Shimen Reservoir Construction Committee signed service contracts with U.S. companies Tiams and Mock, and American consultants participated in design, inspection, construction, and training. This made the Shimen Reservoir a cross-national technical cooperation field, and Taiwanese engineers learned new construction systems through collaboration and friction.[^2] [^5]

This form of cooperation also has an easily overlooked time scale. The services provided by consulting firms were concentrated during the design, inspection, and construction phases; the Construction Committee had to translate foreign technology into rules that local Taiwanese agencies could manage long-term. Engineers had to learn how to operate equipment, and they had to establish processes for documentation, contracts, testing, and acceptance. After the reservoir was completed, these processes did not disappear when the foreign consultants left, but remained in subsequent water resource administration and construction habits. This is where the influence of American aid cannot be measured by a single loan amount.[^2] [^4]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** American aid changed the ledgers of the reservoir, and it also changed the professional relationships regarding dam safety, efficiency, and subsequent investment.

## Dam Types Also Changed

When initially applying for American aid, the Shimen Reservoir proposed an arch dam type. After the design team signed a service contract with TAMS Company to re-evaluate the advantages and disadvantages of arch dams versus earth and rock dams, the collapse of the Marbassy Arch Dam in France in 1959, which killed over four hundred people, changed the safety assessment of the Shimen Reservoir. Construction was thus paused, and TAMS re-examined the design, ultimately changing from an arch dam to an earth and rock dam.[^1]

This turning point allows us to see another side of engineering history. Reservoirs in textbooks are often written as a straight line: survey, design, construction, completion. But on site, the dam type was not fixed from the beginning; safety accidents, international experience, consultant opinions, and administrative approvals all caused the plan to change course. The "completion" of large-scale projects is often built upon many moments where previous plans were deemed insufficient.

Engineering records preserved by the Water Resources Agency show that a preliminary construction ceremony was held on July 7, 1955. In July 1956, the Shimen Reservoir Construction Committee was officially established, and the water diversion tunnel project began. On August 5, 1958, the foundation-laying ceremony for the Shimen Dam was held. The water diversion tunnel was completed and put into service in December 1960; the Shimen Great Canal was completed and put into service in October 1963, with the final completion ceremony held on June 14, 1964.[^2]

The National Archives Administration records that the entire project required 7,500 workers over eight years. After the main dam was finished, the reservoir's water collection and storage projects also included the water diversion tunnel, spillway, power plant, tailwater weir, and Shimen Great Canal. The area of the valley inundated was about 8.15 square kilometers, with a length of approximately 16.55 kilometers, and a total storage capacity of 316 million cubic meters, with an effective storage capacity of 251 million cubic meters.[^1]

## Another Map of the Project: Flooded Areas and Resettlement Sites

The reservoir's engineering drawings show the dam body, tunnels, spillways, and power plant, but they do not necessarily depict the original residents of the valley with equal clarity. After the Academia Sinica organized the Shimen Reservoir Construction Committee files, it was noted that the project required land acquisition for over a thousand hectares; after filling, over 600 hectares of land and housing, involving more than 400 households and over 2,000 reservoir migrants, needed to be resettled.[^3]

These numbers cannot just be treated as engineering appendices. They represent a family potentially losing farmland, ancestral homes, neighbors, and familiar roads. The reservoir migrants were not relocated in one go. Research reports mention that some people were forced to move multiple times, having to redevelop land in coastal or riverine wasteland. Areas originally planned for resettlement might have already been cultivated, leading to new land conflicts between migrants and original cultivators.[^3] [^7]

In 1959, a conflict occurred at the Shulinzi resettlement site in Guanyin Township, Taoyuan. A report from Academia Sinica records that Zhao Jizheng, who was in charge of migration affairs, had an argument with the Wu family over land issues; the office was damaged, and the incident was later settled through mediation at the police station. The land originally cultivated by the Wu family was planned as a resettlement area, which the Construction Committee initially insisted on handling according to law, but due to the complexity of the county government leasing land and existing encroachment, the resettlement principles were re-examined, and the land was eventually returned to the Wu family.[^3]

This small incident shows that "migration" in the Shimen Reservoir is not just about people moved by the state. The resettlement policy involved, at minimum, reservoir migrants, farmers cultivating the original land, local government, courts, and the Construction Committee. Hydraulic engineering renamed a piece of land as a "resettlement area," but the existing life on that land did not automatically disappear because an official document changed its name.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** This article approaches migrant resettlement from a local history scale; it does not generalize the single Shulinzi incident to be the shared experience of all residents in flooded areas. What truly needs to be examined is how planning terminology encountered existing cultivators, and how compensation, land, and housing were redefined within administrative procedures.

## The American Dormitories and Engineer Life: Eleven Sections

The construction site of the Shimen Reservoir was not just the dam body and machinery. A research team from Academia Sinica found that engineers and their families formed a living community in the Shifen-Liudan area (Eleven sections) of Taoyuan. Foreign consultant dormitories had swimming pools, sports fields, and golf greens, as well as cafeterias, medical centers, kindergartens, and welfare associations. General employees and foreign consultants lived in different areas; the overall office floor plan showed nearly 300 households at the time.[^3]

These facilities constituted the living conditions for long-term cross-national engineering operations. Foreign consultants had to work in Taiwan, and Taiwanese engineers and administrators also had to live near the construction site. The Construction Committee files record a postal society, bridge plaque society, chess club, photography club, badminton team, volleyball team, and tennis team. The modernity of the project was not just in the dam height and storage capacity, but also in how a group of people was organized into a working and living community.[^3]

The files also contain traces of mutual evaluation between Taiwanese and American engineers. A status report on Mock Company personnel included performance evaluations of American engineers by senior Taiwanese engineers—some were described as "diligent and responsible," while others were criticized as "poor at command training," and some were recommended for repatriation due to rough temperaments. These writings transformed the narrative of "American aid technology transfer" from a one-way aid story into a working relationship that required negotiation, judgment, and adjustment.[^3]

## A Dam Height Debate Gives Voice to the Archives

From May 2020 to December 2021, a team from the Institute of History, Academia Sinica, stayed at the Northern Water Bureau for nineteen months, organizing over 7,000 files and hundreds of thousands of pages related to the Shimen Reservoir Construction. The digital images released in 2023 included 1,700 items, totaling about 140,000 pages. These numbers are important, but more importantly, the archives made visible the internal hesitation and debate within the project.[^3] [^4]

On November 29, 1954, Liu Yong-tuan, a representative from the Provincial Construction Bureau of Taiwan Province, attended a Shimen Reservoir Design Meeting. The official meeting minutes calmly recorded that the dam height of 250 meters was approved. However, Liu Yong-tuan's diary preserved another scene: "Today I went to the Shimen meeting again; the dam height was decided at 250 meters after fierce debate until three o'clock."[^3]

The same decision is institutional completion in the meeting minutes, but it is a debate lasting until three in the afternoon in the diary. Both are true, yet each retains a different history. Only by placing official documents, diaries, photographs, blueprints, and private letters together can one see a reservoir constructed from overlapping professional judgments, power dynamics, and personal emotions.

Research by Airiti points out that the Shimen Reservoir Construction Committee files mainly cover 1954 to 1966 and are among the few relatively complete national important hydraulic construction records from the post-war period. The organization of the research rebuilt the series and context of the archives based on source principles, allowing researchers not only to see photographs but also to know which agency originally produced the documents, how they were preserved, and their place in the overall project process.[^4]

## After Completion, Problems Did Not End

After completion in 1964, the Shimen Reservoir provided irrigation, power generation, flood control, and public water. The National Archives Administration called it Taiwan's first multi-functional reservoir, and the Water Resources Agency recorded that it was once the largest reservoir in East Asia. These achievements explain why the reservoir was so important in post-war national development history.[^1] [^2]

But "multi-functional" also meant that multiple demands had to be met by the same reservoir simultaneously. Agriculture needed irrigation, cities needed tap water, industry needed a stable supply, the power system needed electricity, and the Taipei Basin needed flood risk reduction; tourism turned the reservoir into a public landscape. Whenever there was insufficient water or increased siltation, these demands competed with each other. Reallocating limited water among different times and regions did not eliminate the conditions of uneven rainfall and limited water resources.

Research from Academia Sinica also reminds us that while the reservoir was once seen as a panacea for solving water and flood problems, its glory gradually faded after the rise of environmental movements. A more illustrative approach is to return to the conditions of the time, question why people believed dam construction could solve problems, and ask whose land and voices were not included in the early project narrative.[^3]

The reservoir is still operational today, but the archives allow us to revisit its unfinished years. This timeline starts from the unadopted concepts in 1902, goes through the Taoyuan Great Canal in 1928, the dam height debate in 1954, the American aid contracts in 1958, the resettlement conflict in 1959, and finally reaches the completion ceremony in 1964. Engineers, migrants, original cultivators, and families all left their mark on this map from different directions.

The hydraulic system of the Shimen Reservoir also leaves a sample of post-war national governance. The Design Committee first integrated various agencies; the Construction Preparatory Committee then handled finances and administrative preparations; only after the Construction Committee took over did long-term construction begin. After completion, the management committee took charge based on multi-objective operation standards. These organizational changes show that the reservoir was also an administrative experiment. Agriculture, power generation, urban water supply, flood risk, and local land were placed within a single decision framework; previously dispersed hydraulic problems were thus centralized for handling by central and professional institutions.[^1] [^2]

This framework improved engineering coordination efficiency but made it harder for local residents to participate directly in decisions. When the government marked the flooded areas, resettlement areas, and construction land separately on plans, residents had to fight for land and compensation through petitions, lawsuits, or local administrative relations. The value left by the Shimen Reservoir archives is that they show these procedures were not just "successfully completed." The dates, maps, petitions, and court exchanges in the documents allow us to see how a national project entered into family, land, and neighborhood relationships.[^3] [^7]

This is why the Shimen Reservoir deserves to be read alongside cultural landscapes and archival history. The reservoir changed the original relationship between _pí-táng_ and canals in the Taoyuan plain; new great canals, roads, settlements, and resettlement areas formed another set of living geographies. Later studies that only measure dam body, storage capacity, or water supply efficiency miss how residents reorganized cultivation, transportation, and kinship ties. The archives offer more than just an engineering evaluation. They allow different scales of change to be cross-referenced: the construction progress of the dam body, the decision-making process of agencies, the boundaries of farmland, the petitions of residents, and how a community reestablished life after relocation.[^4] [^6]

The Shimen Reservoir thus belongs to hydraulic history, local history, and archival history simultaneously. Readers can see national capacity from the dam, but they can also see people outside the engineering boundaries from the resettlement areas and petitions. Only by putting these two perspectives together can we prevent water supply benefits from overshadowing the cost of relocation, nor allow the cost to obscure the real water scarcity and flood problems people faced at the time.
