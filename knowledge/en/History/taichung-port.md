> **30-second overview:** On October 31, 1973, Taichung Port threw its first stone at Wuci's north dike. On October 31, 1976, the first phase was completed; six navy speedboats, three military vessels, and five merchant ships entered the new port, with about 300,000 people in attendance. The port was once expected to become a port city accommodating 500,000 people, yet by 1981 it had only about 210,000 residents. It successfully brought goods into central Taiwan and changed the lives of Wuci's old streets, sandbanks, and marine life. [^1] [^2] [^3]

## Before the First Stone Fell, Wuci Was Already a Port

Wuci was not a place that only connected with the sea after Taichung Port appeared. Local historical records preserved by the National Taiwan Library's Taiwan Historical Materials Collection show that Wuci already had sailing ships coming and going during the Qing Dynasty, with the market repeatedly shifting southward along the port's location. Residents fished, dried nets, built shelters, loaded and unloaded goods at the wharf, then entered shops on both sides of Wuci Road. A port is not a fixed line, but a place pushed and pulled by sediment, tides, and ships. This kind of change also means that port history must be read alongside engineering maps, local maps, and human migration patterns; otherwise, only a seemingly stable coastal facility remains — one that has been continuously reshaped. [^5]

Taiwan Port's historical development also preserves another place-name clue. Wuci was anciently called "Zhu Fa Xue," and because residents mostly lived by fishing, it was also known as "Wang Gu Li Ao." These names are not mere tourist guide decorations; they show that the original port city was composed of small boats, fishing nets, and local markets, later reimagined as an international port capable of accommodating large merchant ships. [^2]

The boats, shoreline, and buildings in the background of this photo belong to a different era than the Qing Dynasty's sailing ship market of Wuci Port. Yet "port" remains a way of life, not merely an entry and exit facility for large vessels. Taichung Port connected Wuci to the global shipping system, while Wuci Fishing Port preserved the close relationships between people and boats, fish and goods, and neighborhoods. The coexistence of these two ports is precisely evidence that the local port city was not entirely replaced by a single project. [^2] [^5] [^11]

In the 1930s, the Japanese colonial government included Wuci in the "New Gaoping Port" plan, attempting to develop commercial, industrial, and fishing ports simultaneously. The war halted the project. In 1946 after the war, the Taichung Port Construction Office was established to prepare for rebuilding; Wuci briefly resumed trade with Wenzhou, Fuzhou, Xiamen, Quanzhou, and Shantou. However, financial difficulties and the cross-Strait tensions after 1949 caused the rebuilding to be suspended again. The port's earliest failure was not due to insufficient engineering technology, but because it was paused by national finance and geopolitics. [^1] [^3] [^5]

> The starting point of Taichung Port was not a grand blueprint, but a memory of a port city repeatedly pushed away from its original position by sediment and politics.

## Central Taiwan Needs a Door; the Question Is Where to Open It

In the 1960s, Taiwan's import and export trade grew rapidly, and Keelung Port and Kaohsiung Port gradually became congested. According to the historical development of Taichung Port Branch, the government launched a new port site survey in 1968, completed the report the following year, and the Executive Yuan approved Taichung Port as a comprehensive international new port combining commerce, industry, and fishing. This decision placed Wuci from a local port city into the island-wide resource allocation issue. [^2]

The construction history compiled by the Archives Administration shows that in 1966, two United Nations port consultants visited Taiwan in turn. The consultants believed Keelung was constrained by topography with limited expansion space, making the development of a new port necessary. Local elites not only argued that Taichung needed a port but also put forward reasons regarding cargo volume, land prices, hinterland, and power supply in central Taiwan, attempting to prove that building Taichung Port was an issue of north-south balance across the island. [^1]

There is a detail often obscured by the narrative of the Ten Major Construction Projects. Taichung Port was not simply a port placed on the map by the central government; local figures also organized discussions, submitted research, and lobbied. This does not mean that locals subsequently controlled the direction of development, but rather that the birth of the port simultaneously contained national planning and local demands, and the two did not entirely overlap. [^1] [^5]

On February 1, 1971, the Taichung Port Engineering Bureau was established. On October 31, 1973, Deputy Director Chen Shuhsi of the Taiwan Province Taichung Port Construction Committee threw the first stone on the north dike, officially beginning the main construction. This action was later recorded as the groundbreaking ceremony, but it actually marked a landform transformation: from that day on, the sandbank was no longer merely a place waiting for ships to dock, but an engineering site that had to be rearranged with breakwaters, channels, and piers. [^1] [^2]

## Engineers Are Not Building a Wall at Sea

The hardest part of Taichung Port was not drawing the pier on the nautical chart, but keeping a port on the western sandbank usable. The Archives Administration's special article on port construction points out that the port area had to face the northeasterly monsoon, tidal differences, and sand drift. The three phases of construction built a total of 14,045 meters of breakwaters and 27 deep-water piers. These numbers are not simply a competition of scale; they show that the port had to first isolate the waves and sediment within a controllable range before ships could enter. [^1]

The port construction adopted caissons. Caissons can be manufactured on land first, then towed to the sea for positioning, like building blocks forming breakwaters. Because the construction season each year was limited, the construction team did not simply push outward along the shoreline, but adopted a "island-hopping" construction method: placing isolated caissons in the sea first, then connecting them outward from multiple points. This method negotiated the construction rhythm with the monsoon season, rather than pretending the monsoon did not exist. [^1]

The Transportation Research Institute of the Ministry of Transportation shows that the sand drift and flying sand around Taichung Port were not old problems that disappeared after port construction. The study analyzed long-term terrain data and on-site monitoring of siltation, erosion, flying sand volume, and air quality around the port area. The abstract points out that sand barriers, windbreak forests, and woven sand fences can stabilize flying sand within designated areas, reducing the chance of entering channels and port pools; however, these facilities also mean that the port must invest in long-term maintenance. [^8]

Another 2022 hydrological model study simulated the changes in sand drift inside and outside the port after the completion of the remote outer protective dike. The study observed that sediment accumulated on the north side of the north breakwater, scouring pits appeared on the west side of the dike, and the southern area showed slight scouring. These results cannot directly replace a complete engineering evaluation, but they are enough to remind readers that breakwaters are not simply closing the door on the waves; they change the path of the sand. [^7]

## 1976: 300,000 People Came to See a New Port Open

On October 31, 1976, Taichung Port's first phase was completed and navigation was officially opened. The opening ceremony video preserved by the National Cultural Memory Database makes this date more than just a line in an engineering timeline. The video records six navy speedboats performing water stunts first, followed by fire-fighting ships leading the celebratory fleet; three military vessels and five merchant ships loaded with corn, groceries, and cloth slowly entered the port. [^3]

There was a political ceremony aspect to the event. The opening was arranged as a tribute to Chiang Kai-shek's 90th birthday, and meritorious personnel were awarded letters of appreciation. However, the same scene also contained memories of the crowd that were difficult to fully control. Archives and videos show that about 300,000 people poured into the port area that day, causing traffic congestion for several hours; within three days of opening for public visits, about 500,000 people visited. This port was both a stage for the nation to display modernization and a public site where central Taiwan residents first approached a large-scale port engineering project. [^1] [^3]

The first phase of the project had seven piers, with an annual planned throughput of 2.8 million tons. The subsequent second and third phase projects continued until 1983, when the first stage of construction was considered complete. Port data also placed the 1976 opening within a longer institutional transition: Taichung Port was opened to private investment for warehouse and loading and unloading operations, becoming an early case of port operations introducing privatization. [^2] [^3]

This shows that "port opening" is not completion, but the beginning of the transition from construction to operations. Ports need cargo sources, shipping routes, warehouses, loading and unloading operators, management regulations, and hinterland industries. Without these, no matter how long the breakwater is, it is just an expensive coastal shape. [^2]

## Why the Port City Dream Did Not Grow According to the Blueprint

The 1972 Taichung Port Special Zone Plan included Wuci, Qingshui, Shalu, and Longjing in a port city development vision, aiming to create a modern port city that could accommodate 500,000 people. This kind of planning believed that ports would bring factories, housing, roads, and service industries, and that population would move in along with industry. [^1]

However, the data compiled by the Archives Administration shows that the actual population of the special zone in 1981 was about 210,000, still far from the goal of 500,000 people. The port was completed, but the port city did not grow at the speed shown on the map. This is not simply a planning failure; it also shows that the economic benefits of a port do not automatically distribute to every neighborhood. [^1]

The local articles of the National Taiwan Library's Taiwan Historical Materials Collection describe this gap more closely to life. During the port construction process, sea sand was used to fill in farmland and graves east of Wuci Cultural Road, forming a new development zone. After the town hall, household registration office, police station, and market successively moved out, the population and crowd of Wuci's old street moved outward; the original wharf and river channel were also changed. The old street did not recover its former prosperity with the appearance of a large port; instead, it lost its central position between the new development zone and the port city imagination. [^5]

The port brought new business, and also new controls. The library's records show that in the mid-1970s, Wuci and Qingshui formed an imported goods street, with goods entering the market through sailors who docked at Taichung Port or via home delivery. During the martial law period, shop owners might also face political surveillance, tax, health, and state monopoly inspections. This history cannot simply be written as "the port brought prosperity," because the flow of prosperous goods was simultaneously framed by foreign exchange, imports, and political security rules. [^5]

> A port can make goods more easily cross the sea, but it does not guarantee that the crowd of the old street will return along the same road.

## After the 921 Earthquake: From Heroic Project to Maintenance Site

The 1999 921 Earthquake caused the Taichung Port piers to sink, and the grain conveyor belt and other facilities were also damaged. If the history of the port stops at the 1976 opening, it would mistake the infrastructure for a one-time work. Archive data shows that after the earthquake, the port area continued to rebuild, and business and loading and unloading equipment were gradually restored. [^1]

The Archives Administration's article describes this reconstruction with a contrasting number: the initial cargo throughput of the port was about 6,000 tons, and over the decade after the earthquake, it reached more than 30 million tons per year. This number is suitable for understanding the long-term change in port scale, but it cannot be directly inferred that all nearby residents benefited equally. Between the increase in cargo volume and the improvement of local life, more data on industry, employment, pollution, transportation, and land distribution is needed to make a distinction. [^1]

Taichung Port's system also changed. In 2012, the original Taichung Port Authority was reorganized into the Taichung Port Branch of Taiwan Port Company Limited, and the port moved from government agency management into the business operation framework of a state-owned company group. This is not merely changing the signboard, but a redivision of labor between maritime administration, public power, and operational efficiency. [^2] [^3]

The increase in port cargo volume does not mean that all local industries rose along the same curve. Wuci's documents record the rise and fall of the imported goods street: from about 20 shops in the mid-1970s, to about 200 shops at its peak in 1975-1976, and then to about 30 shops still operating in 2010. This rise and fall is related to outbound tourism, foreign exchange controls, import liberalization, and industrial exodus. The port brought the world to the street; when the rules of the world market changed, it also took away the street that relied on scarce goods to survive. [^5]

Therefore, the local history of Taichung Port cannot simply be summarized as "the port brought prosperity." To port authorities, the port is throughput, piers, and shipping routes. To old street shop owners, the port may be tea leaves, clothing, and groceries brought ashore by sailors. To merchants during the martial law period, the port may also mean tax inspections, political surveillance, and the risk of goods being detained. People standing on the same port see different worlds. [^2] [^5]

The port also continues to face sand. The Transportation Research Institute's report points out that the flying sand and drifting sand in the Taichung Port siltation area need monitoring, sand fixing, and port pool maintenance. Taiwan Port Company's recent engineering pages also listed the comprehensive management of drifting and flying sand as an ongoing port area task. The port is not something that starts operating after excluding nature; it continuously pays the maintenance cost of natural conditions during operation. [^8] [^9]

## When "Land Reclamation" Becomes Present Tense Again

In 2026, Public Television's "Our Island" reported on Taichung Port's new fill area dike engineering. Port company public data shows that the project budget is NT$104.63 million, the schedule runs from May 31, 2023 to September 2027, planning to complete 50 hectares of land reclamation, and using caissons, dikes, dredging, and filling to provide space for energy facilities and port earthwork. [^4] [^6]

The Public Television report pulls the question back to the sea outside the port. The Taiwan white dolphin is an endemic subspecies of the western coast; Taichung Port has been reported as an important activity zone. Private groups question whether the fill project involves the white dolphin habitat, and put forward different legal and environmental arguments regarding the environmental impact assessment's determination of "within the existing breakwater." The port authority says that the land reclamation area is within the existing breakwater and belongs to port area engineering. Both sides' statements should be preserved here, because the report itself presents a controversy in progress, not a completed judicial decision. [^4]

The historical prick is that in the 1970s, Taichung Port used breakwaters and caissons to turn the coast into a manageable port area; in the 2020s, it used dikes and caissons to deal with energy transition, dredging earthwork, and land needs. The engineering vocabulary looks similar, but society's questions have changed. Earlier, the main question was "can it be built"; now, we also need to ask "who bears the cost after it is built," "which species and lives are counted in the plan," and "at what stage should environmental procedures occur." [^1] [^4] [^6]

This does not mean that the early projects were only destructive, nor does it mean that all environmental questions today will automatically negate the port. A more accurate statement is that Taichung Port has condensed Taiwan's modernization issues onto a coastline: the state needs energy, shipping, and industrial hinterland; the local area needs jobs, transportation, and living space; the coast is not a blank land, but an environment with sediment, fish, fisheries, and long-term memories. [^4] [^5] [^8]

This coastline also has another time scale. Port data connects the 1976 opening, the increase in cargo handling volume in 2010, and the 2012 corporatization into a business history. Local historical data places the Qing Dynasty's sailing ships, the brief post-war trade, the rise and fall of the imported goods street, and the population outflow of the old street on another life history. Both timelines are true, but they do not naturally merge into "the project was successful." The former answers whether the port can continue to operate; the latter asks whether the port's operation allows the original neighborhood to maintain its position. [^2] [^5]

If you only look at the opening ceremony, Taichung Port looks like the state bringing the world in. If you look again at the old street and sandbanks, it also looks like the state rearranging who can approach the sea, which land can be filled, and which industries are worth keeping. This is not about attributing all consequences to a single project, but about recognizing that the completion of a large-scale project is often just the date when different interests begin to coexist. [^1] [^4] [^5]

## Looking at a Port, Not Just the Cargo Ships

The history of Taichung Port can begin with the first stone thrown in 1973, or it can begin with the wharf of Wuci's Qing Dynasty. The former allows us to see how state projects connect sandbanks to international trade; the latter reminds us that ports have never been places first named by engineers. There were fishermen, cargo shop owners, changing channels, and lives altered by sediment. [^2] [^5]

The 1976 opening ceremony packaged the port as a door to the world. 300,000 people attended, merchant ships honked, and military vessels and fire-fighting ships lined up to enter the port. But whether the door can truly be opened depends on whether goods, systems, industries, and local areas can receive it together. The blueprint for a port city of 500,000 people was not fully realized, and the old street did not recover its original appearance with the new port. [^1] [^3] [^5]

Today, looking at Taichung Port, we can first look at the breakwaters, then the sand. The breakwaters tell us how humans fix the coast; the sand tells us that the coast has never truly been fixed. Looking further outward, if we can see the issue of white dolphins, we will understand that Taichung Port is not a completed Ten Major Construction Project, but a public project that is still deciding how to use the sea. [^4] [^7] [^8]

We can also take another look at Wuci Street outside the port area. The roads, piers, and dikes inside the port bring the large scale into the local area; the temples, old houses, and shop fronts of the old street preserve the time of small scales. There is no need to arrange them as if one represents progress and the other represents backwardness. They together show that infrastructure will leave new paths, and also make the old paths harder to see. If a historical article only preserves the port area, it will miss those who were not written into the project completion report. [^1] [^5] [^6]

> **📝 Curator's note**
> The most memorable thing about Taichung Port is not that humans finally turned the sea into a port, but that after the port was built, humans still had to learn to live with the sea.

## Extended Reading

Taichung Port is still worth reading along three lines. The first is Wuci's local history, tracking how the port location, old streets, wharves, and imported goods streets changed. The second is engineering history, reading breakwaters, caissons, sand drift models, and earthquake reconstruction. The third is coastal governance, comparing how port engineering, white dolphin habitat, and environmental impact assessment put forward different marine usage imaginations.
