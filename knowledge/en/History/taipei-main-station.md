> **30-Second Overview:** The 1891 Taipei Station was a ferry terminal near the riverbank, while the fourth-generation system activated in 1989 moved the railway underground. Today, TRA, Taiwan High Speed Rail, Taipei Metro, and Taoyuan Airport MRT all converge here; however, what is most worth seeing about Taipei Main Station is not how quickly it allows people to leave, but how people repeatedly transform this transportation space into a place for lingering, gathering, dining, and debating publicness.[^1] [^2] [^3]

In 1891, the railway promoted by Liu Ming-chuan established Taiwan's first station on the banks of the Tamsui River. Early records also described it as a "ferry terminal" near Dazhong. This name reveals that the railway was not initially an isolated transport facility but was connected to waterways, cargo, and the flow of movement at the city's periphery.[^1] [^9] [^16]

This starting point is far removed from today's checkerboard lobby. The station first functioned as a peripheral facility before gradually becoming a face of the city. Each transformation it underwent leaves traces of shifting urban centers.[^1] [^3]

## Four Generations, Four Rewritings of the City

| Generation | Time and Location                                                      | Urban Clues Left Behind                                                                       |
| :--------- | :--------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| First Gen  | 1891, near Tamsui River and Dazhong area                               | The starting point of the ferry terminal and railway operations[^1] [^16]                     |
| Second Gen | Japanese colonial period, moved to the north end of Guanqian Road area | The station became the urban face on the north side of Taipei Prefecture City[^3] [^16]       |
| Third Gen  | Completed between 1939–1941                                            | Post office, restaurants, and concrete station building entered the station[^1] [^16]         |
| Fourth Gen | Activated September 2, 1989                                            | Ground-level rail moved underground, forming today's vertical transfer system[^1] [^16] [^24] |

In 1901, with the main line rerouted and the Tamsui Line opened, the station moved to the area of Guanqian Road and became "Taipei Station." The Taipei Gazette described this station as having a brick structure, tiered waiting areas, and VIP lounges. At the time, "Station" was used for the building, not for parking automobiles.[^3] [^10]

![2004 年的臺北車站與臺北車站地下街入口](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/e/eb/TRA_Taipei_Station_%26_Taipei_Station_Underground_Mall_20041227.jpg/1280px-TRA_Taipei_Station_%26_Taipei_Station_Underground_Mall_20041227.jpg)

_Image: Taipei Main Station and Taipei Station Underground Mall in 2004; Author: Loren36; Licensed under Public domain; Original page: [^25]_

The Third Generation Taipei Station, completed in 1939, featured a Modernist style facing Guanqian Road. Historical data provided by users describes it as a block-like concrete building mixing Eclecticism and Modernism, noting that the station housed a post office and restaurants. These functions indicate that the station was no longer just a place to board or alight, but had begun to function as a complex space handling communication, dining, and daily traffic.[^1] [^16]

The disappearance of the Third Generation building is an often-overlooked cost in Taipei's modernization. In 1979, the central government approved the undergroundization project for the urban rail network in Taipei City. The Third Generation building was demolished in 1986, and passengers used temporary stations. On September 2, 1989, the Fourth Generation underground station building was activated.[^1] [^24]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The history of Taipei Main Station is not about "one building being replaced by another," but a record of how the city continuously re-decides "how people should move."

## After the Railway Went Underground

Undergroundization cannot be glossed over with just the word "modernization." For engineering, it means the integration of routes, platforms, tunnels, and floors. For users, it is a bodily judgment: "Which floor am I on?" and "Is the exit on the other side?" The railway no longer cuts directly through the ground city; instead, it transforms what was once an immediately readable path into a three-dimensional space that must be read using signage, floors, and transfer logic.[^2] [^24]

Another study of underground spatial complexity views Taipei Main Station as a complex area requiring navigation cues and spatial clues, using the Taipei Railway Station as a case study for visual guidance information systems.[^2] [^23] Therefore, getting lost is not necessarily just individual carelessness; it can also be the cognitive load generated by decades of engineering, different transport systems, and commercial spaces stacked within the same underground cross-section.

Today's Taipei Main Station is more than just a TRA station. Taoyuan International Airport's official transfer information lists A1 Taipei Station as a major urban node for the Airport MRT, with direct trains taking about 35 minutes to Terminal 1 and regular trains taking about 49 minutes. The official page also lists pre-check-in services in the city.[^18]

This means the station not only serves the function of "transferring after arrival" but also brings some airport procedures into the urban area. Passengers can transfer here to Taipei City, other cities in Northern Taiwan, or for their next domestic journey. Commuters, workers, shoppers, and those waiting all use the same main lobby.[^18]

Official TRA data describes the traffic scale of Taipei Station as over 500,000 daily passengers, placing it alongside Osaka Station as a transportation hub. This number makes the station look like a massive machine, but human flow is not water: people stop in the lobby, wander in the underground mall, and reconfirm their direction at an exit.[^19]

## How One Station Becomes an Underground City

Ground-level stations are easily seen; what truly makes North Station "North Station" is the invisible segregation, equipment, and joint management. The Joint Disaster Prevention Center for Taipei Main Station, activated in 2019, integrates disaster information, equipment control, and emergency response for specific areas of Taipei Main Station, involving different operating units such as TRA, High Speed Rail, Taipei Metro, Taoyuan Airport MRT, the underground mall, and department stores.[^8]

![臺北車站樓層圖](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/79/Taipei_Main_Station_Floor_Map.gif)

_Image: Taipei Main Station Floor Map; Author: Tsungyenlee; Licensed under [CC BY 2.5](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.5/); Original page: [^26]_

The floor map turns "North Station is complicated" from a feeling into observable data. It arranges different floors, platforms, transfer areas, and exits on one long sheet, allowing users to see that they are not lost in a corridor, but changing levels within a vertically stacked transportation system. This image also reminds us that finding the way is not an incidental task before arrival, but part of the station's user experience.[^2] [^26]

The underground mall around Taipei Main Station is not a single, homogeneous corridor. A 2008 study on the Taipei Underground Mall, the station forecourt underground mall, and the Xinyi District underground mall observed users, commercial facilities, and public activities in different underground malls, pointing out that transportation systems, types of commerce, spatial dimensions, and ground land use all influence usage patterns.[^20]

The research also recorded that wide walkways and stairwells in the underground mall are not just for passage but can become places for hobby groups to gather and interact. Another study interprets the underground mall as an urban downward expansion formed after the railway went underground and the MRT network was built: transportation, commerce, management, and leisure activities were all buried within the city.[^20] [^21]

This perspective refuses to treat the underground mall as a mere appendage of the station. Some people just pass through; some use it as a shopping route; others conduct social lives unrelated to transport within it. Planners design flow lines, but users actually live out places.[^17] [^20]

Underground space does not always conform perfectly to planning. Research has observed teenagers using the mall's plazas and passages for street dance practice, forming a kind of resistance to established spatial use. This resistance is not always expressed through slogans; it might just be several people stopping simultaneously in an area originally intended only for passage.[^21]

📝 **Curator's Note:** What is most worth seeing in the underground mall is not the list of shops, but who can turn "passing by" into "being here." In that moment, the transportation space begins to possess social memory.

## The History of Transport Did Not Disappear After West Station Closed

If only looking at the main station building, the story of Taipei Main Station seems too neat. The adjacent Taipei West Station was built in 1954 and functioned as a parallel transport hub to Taipei Main Station. The Kingma, Kinglung, and Kuo-kang trains all departed from there. The opening of the highway brought a peak period, and the introduction of High Speed Rail changed its original function.[^4]

After West Station was demolished in 2016, the site was planned as parkland and new waiting facilities. A building with 62 years of history was removed, leaving behind memories of people traveling home for Chinese New Year, platforms, and waiting benches.[^4]

The disappearance of West Station reminds us that the life of a transport building is determined not only by its appearance but also by new speeds and routes. Looking back from West Station to the main station, Taipei Main Station was never an unchanging urban face, but merely the generation currently in use.

## The Lobby: Corridor, Exhibition Hall, or Living Room?

Perhaps the hardest place at Taipei Main Station to define is not the platform, but the lobby. CCTV reported that the lobby was originally designed as a multi-functional exhibition space and later evolved into a cultural area where people sat on the floor when there were no events.[^5]

This transformation created a gap in spatial use: designers envisioned an open venue for events, while passengers and residents used it as a public indoor plaza to wait, rest, and gather. When seating was insufficient, the checkerboard flooring became not just decoration, but a surface where people could linger without consuming anything.[^6] [^7]

In 2012, Indonesian migrant workers gathered in the lobby during Eid al-Adha. When it rained, crowds flooded indoors, and station staff installed red ropes and notices citing maintaining flow lines and order, provoking protests from human rights groups regarding discriminatory use of public space. The following year, the station adopted flexible persuasion, pre-planned flow lines, and enhanced cleaning, temporarily shifting the conflict toward coordination.[^5] [^7] [^22]

During the 2020 pandemic, TRA prohibited people from sitting in the lobby. Reports documented that this measure affected migrant workers who used the lobby for gatherings and clashed with the traditions of Muslim migrant workers holding Eid al-Adha celebrations there.[^6] [^22]

The controversy quickly shifted from epidemic prevention measures to a debate over public space. In 2012 and 2013, civil groups had held picnics in the Taipei Main Station lobby. After the 2020 ban on sitting, actions reasserting the right to use the lobby through activities like silent sitting and picnicking appeared online.[^22]

The controversy is so striking because the lobby appears to be the most impersonal place: checkerboard floor, large open space, signage, and moving people. Yet, precisely because it is used by many, it becomes a meeting point where different life rhythms collide. The holidays of migrant workers, the waiting of passengers, and the considerations of evacuation by managers are not three unrelated stories, but different demands on the same lobby.[^5] [^6] [^22]

A design thesis based on Taipei Main Station and its surrounding underground mall describes it as an underground space connecting mass transit, old commerce, and new public leisure, also discussing how the gathering of migrant workers and homeless people challenges the formal urban image envisioned by planners.[^21] [^23]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The true entrance to Taipei Main Station is not just on the ground, nor just on the map. It is in every moment someone asks, "Can I sit here?"

## Bento: Packing a Journey into a Box

![60 元臺鐵排骨便當](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/d/dc/60%E5%85%83%E8%87%BA%E9%90%B5%E6%8E%92%E9%AA%A8%E4%BE%BF%E7%95%B6_%2813344634153%29.jpg)

_Image: 60 Yuan TRA Pork Ribs Bento; Photo by hirotomo t; Licensed under [CC BY-SA 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/2.0/); Original page: [^15]_

Food at a railway station is not just sustenance for travelers who are hungry on the road. The Taiwan Guanghua report, which featured staff pushing carts to sell bento boxes on trains, is considered a shared memory for many Taiwanese people. The lunchbox, the train window, and the scenery along the way combine to give the journey a memorable shape.[^12]

The history of TRA bento is not based on one unchanging recipe. Data compiled by StoryStudio indicates that first-class dining cars were available on express trains in 1912. After WWII, in 1949, TRA produced and sold bento boxes from railway restaurants at stations like Kaohsiung, Tainan, Taichung, Taipei, and Songshan, establishing the "Small Camp" department to integrate related sales in 1960.[^13]

The bento evolved from dining service on platforms and trains into a product managed by the railway system itself. Like the station, it possesses both transport and life functions. What passengers buy is not just calories; it is a ritual of "I am departing."[^13]

Taipei Main Station is one place where this ritual occurs intensively. A 2013 report in Taiwan Guanghua recorded queues forming at the TRA bento shop near East Gate Two of Taipei Main Station by 11 AM. Some bought them to catch a High Speed Rail or train, some ate nearby, and others specifically bought them to share with family members.[^14]

The pork ribs bento survived not just because of the ribs themselves. The Guanghua report records that TRA bento containers changed from thin wooden boxes, aluminum tins, stainless steel boxes, foil packages, to paper boxes. Only after the plastic food container ban in 2003 did they fully switch to octagonal, round, and oval paper and wood-material boxes. The shape of the box thus became part of the passenger's memory: opening it reveals not just the dishes, but the way a generation traveled by train.[^14]

A 2020 report noted that TRA pork ribs bento made in Taipei Railway Restaurants is made with Taiwanese rice, and the ribs undergo processes like pounding, marinating, deep-frying, and braising. These details show that railway bento must be suitable for movement, waiting, and sharing to truly become station culture, rather than just a dish in a restaurant.[^12]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The station sends people somewhere else, but the bento packs the homely flavor of departure into the carriage. It is the smallest station that starts operating when you open the lid.

## How One Station Preserves Its Memory

Participants in the 2024 TRA cultural reading tour entered the archives, which are normally closed to the public, and saw items such as old tickets, electrical signal blockers, manual exchanges, and train operation manuals. A docent said: "Every object, when stored, connects stories on its own; that is what history is."[^9]

This statement changed our way of viewing stations. The roof, platforms, and exits are large-scale history. An obsolete hard ticket or an unused telephone exchange makes the system's operation tangible.[^9]

The current station structure is nearly square, with three doors on each side, totaling twelve doors. Some ground areas have compasses, and South Gate Two has a secondary escape function due to inward expansion. These details would not appear in the four words "transport hub," but they explain how the building embeds direction, segregation, and safety into daily actions.[^9]

Taipei Station also preserves the layers of its surrounding city. Leaving the station area, one can reach North Gate, the National Taiwan Museum, the Railway Park, the 228 Peace Park, and Zhongshan Hall. The station is not an isolated box but an entrance connecting different regimes, different transport technologies, and different urban memories.[^2] [^9]

## Stations Also Determine Where a City Grows

The location of Taipei Station influenced more than just where trains stopped. During the Qing dynasty, the station was near the river ditch street and Dazhong to facilitate unloading cargo from the Tamsui River and allowing officials access to Taipei City by water. Later route changes caused the city's development focus to shift.[^9]

This detail transforms "station as a transport node" into a larger question: transportation nodes, in turn, create cities. When the railway no longer proceeds along its original planned direction, the relationship between Xinzhuang, Banqiao, and West Taipei today is reordered over decades.[^9]

When the station was located near the current site in 1901, the Guanqian Road area was designated as the city entrance. Today, North Gate, the National Taiwan Museum, the Railway Park, and Zhongshan Hall still form a walkable urban cross-section around the station.[^2] [^3] [^9]

Therefore, the "center" of Taipei Station is not just inside the station. It pulls outward to roads, underground malls, old city gates, museums, and commercial areas, while inward it compresses passengers of different speeds into one lobby. People pass through quickly here, and they also see how Taipei stacks its past onto its present.[^9]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The deepest influence of a station is often not how many people it carries, but how many people it makes start to consider a place as a "center."

## The Next Version of North Station

The future of Taipei Main Station cannot only be asked in terms of whether it will be faster, brighter, or more enjoyable. It must also ask: after transfer efficiency improves, who can still linger here? After the mall expands, does anyone without consumption still have a seat? After the space becomes cleaner, are some people's traces of life also cleaned away? These questions have repeatedly appeared in records of lobby prohibitions on sitting, migrant worker gatherings, and underground mall usage.[^5] [^6] [^7] [^22]

TRA established the Joint Disaster Prevention Center for Taipei Main Station in 2019, involving TRA, High Speed Rail, Taipei Metro, Taoyuan Airport MRT, as well as the underground mall and department stores. A drill that year involved about 25 units and about 430 people. These numbers show that North Station's underground city is too large to be managed by the intuition of a single station manager or one company.[^8]

The ferry terminal near the river in 1891, the "station" moving the city gate to Guanqian Road in 1901, and the Fourth Generation station hiding the railway underground in 1989; West Station concluded its mission in the High Speed Rail era. Each renovation made Taipei easier to pass through, and also made "staying around" something that needed more discussion.[^1] [^3] [^4]

So, what is most worth remembering about Taipei Main Station is not how many lines it has, how many exits it has, or what color the next renovation will be. What it truly preserves is a city's contradiction: **people need a place to leave quickly, and they also need a place to sit down before leaving.**
