On January 27, 1949, the Taiping set sail from Shanghai toward Keelung. That same night, it collided with the Jianguo off the Zhoushan Islands. Keelung city's official records list 932 victims, with only about 50 survivors. [^1]

In 1950, the bereaved families' aftermath committee initiated the erection of a memorial stele at the intended destination. It was not until 2018 that the stele was moved out of the military zone, becoming accessible to the general public. [^1] [^2] The most counterintuitive aspect of the Taiping is not that it sank outside of Taiwan, but that although it never actually arrived, it left behind a memorial stele, a park, and a memory about "who is becoming Taiwanese."

## A Ship Bound for the "Shore of Refuge"

This was not an ordinary journey. In 1949 Shanghai, the war was closing in, and the Spring Festival was approaching. The Taiping, bound for Keelung, became the vessel for many who left behind their families, fleeing the flames of war and seeking a new place to start their lives. Keelung city's official records refer to Taiwan as the "shore of refuge" in the eyes of these passengers. [^2]

The ticketed capacity was 508, but the ship also carried crew members, ticketless passengers, and a large amount of cargo. A historical article by the Shanghai Civil Affairs Bureau records that the Taiping had been making round trips between Shanghai and Keelung since 1948, completing 35 voyages before sinking. The final voyage, driven by a surge in demand before the Spring Festival, saw the actual number of passengers far exceeding the ticketed capacity. [^3]

The cargo was not as simple as passenger luggage. Different reports mention that the ship carried Chinese medicinal materials, hardware, and antiques destined for Taiwan by merchants, as well as printing equipment for newspapers and government files. For some, the ship was not just carrying refugees, but was dismantling a city on the verge of collapse into boxes, ready to be moved to another port. [^3] [^4]

📝 Curator's note: The Taiping was not an abstract arrow "from China to Taiwan." It was a ship that squeezed people, goods, documents, and unfinished family lives into its hold all at once.

This overloading later became part of the liability pursuit. Data compiled by the People's Political Consultative Conference website records that courts in Shanghai and Taipei once heard compensation claims from families against the Zhonglian Company, but under conditions of currency chaos, prolonged conflicts, and the company's inability to pay, compensation enforcement was extremely difficult. [^4]

The consequences of the accident were therefore not just a death list. They also included documents scattered across different legal systems in Shanghai and Taiwan, many unrecovered bodies, and family sacrifices that had to be maintained by the bereaved themselves. [^4] [^5] This is also why the history of the Taipei cannot be left to a single accident report: a report can explain how the ship sank, but it will not automatically explain how people continued to live after the ship sank.

## Disaster Is Not a Single Moment

Regarding the sinking time and casualties, different sources show statistical discrepancies. A 2005 Taipei Times report, based on contemporary newspaper data, records that after selling all 508 tickets, the ship carried approximately 1,500 passengers, that the Taiping turned off its navigation lights at night and was struck by the Jianguo at around 23:25, and that of the 1,500 passengers, only 37 survived. [^5]

The lengthy compilation by the People's Political Consultative Conference website, based on data from Zhejiang's Dongnan Daily, records 508 ticketed passengers, 124 crew members, and approximately 300 ticketless individuals, citing the final official figures of 36 rescued and 932 dead. [^4]

These numbers cannot be forced into a seemingly precise answer. Official memorial pages, news reports, and later family research may use different statistical scopes: only Taiping passengers, including crew, or also counting the crew of the Jianguo. What can be determined is that this was a maritime disaster that cost hundreds, even close to a thousand lives. We cannot pretend that all sources have never diverged. [^1] [^4] [^5]

The official page states the cause as overloading and night sailing without navigation lights, and records that the Taiping collided with the Jianguo, which was carrying coal and timber, near Baijie Mountain. [^1] The People's Political Consultative Conference website further places overloading, delays, night sailing under curfew, and crew duty issues into the context of the accident. [^4]

📝 Curator's note: Verification is not about picking the most palatable number, but about keeping the different statistical methods behind each number within the text.

## Those Left Behind at Sea, Those Waiting on Shore

The Australian warship HMAS Warramunga once participated in the rescue, and Keelung city's official page records that it saved 34 people. Combined with the unnamed individuals rescued by Zhoushan fishermen, the total number of survivors was only about 50. [^1] The survivors returning to shore did not mean the journey was over. Many had to face the reality that their family members had not returned, their bodies had not been recovered, and there was no place that could truly accommodate their names.

Writer Chang Tien-hsuan said in a 2013 interview that her mother had taken the Taiping to Taiwan in December 1948. Until her mother passed away in 2000, she encountered old Shanghai phone books, notebooks, and ID cards among her belongings, which brought her back to this history more deeply. She also said that relevant data was scarce, with the Shanghai Archives still holding old files, while materials in Taiwan were even scarcer. [^6]

A 2015 report by Hakka Television Network collected the memories of the victim's family member, Zhang Zhaoxiong. He said: "On New Year's Eve, when the ship was about to set sail, including the captain, the first mate, and the second mate, they were very happy to be returning to Taiwan, and they were all drinking, as far as I know." [^7]

The most important thing in this sentence may not be "drinking," but "as far as I know." Family members often can only piece together an unseen night through retellings, newspapers, scattered objects, and others' memories. The maritime disaster took away the victims, and also took away the witnesses who could describe every detail. [^6] [^7]

## A Stele, First Hidden Away

In 1950, the Taiping disaster victims' aftermath committee initiated the erection of a memorial stele, hoping to commemorate those who never set foot on land at the original endpoint of the journey. The stele, with calligraphy by You Ren, was originally located in the Dajia area of Keelung. Later, due to land reclamation and changes in military facilities, the stele was incorporated into a military zone, and family visits required permission to enter. [^1] [^2]

The Democratic Progressive Party's ethnic affairs department's 2006 commemorative report records that Wang Shu-liang still hoped that her brother's name would be carved on the stele 57 years after the sinking. She said that after her brother's death, her younger sister died of depression two years later, and her parents did not believe their son had died until late in their lives. [^8]

Zhang Zhao-mei recalled that when she was young, she had performed a soul-calling ceremony at the memorial stele for her father. Years later, land reclamation and port construction caused the originally sea-facing memorial stele to be blocked by buildings, making remembrance difficult. [^8]

If a memorial stele cannot be accessed by family members, is it still public memory? It can prove that the government once knew about this tragedy, but it does not necessarily allow later generations to truly encounter this history.

📝 Curator's note: The longest journey of the Taiping memorial stele was not at sea, but from the military camp fence back to the city.

## From Military Camp to Pocket Park

Starting in 2017, under the support of the Keelung Great Harbor historical scene restoration plan, the Keelung city government promoted the retreat of the walls around the memorial stele and greening. In March 2018, the pocket park was completed, allowing the memorial stele to be removed from the military zone, and both family members and the general public could visit. [^1]

The Keelung city tourism official page names this place the "1949 Taiping Memorial Park," placing the stele's inscriptions, the path between land and sea, and the changes that no longer require family members to apply for entry into the same field story. [^2]

The 2022 reconstructed historical site report by the National Cultural Heritage Network records that the Keelung city Cultural Affairs Bureau held a memorial service at the memorial site and set up a name wall, connecting the Taiping and Shatian historical and cultural park, the French Cemetery, the Fortress Commander's Residence, and other places. [^9]

This change was not just a landscape project. It turned a family tragedy that had been placed in a military space into urban history that anyone could approach. The park did not cause the sunken ship to surface, but it gave names a position where they could be seen again.

## Beyond the Film, Who Preserves the Facts

After director Tsui Hark's film "The Taiping" was released in 2014, the incident re-entered the public eye. Hakka Television Network reported that the bereaved families felt a gap between the film's beautiful love story narrative and the real maritime disaster. Wu Su-ping said: "I originally had a lot of expectations for him (Tsui Hark), but as he said, he wanted to make this movie, to be honest, this is using a thousand lives to make his beautiful century love epic." [^7]

This is not a demand that history cannot be adapted, but a reminder that adaptations have their own responsibilities: films can choose the perspective of a couple, but memorial sites must accommodate those who were not written as protagonists. The victims, crew, ticketless passengers, and people on the Jianguo cannot just become a vague "nearly a thousand people" in the end credits.

Chang Tien-hsuan said in an interview that the bereaved families have been holding memorial services and offering flowers every January 27 since 2005. She also opposed simplifying the sunken ship into a conspiracy story, believing that the currently available materials more support an understanding of accidents intertwined with systemic failures. This is the interviewee's judgment, not a conclusion that has already been drawn for all controversies. [^6]

Articles by the Shanghai Civil Affairs Bureau, the compilation by the People's Political Consultative Conference website, and reports by the Taipei Times all place overloading, navigation lights, routes, delays, and the shipping order during wartime into the background of the accident. But their numbers and details are not completely consistent. [^3] [^4] [^5] This inconsistency itself is part of the memory of the Taiping: archives are incomplete, families each preserve fragments, and later authors piece together the fragments into a readable story.

## Why This History Nearly Disappeared

The silence of the Taiping had several sources. It occurred at a time of regime change and war overlap, with families scattered across Shanghai, Taiwan, Hong Kong, and overseas. Lawsuits spanned different courts, and data was scattered across different file systems. When interviewed by Chang Tien-hsuan, she said that Shanghai still has some old files, while materials in Taiwan are even scarcer. [^6]

It also did not have a single conclusion that was easy for the state narrative to incorporate. Some saw it as a history of escape, some remembered the ship's responsibility, some traced it from the families' oral accounts, and some first heard the ship's name through a film. These perspectives overlap with each other, but cannot replace each other.

Therefore, the Taiping was not simply "forgotten." More accurately, it was long scattered across different people's lives. Some preserved a photo, some preserved the last words their mother said, some preserved a court document, until the memorial stele was reopened, these fragments finally had a place to meet each other.

📝 Curator's note: Public memory is not about putting all the answers on a single wall, but about letting scattered testimonies finally have a place to face each other.

## Taiwan Did Not Receive a Ship

The Taiping did not deliver passengers to Keelung, but it delivered a question to Taiwan: how should a society remember those who became part of it before they arrived?

Their story does not only belong to the categories of "mainlander" or "immigrants from China to Taiwan." Keelung's official placement of the memorial park is within the context of Taiwan's immigration history and liberal democracy. This statement does not eliminate the complexity of war, migration, and identity politics, but points to a very concrete fact: Taiwan's post-war population history is also composed of those who could not disembark. [^2] [^9]

If the Taiping is simply written as the "Orient's Titanic," readers will remember the collision, seawater, and death. Walking to the Keelung memorial park, one discovers another ending: the families no longer need to apply for entry, the name wall begins at the foot of the stele, and public memory finally has an entrance. [^2] [^9]

The Taiping sank near Baijie Mountain in 1949. The memorial park in Keelung opened to the public in 2018. [^1] [^2] The former was a journey that never arrived, and the latter allowed Taiwan to acknowledge that those who never set foot on land have already become part of this island's history.
