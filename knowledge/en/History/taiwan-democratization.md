# Taiwan’s Democratic Transition: The Grave Dug by Authoritarianism

On the morning of April 7, 1989, two hundred police officers surrounded the _Freedom Times_ magazine office on Minquan East Road in Taipei. Zheng Nanrong, 41, had already imprisoned himself in the editor-in-chief’s office for 71 days, refusing to appear in court on charges of "suspected treason."[^1] The moment the SWAT team broke down the door, he ignited pre-prepared gasoline, responding to a subpoena with self-immolation.

Seven months later, the Berlin Wall fell. Eight years later, Taiwan elected its first directly elected president. Eleven years later, the party behind that subpoena lost power.

Zheng Nanrong’s wife, Ye Ju-lan, came from the advertising industry and had never entered politics. Eight months after her husband’s death, she ran for Legislative Yuan member in his place. Her campaign slogan was: "Children, accompany me to fight a mother’s holy war!"[^1] She was elected with a high vote count. Fifteen years later, she took the seat of Vice Premier of the Executive Yuan.

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan traversed the path from the world’s longest martial law to Asia’s most free and democratic system in forty years, with almost no bloodshed. This was not because the rulers were benevolent, but because every crackdown backfired: the February 28 Incident created silent resisters, the Formosan Magazine Trial put defense lawyers on the political stage, and Zheng Nanrong’s fire made freedom of speech an irreversible baseline. The most ironic legacy of an authoritarian regime is that it trains the people who bury it.

## Seeds of Trauma: The Shot at the Roundabout (1947)

On the evening of February 27, 1947, in Taipei’s Roundabout, a Tobacco and Monopoly Bureau inspector, Ye De-gen, smashed the head of widow Lin Jiang-mai with his gun butt. She fell beside scattered illicit cigarettes, her face covered in blood. Onlookers chased the fleeing inspector. Another inspector, Fu Xue-tong, fired a warning shot into the sky; a stray bullet struck 20-year-old Chen Wen-xi, who was watching from his own doorway. He died the next day.[^2]

A pack of illicit cigarettes ignited the entire island.

The next day, citizens surrounded the Office of the Provincial Administrator to petition. Guards opened fire from the balcony, shooting into the crowd. Protests spread across Taiwan. On March 8, the ROC Army’s 21st Division landed in Keelung, launching a "pacification" campaign. The death toll remains disputed; a 2006 ROC Executive Yuan investigation report estimates deaths between 18,000 and 28,000.[^3] Intellectuals, doctors, lawyers, and local gentry were systematically purged.

> "What they killed were not rioters, but an entire generation of people who could have led Taiwan." — Wu Zhuoliu, _Fig Tree_

The direct effect of the February 28 Incident was fear. The indirect effect was planting an indelible question in the memory of an entire generation: **Why can’t we decide our own destiny?**

On May 19, 1949, Taiwan Provincial Chairman Chen Cheng declared martial law. This "temporary measure" lasted for 38 years and 56 days, the longest martial law period in world history.[^4] Forming political parties was banned, assemblies were banned, strikes were banned, newspapers were censored before printing, and there were only three TV stations. An infant born in 1949 had to wait until age 38 to know what a Taiwan without martial law looked like.

The authoritarian regime made a fatal error: it assumed silence equaled submission.

## Authoritarianism’s Most Expensive Talent Training Camp (1979–1980)

On December 10, 1979, International Human Rights Day. The _Formosan Magazine_ office planned a rally and march in Kaohsiung. Authorities denied permission, but approximately 20,000 people gathered anyway. After nightfall, riot police surrounded the venue, with tear gas and batons flying.[^5]

The great arrests followed. Huang Xin-jie, Shi Ming-de, Lu Hsiu-lien, Chen Chu, Lin Yi-hsiung, Yao Chia-wen, Chang Chun-hung, and Lin Hung-hsuan were sent to military court on charges of "treason."

Then the authoritarian regime made a second mistake: it decided to hold a public trial.

On March 18, 1980, a nine-day military court trial unfolded under the spotlight of domestic and international media. The performances of fifteen defense lawyers (including Chen Shui-bian, Hsieh Chang-ting, Su Tseng-chang, and Chang Chun-hsiung) made them famous overnight.[^6] The government intended to use the public trial to make an example of them, but instead created a whole batch of tomorrow’s stars. Twenty years later, one president and three Premiers emerged from among these defense lawyers.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> Chen Chu, one of the defendants in the Formosan Magazine Trial, wrote a suicide note after being imprisoned at age 29. She was not writing to her family, but to the people of Taiwan. The note quoted the Epistles of Paul: "I have fought the good fight."[^19] She believed she would be executed. Forty-one years later, this former political prisoner became the President of the Control Yuan, responsible for investigating whether the government had committed crimes.

On February 28, 1980, the 33rd anniversary of the February 28 Incident, Lin Yi-hsiung’s mother and seven-year-old twin daughters were murdered in their home; his eldest daughter was severely injured.[^7] The case remains unsolved. This bloodshed made more Taiwanese see clearly: authoritarian rule is not just an abstract political issue; it breaks into your home and kills your children.

## The Fatal Blow: Gunshots from a California Garage (1984)

On October 15, 1984, overseas Chinese writer Liu Yi-liang (pen name "Jiang Nan") was shot dead in the garage of his California residence. The FBI investigation revealed: the killer was a Zhuhui Gang member recruited by the ROC Military Intelligence Bureau, orchestrated by Bureau Director Wang Hsi-ling.[^8]

The ROC government assassinated an American citizen on U.S. soil.

Washington was outraged, threatening to cut off arms sales. Chiang Ching-kuo was forced to hand over Wang Hsi-ling and two others for trial. The Jiang Nan case forced Chiang Ching-kuo to face a cold equation: the cost of continuing authoritarian rule had become higher than the cost of opening up.

> "Changes in the international situation stripped the legitimacy from Taiwan’s authoritarian rule. Democratic transition was not a gift, but an inevitable choice under internal and external pressure." — Larry Diamond, _Taiwan: A Democratic Success Story_[^9]

## "Arresting People Doesn’t Solve Problems" (1986–1987)

On September 28, 1986, the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) was illegally founded at the Taipei Grand Hotel. The Police Headquarters sent over an arrest list. Chiang Ching-kuo’s response was six words: **"Arresting people doesn’t solve problems."**[^20] He set the list aside.

In January 1987, Chiang Ching-kuo gave an interview to the publisher of _The Washington Post_, Marvin Kalb, announcing the lifting of martial law and the opening of political party formation. Ma Ying-jeou, 36 at the time, served as the interpreter. He later recalled: "My scalp went numb, and my whole body felt like it was running with electricity."[^20]

Why lift martial law? Not out of a change of heart. The Jiang Nan case had ruined the international image; pressure from the non-KMT movement continued to rise; the end of the Cold War was stripping authoritarian rule of its international backing; and Chiang Ching-kuo’s own diabetes had left him nearly blind, with his health deteriorating rapidly. Lifting martial law was the result of calculation, not compassion.

At midnight on July 15, 1987, the martial law decree was lifted. Taiwanese people could suddenly form parties, assemble, and march. But thirty-eight years of silence would not turn into clamor just because of a piece of paper. Most people didn’t know what to do.

**Democracy is not a switch. It requires an entire society to relearn how to be citizens.**

## Seventy-One Days and a Fire (1989)

Taiwan was not peaceful after the lifting of martial law. Chiang Ching-kuo died in 1988, and Lee Teng-hui succeeded him as president, with conservative factions within the party watching like tigers. The boundaries of freedom of speech remained blurry.

Zheng Nanrong decided to test this boundary. In December 1988, he published the full text of the _Draft Constitution of the Republic of Taiwan_, drafted by Hsu Shih-kai, in _Freedom Times_. Authorities issued a subpoena on charges of "suspected treason." Zheng Nanrong publicly declared: "The KMT cannot catch my person, only my corpse."[^1]

Starting January 27, 1989, he locked himself in the magazine’s editor-in-chief office, refusing to appear in court. Seventy-one days later, on the morning of April 7, he fulfilled his promise with fire.

His wife, Ye Ju-lan, said: "One person runs a magazine, writes articles, and dies. You think once they’re dead, it’s over—but even though I am a woman, I can do something too."[^1]

April 7 was later designated as "Freedom of Speech Day." Zheng Nanrong’s self-immolation turned "freedom of speech" from a policy issue that could be discussed into an un-negotiable baseline. After him, no one could righteously say, "You can’t say such things."

## Six Thousand Wild Lilies vs. Tiananmen (1990)

On the afternoon of March 16, 1990, a small group of students sat in silence in the plaza of the Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall. The trigger was the National Assembly, elected on the mainland in 1947 and never fully re-elected after coming to Taiwan, preparing to elect Lee Teng-hui for a second term as president.

Word spread, and the response came from across the island. Within a few days, nearly 6,000 people gathered.[^10] They presented four major demands: dissolve the National Assembly, abolish the Temporary Provisions, convene a National Policy Conference, and establish a reform timeline.

The key question arose: Would Lee Teng-hui respond like Tiananmen did the previous year, or take another path?

On the evening of March 21, Lee Teng-hui met with 53 student representatives at the Presidential Office.[^10] He promised to convene the National Policy Conference. After the representatives returned to the plaza, student movement leader Fan Yun reported to the students. By a vote of the inter-school conference of 22:1, the students decided to withdraw voluntarily.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> The students in Tiananmen waited for tanks. The students in Taipei waited for the president’s promise. And the promise was kept: the Temporary Provisions were abolished in 1991, the Legislative Yuan was fully re-elected in 1992, mayors of Taipei and Kaohsiung were directly elected in 1994, and the president was directly elected in 1996. Within nine years, the transition from authoritarianism to full democracy. The subsequent trajectories of the Wild Lily Movement leaders read like a political encyclopedia: Fan Yun became a DPP Legislative Yuan member, Lin Chia-lung became Minister of Foreign Affairs, and Cheng Wen-chen served as Mayor of Taoyuan.

## Voting in Line Under Missiles (1996)

On March 23, 1996, Taiwan held its first direct presidential election. China’s response was to fire missiles in the waters off Keelung and Kaohsiung, attempting to intimidate Taiwanese voters. The U.S. dispatched two carrier strike groups to cross the Taiwan Strait.[^11]

Polling stations in Taipei were set up in temples, with voters receiving ballots before deities. A newlywed couple in Kinmen rushed to vote on their wedding day, celebrating both their marriage and Taiwan’s first free election.[^12]

The result backfired. With a 76.04% turnout, Lee Teng-hui was elected with 54% of the vote. The missiles instead galvanized the Taiwanese people’s will to vote.[^12]

> "For every missile China fires, Lee Teng-hui gains another percentage point." — A joke circulating at the time

Four sets of candidates, an army of international media, missile threats, and then peaceful ballot counting, with the losers accepting the results. For the first time in the Chinese-speaking world, a nation’s leader was chosen by ballot.

## Party Rotation: Democracy’s Stress Test (2000–2024)

On the evening of March 18, 2000, TV stations announced successively: Chen Shui-bian and Lu Hsiu-lien were elected. The KMT lost power in Taiwan after 55 years. Lu Hsiu-lien, a political prisoner 20 years prior, became Vice President. The defense lawyers from the Formosan Magazine Trial took up residence in the Presidential Office. On May 20, Lee Teng-hui handed the presidential seal to Chen Shui-bian. Peaceful, complete, bloodless transfer of power.[^13]

Ma Ying-jeou was elected in 2008, the second rotation, proving the first was no accident. Tsai Ing-wen was elected in 2016, bringing Taiwan its first female head of state. Lai Ching-te was elected in 2024, opening the era of a minority government.

**Four party rotations within 24 years. Democracy has become routine operation rather than a historical event.**

## A 30-Second Declaration and a 24-Day Occupation (2014)

On the afternoon of March 17, 2014, KMT Legislative Yuan member Chang Ching-chung declared the Cross-Strait Service Trade Agreement "deemed reviewed" in 30 seconds. The next night at 9:30, more than 200 students and civic groups rushed into the Legislative Yuan chamber.[^14]

This occupation lasted 24 days. Unlike the Formosan generation, the Sunflower Movement was a decentralized digital movement. The civic hacker community g0v set up the `g0v.today` aggregation platform, simultaneously streaming 17 live video feeds, exposing every corner inside and outside the chamber to global viewers.[^21] Engineers from the Industrial Technology Research Institute even assisted in installing six cameras to eliminate corridor blind spots. Audrey Tang (then a g0v member) later said: "Most of the technology we deployed was neutral; its purpose was simply to encourage people to talk."[^21]

On March 30, hundreds of thousands gathered on Ketagalan Boulevard. The Service Trade was indeed blocked. More profoundly, it redefined how Taiwan’s younger generation engages in politics: you don’t need to join a party; you just need a laptop and a presence on the ground.

> **💡 Did You Know**
> The open-source code of the Sunflower Movement was used by protesters in Hong Kong’s 2014 Umbrella Movement to build their own platform. After the occupation ended, g0v collaborated with the government to spawn vTaiwan and the "Legislative Yuan Unparalleled" legislative live-streaming system. An occupation became an institution.

## Asia’s First Rainbow Certificate (2019)

At 3:27 PM on May 17, 2019, Legislative Yuan Speaker Su Tseng-chang struck the gavel. The _Act for Implementation of Judicial Yuan Interpretation No. 748_ passed its third reading. Taiwan became the first country in Asia to legalize same-sex marriage.[^15]

It took effect on May 24. On the first day, 526 same-sex couples completed registration.

The significance of this goes beyond marriage itself. It proves that Taiwan’s democracy is not just about majority rule, but has the capacity to protect minority rights, even when the majority votes against them in referendums. From the total ban on homosexuality during martial law to Asia’s first, it took more than thirty years.

## Freedom on a Treadmill

Freedom House 2024 Report: Taiwan scored 94 (out of 100), second in Asia, seventh globally.[^16] The Economist Democracy Index 2024: First in Asia, twelfth globally.[^17]

The numbers are impressive. The challenges are real. Information warfare from China continues to escalate; social media exacerbates political polarization; youth voter turnout is declining. The Transitional Justice Commission ended its mission in 2022, but political archives are not yet fully open, perpetrator accountability is barely pursued, and the transformation of the Chiang Kai-shek Memorial Hall remains controversial.[^18]

> **⚠️ Controversial Viewpoint**
> Chiang Ching-kuo’s historical positioning has always been one of the most divisive issues in Taiwanese society. Supporters view the lifting of martial law as his gift. Opponents point out that he directed the security system during the White Terror, and the lifting of martial law was also a calculation forced by internal and external pressures. Both narratives have factual basis, but choosing which side to emphasize is itself a political stance.

Democracy is not a trophy; it is a treadmill. The moment you stop, it moves backward.

In 1980, 29-year-old Chen Chu wrote a suicide note in prison, saying goodbye to the Taiwan she loved. She quoted the Epistles of Paul: "I have fought the good fight."[^19]

She was not executed. She served six years. After release, she helped found the DPP, served as Mayor of Taipei, Chairman of the Council of Labor Affairs, and Mayor of Kaohsiung for 12 years. In 2020, she was nominated as President of the Control Yuan.

The same person. The same island. The only difference is: in 1979, her ideas were a crime. In 2020, this former political prisoner was responsible for investigating whether the government had committed crimes.

From suicide note to President of the Control Yuan: 41 years. This is what democratic transition means. Not a smooth inspirational arc, but a political experiment full of absurdity, contradictions, and irreversible costs. The starters didn’t know it would succeed. Many thought they would die. The experiment continues.
