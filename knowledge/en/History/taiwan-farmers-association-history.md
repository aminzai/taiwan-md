# The Farmers' Association Was Not Just About Selling Fertilizer: How Taiwan's Rural Public Organizations Transitioned from Colonial Systems to Credit Union Reform

In 1900, the first farmers' association was established in the _Sanxia_ (三角湧) area of what is now New Taipei City. In 1943, the farmers' associations, industrial cooperatives, and other agricultural groups at the end of Japanese rule were merged into an Agricultural Association. Following the war, the farmers' associations and cooperatives were reorganized again in 1949.[^1] [^2]

In contemporary local life, the farmers' association might just be the building where one buys fertilizer, takes out a loan, attends exhibitions, or participates in agricultural courses. Only by extending the timeline does one realize that this seemingly familiar interface once managed agricultural production, rural finance, local administration, and political power. Its continuity to the present day relies on successive regimes continuously reallocating service recipients, management methods, and resource flows.

> **30-Second Overview:** The history of Taiwan's farmers' associations encompasses both mutual aid among farmers and state governance. Technology, funding, and agricultural policies entered the locality through this organization, while local elites, elections, and resource competition also left their marks within it. The role of the farmers' association gradually took shape at the intersection of these two trajectories.

## The First Farmers' Association in _Sanxia_ (三角湧)

Historical accounts from the Ministry of Agriculture state that the first farmers' association was founded in 1900 in an area then called _Sanxia_ (三角湧), which is today the _Sanxia_ area. Its original purposes included assisting the Japanese governing authorities in collecting land rent, improving farmland, and promoting pig farming and fishing. According to the Farmers' Association Law promulgated by Japan in 1899, existing agricultural groups were unified under the name "Farmers' Association" and granted legal status.[^1]

From the beginning, the farmers' association carried two tasks simultaneously. While it was ostensibly for improving rural production, it also assisted administrative execution and served the interests of the landowning class. To view it merely as a private cooperative organization is to overlook its connection to the governing apparatus. To see it only as an administrative agency is to miss its actual operation within the local economy.

This hybrid nature explains why the farmers' association could take root locally. It had tasks related to agricultural technology and production improvement, as well as an organizational position that could assist in administration. For the state, the farmers' association was a ready-made network into the countryside. For local elites, it was a public arena where they could gain information, resources, and institutional status.

Research on farmers' association archives by Airiti indicates that the three-tiered system of Taiwanese farmers' associations was established during the Japanese colonial period, and its organization and functions were frequently influenced by the demands of the ruling power, laws, administrative orders, and financial subsidies. The archives preserved by local farmers' associations also record institutional reforms, legislative amendments, and regime changes.[^4]

## 1943: When the Farmers' Association Became the Agricultural Association

In 1943, in order to strengthen control over Taiwan's agricultural economy, Japan enacted the Agricultural Group Law, integrating various agricultural groups—including farmers' associations, livestock associations, industrial cooperative associations, fruit and vegetable industry association unions, and fertilizer distribution cooperatives—under the unified name of "Agricultural Association."[^1]

This reorganization changed the way resources flowed. Production, finance, marketing, materials, and distribution, which were originally scattered across different groups, were now consolidated into a single agricultural organization. This made it easier for the wartime government to allocate materials through the Agricultural Association and deliver orders to the villages.

Airiti's post-war farmers' association research refers to this period as one of the institutional legacies. The study points out that after the Japanese colonial era, the Agricultural Association and industrial cooperative system were officially merged into a new organization, but when the post-war government took over Taiwan, there was no immediate consensus on how to handle it. The Central Social Department, which supervised cooperatives, tended to maintain the Agricultural Association, while others demanded that cooperatives be separated.[^2]

Therefore, the history of the farmers' association cannot be summarized merely as "established by Japan, continued post-war." What the new government inherited was a local organization where assets, members, functions, and power overlapped. The fate of the farmers' associations and cooperatives became an issue of how the new regime reallocated rural resources.

## Post-War Separation and Recombination

Historical accounts from the Ministry of Agriculture state that after Taiwan's liberation, the Agricultural Association was reorganized into two entities: the Farmers' Association and the Cooperative. The Farmers' Association was responsible for agricultural promotion and protecting farmers' rights, while the Cooperative handled economic affairs. The two were merged again in 1949, underwent a second merger with the intervention of the National Rehabilitation Committee (農復會) in 1950, and were reorganized according to the provisional regulations of various levels of Taiwanese farmers' associations in 1953, completing the reorganization in 1954.[^1]

The separation and recombination of the farmers' association involved far more than administrative efficiency. Airiti's archival research points out that the issue stemmed both from differences between old and new political legal systems and from control over cooperative businesses by different political forces at the central level. In 1949, Chen Cheng (陳誠) advocated for a re-merger, and related disputes continued until the farmers' association reforms in 1953, concluding only after Chiang Kai-shek (蔣介石) supported the merger.[^2]

Thus, the farmers' association reforms of the 1950s should be viewed as an act of institutional construction, not a single consolidation. Research by Lin Baoan (林寶安) points out that the post-war initial farmers' association reform policy established the basic system where the farmers' association possessed multiple functions and monopolized rural affairs, incorporating Japanese colonial legacies, the spirit of mainland farmers' associations, the claims of the National Rehabilitation Committee, and the experience of post-war agricultural reform in Japan into one system.[^7]

This system placed agricultural promotion, supply/marketing, distribution, and credit functions in one place. Farmers could access production technology, materials, loans, and sales channels within the same organization. The influence of the farmers' association thus extended beyond its membership numbers to encompass the businesses and resources it controlled.

> **Curator's Note:** The key to the farmers' association reform was not that the organization became simpler, but rather that different institutional legacies were placed into a single rural public institution. Subsequently, the farmers' association carried characteristics of cooperation, administration, and politics simultaneously.

## Why Was the Credit Union the Core of the Farmers' Association?

The relationship between the farmers' association and its credit union is one of the most crucial entry points for understanding the post-war farmers' association. Research by Ding Wenyu (丁文郁) divided ten different relational stages based on the chronological sequence and major events concerning the farmers' association and its credit union. The research summary indicates that the surplus and number of employees in the credit union held an important position within the farmers' association, and the relationship between the farmers' association and the credit union can be seen as a microcosm of Taiwan's farmers' association development.[^3]

The position of the credit union within the farmers' association far exceeded that of a mere ancillary function. It provided capital and supported the organizational scale and local service capacity of the farmers' association. When farmers deposited money or came to the counter with loan applications, the farmers' association transformed from an institution promoting new varieties and conducting seminars into a public interface encountered during household livelihood arrangements.

The larger the credit union, the harder it was to hide risks in local ledgers. The Ministry of Agriculture reviewed agricultural finance reform and noted that the government designated banks to take over 36 poorly performing farmers' and fishermen's association credit unions in 2001 and 2002. After the Agricultural Finance Act was implemented in 2004, the Financial Supervisory Commission (農業金融局) was responsible for supervision and guidance, as well as handling issues such as credit union assignments to banks, re-establishment, and mergers.[^5]

The difficulty of credit union reform lay in the fact that it simultaneously bore local financial services, policy-based agricultural loans, and farmers' trust. Supervision could not only pursue zero risk but also ignore the convenience with which farmers obtained funds locally. The Agricultural Finance system was precisely grappling with this tension.

## After 2003: When the Credit Union Was Placed into a Larger Financial System

Before credit union reform, the farmers' association counter dealt with very specific local needs: farmers needed to buy fertilizer, required working capital during peak farming seasons, needed funds for the next crop, and might need to settle old debts from poor harvests. These needs could not always be handled by standard bank branch procedures. The existence of the farmers' association credit union allowed financial services to operate according to the rhythm of local industry.

The problems also began here. If a credit union relied excessively on a few local industries or personal networks, lending risk would become concentrated. If supervision only looked at reports, it might ignore the crops, land, and household livelihoods behind a loan. The reforms in the 2000s were an attempt to draw a line between this locality and financial regulations.

The official history of the National Agricultural Bank states that the government held the National Agricultural Finance Conference on November 30, 2002, and the Agricultural Finance Act was legislated on July 10, 2003. The Financial Supervisory Commission was established on January 30, 2004, which promoted the National Agricultural Bank. On May 26, 2005, the National Agricultural Bank was approved to commence operations. According to the Agricultural Finance Act, the National Agricultural Bank and farmers' and fishermen's association credit unions jointly constituted an agricultural financial institution, with the bank becoming a higher-level body above the farmers' and fishermen's association credit unions.[^6]

This change did not cut the farmers' association credit union off from the farmers' association; rather, it placed it into a larger network of agricultural financial supervision. Research by Sun Tingxuan (孫廷瑄), using the farmers' association credit union as a case study, analyzed efficiency before and after agricultural finance reform. The summary indicates that after the Agricultural Finance Act in 2003, the Financial Supervisory Commission began guiding previously taken-over credit unions, improving financial supervision capabilities, but 13 of the 14 newly established credit unions still performed poorly.[^8]

This result shows that institutional reform can make financial data clearer and supervisory mechanisms more complete, but it does not mean that local organizations immediately become healthy. The issues of the farmers' association credit union involve personnel, resource allocation, lending, local industry, and management capacity. Since industrial structures differ across regions, the loan demands and risks faced by the credit unions are also not the same. Therefore, agricultural finance reform was not just a set of regulations but a long-term project that had to address regional industrial differences.

## Is the Farmers' Association a Farmers' Organization or a Field of Local Power?

The multifunctionality of the farmers' association gave it a strong local presence, which made "who the farmers' association truly represents" a long-standing question. Historical accounts from the Ministry of Agriculture point out that after the reorganization in 1954, the farmers' association rapidly developed its functions and created a group of rural elites. The farmers' association became a political stage with resources locally, and the general secretary also consolidated resources through the credit union.[^1]

One cannot simply label all farmers' associations as tools of local factions. Farmers' associations in different regions have different membership structures, industrial conditions, executive traditions, and financial scales. However, research does remind us that there can be a gap between the legal status and the actual operation of the farmers' association. Nominally, it represents farmers, but operationally, it is jointly influenced by superior policies, subsidies, laws, local elites, and credit union resources.[^4]

The importance of farmers' association archives lies in their ability to ground these abstract statements in reality. Membership rolls can show who was included or excluded. The lists of directors and supervisors can track local elites. Business reports can reveal the flow of funds. Promotion records can show what crops the state wanted farmers to change, or what technologies to adopt. Farmers' associations also preserve traces of how locals responded to policies, such as how members applied for materials, how the credit union handled loans, and how executives translated central regulations into executable local tasks. The farmers' association is not just a set of central regulations; it exists in a large volume of local documents.

Membership qualifications are another often-overlooked entry point. Historical accounts from the Ministry of Agriculture state that after the 1953 reorganization, the farmers' association divided members into regular members and sponsoring members, who had different voting and eligibility rights. This indicates that "farmers being organized" does not mean that everyone related to agriculture has equal organizational rights. Who could participate in a general meeting, or who could become a director or supervisor, directly influenced how the farmers' association's resources were allocated.[^1]

The membership system also explains why the farmers' association became a local political stage. Its influence came from credit union funds, as well as membership rolls, delegate seats, promotional networks, and government-commissioned tasks. When studying the farmers' association, one must not only look at what services it provided to farmers but also question who had the authority to determine the direction of those services.

## Institutional Memory of Taiwan's Countryside Seen Through the Farmers' Association

![The old building of the Tianzhong Farmers' Association photographed in 2012, showing the continuation of the local farmers' association as a daily service space](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2a/TienchungFarmersAssociation.JPG)

The old building of the Tianzhong Farmers' Association shown is not from the Japanese colonial period and cannot independently prove the age of the building. Its value lies in presenting how the farmers' association continued to exist as a local architectural space between daily services, finance, and administration. The photo was taken by Shxpeng and licensed under Creative Commons Attribution-ShareAlike 3.0 Unported.[^10]

The history of the farmers' association connects several historical threads that are often separated. Japanese colonial governance, post-war land reform, rural organizations, agricultural finance, local politics, and agricultural modernization all met within the institution of the farmers' association. It is not just one chapter; it is a place where institutional implementation can be seen.

Research by Lin Baoan argues that the 1950s farmers' association reforms had the significance of maintaining and disseminating the social effects of land reform, in addition to changing the power structure of the farmers' association.[^7] This does not mean the farmers' association _is_ land reform, nor does it mean all reform achievements were accomplished by the farmers' association; rather, the farmers' association provided the grassroots channel for managing rural areas, disseminating policies, and organizing production after land reform.

After 2003, the Agricultural Finance Act and the National Agricultural Bank placed the farmers' association credit union into the national financial supervision system. The farmers' association also had to face financial risks, regulatory standards, and changes in the rural population structure; it could no longer operate solely according to local organizational habits formed in the 1950s.[^5] [^6] [^8]

Today, when discussing the farmers' association, people often first ask if it still exists or whether it makes a profit. The more difficult and important question is: after agricultural population decline, rural aging, and changes in local industry, can the farmers' association still enable farmers to access technology, credit, and public information in a familiar place? There is no single answer. Yet, this question brings the history of the farmers' association back to daily rural life, allowing us to see how institutions allocate resources and how people remember an organization.

The farmers' association was not preserved unchanged from the colonial period until today. In 1900, it became a legal entity governing the countryside. In 1943, it was integrated into the Agricultural Association. Post-war, it separated and recombined between the farmers' association and the cooperative, re-shaping in the 1950s, and later connecting to the national agricultural financial system through the credit union. Its continuation relied on repeated transformation.

The historical position of the farmers' association is here. National agricultural policy entered local daily life through it, and local resource competition returned to the state system through it. Only by clearly seeing this back-and-forth path can one understand that the farmers' association both provided services and endured pressure from control and competition.
