# Taiwan's Fisheries Modernization: How One Net Connects Cianjhen, Three Oceans, and an Island's Dinner Tables

> **30-second overview:** Taiwan's fisheries modernization was never just about swapping small boats for large ones. The fisheries administration, fishing ports, and aquatic research established during the Japanese era were reconnected after the war with ice plants, cold-storage facilities, powered vessels, and trained personnel. When coastal and offshore resources came under pressure, policy pushed the fleet toward the three oceans, forcing Taiwan to confront the institutional realities of exclusive economic zones, regional fisheries management, flag-state responsibilities, and transnational labor. The fish on today's tables may simultaneously connect Cianjhen, the Pacific, and an invisible web of international rules.

In 1952, Taiwan's coastal fisheries production reached 43,900 metric tons. In 1980, offshore fisheries production reached 370,906 metric tons. In 2003, Taiwan's total fisheries production hit 1.5 million metric tons. These three figures appear to trace an upward curve, but they actually record three pivots: from coast to open ocean, from capture to aquaculture, and from pursuing higher output to resource management.[^1] [^2]

## Cianjhen Port's Ice Arrived Before the Distant-Water Fleet

![Cijin Fishing Port and port area landscape](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b2/Cijin_Fishing_Port_in_Kaohsiung%2C_Taiwan.JPG)
_Image source: [Cijin Fishing Port in Kaohsiung, Taiwan](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cijin_Fishing_Port_in_Kaohsiung,_Taiwan.JPG), author SSR2000, CC BY-SA 3.0._

Postwar fisheries reconstruction did not begin with a single high-performance distant-water vessel. The Fisheries Agency's institutional history designates 1946–1970 as the reconstruction period, during which government and private sectors invested in small and medium-sized harbors of refuge, ice plants, cold-storage facilities, onshore infrastructure, and powered fishing vessels. If fish could not be kept fresh after landing, increasing catch capacity would only increase losses. Ports and cold chains performed the critical work of turning catches into commodities.[^2]

The National Museum of Marine Science and Technology's fisheries history materials provide a measure of the postwar rupture. In 1940, Taiwan's fisheries production was 129,261 metric tons, with marine capture accounting for the majority. In 1946, the catch plummeted to 16,860 metric tons, powered vessels numbered only 697, and distant-water fisheries production was a mere 68 metric tons. The market's sharp contraction stemmed from wartime requisition of fishing vessels, destruction of ports and facilities — the entire production network was dismantled.[^3]

By 1952, fisheries production had recovered to 121,697 metric tons; by 1960 it reached 259,140 metric tons. The numbers rebounded rapidly, backed by the simultaneous restoration of vessels, ports, ice storage, shipbuilding and repair, fuel, wholesale, and processing. After the Cianjhen Distant-Water Fishing Port was built in 1964, it became a major base for large-scale distant-water fisheries. The port handled berthing, crew and fish transshipment, machinery repair, and foreign exchange concentration.[^3]

> **Curator's note:** The most visible part of fisheries modernization is the big boats; the most overlooked is the ice that keeps fish from rotting on shore.

## Seamen's Certificates and Fisheries Administration Predated Diesel Engines

Taiwanese history of living from the sea long predates modern fishing ports. Materials compiled by the National Museum of Marine Science and Technology note that during the Ming and Qing dynasties, maritime bans, coastal evacuations, and maritime security policies restricted fisherfolk activities. The Dutch period levied taxes on mullet and fishing vessels; the Kingdom of Tungning collected "water taxes" on fishing gear, vessels, and mullet flags. These systems' primary purpose was not necessarily to protect fishers, yet they show that fishing has long been subject to management of vessel registration, gear, seasons, and taxation.[^4]

Qing-era registration of crew names, ages, native places, and physical descriptions made the crew waist-plate an early form of seaman's certificate. By the Japanese era, the Japanese government, based on Japanese fisheries laws and regulations, established fisheries administration, aquatic surveys, experimentation, fishing ports, and technical incentive systems in Taiwan. The National Museum of Marine Science and Technology's materials simultaneously remind us that this modernization served the colonial economy — Taiwan gained technology and experience, but benefit distribution was determined by the colonial order.[^4]

The Ministry of Agriculture's century of fisheries evolution describes 1895–1945 as the period when modern fisheries institutions were gradually established, including powered fishing vessels, improved gear, fisheries migration, fishing ports, and aquatic experimentation. In 1900, Taiwan's fisheries production was under 5,000 metric tons; in 1921 it reached 23,306 metric tons; in 1940 it hit the Japanese-era peak. Growth cannot be written as mere technological progress; one must also ask who controlled the boats, ports, and markets, and where Taiwanese stood in that system.[^1] [^3]

## The Coastal Peak Forced the Decision to Go Outward

In the early postwar period, the government needed food and revenue; policy encouraged shipbuilding, capture, and aquaculture. Coastal fisheries peaked at 43,900 metric tons in 1952; offshore fisheries peaked at 370,906 metric tons in 1980. These achievements made fisheries look like an industry that could keep scaling up, but the coastline is not an infinite production line. As boats multiplied, gear densified, and catching power rose, resource pressure increased in step.[^1]

Around the 1980s, policy began addressing two problems simultaneously. First, pushing part of production capacity outward to the open ocean; second, using artificial reefs, stock releases, construction limits, vessel retirement, fleet reduction, and fishing moratoriums to control coastal and offshore fishing effort. The 1982 United Nations Convention on the Law of the Sea established the exclusive economic zone regime, increasing coastal states' resource rights and management responsibilities within 200 nautical miles. This meant the problem facing Taiwan's fishing vessels was no longer just finding fish schools, but whether they could legally enter certain waters.[^1]

In 1987, the Council of Agriculture drafted a fisheries development program, formally listing distant-water fisheries as a key direction. Tuna longline, large-scale skipjack and tuna purse seine, squid jigging, and saury stick-held dip net became the main methods for Taiwan's fleet to enter the three oceans. Distant-water expansion was an industrial choice, jointly driven by government loans, ports, shipbuilding, cold storage, processing, international cooperation, and corporate investment.[^2]

## The Distant-Water Fleet Brought Taiwan into International Rules

The scale of distant-water fisheries meant Taiwan could no longer understand itself through domestic policy alone. In the 2000s, regional fisheries management organizations (RFMOs) strengthened conservation measures, setting clearer requirements for quotas, vessels, observers, transshipment, and monitoring. A _Marine Policy_ study indexed in RePEc notes that Taiwan's distant-water fisheries experienced postwar reconstruction, pre-1970s expansion, and a later phase constrained by international management regimes.[^5]

In 2005, the International Commission for the Conservation of Atlantic Tunas (ICCAT) determined that Taiwan had violated management measures and imposed sanctions on Taiwan's Atlantic tuna longliners. The Fisheries Agency's institutional history places this event at a turning point for distant-water fisheries management and industry restructuring; subsequent reforms included reducing large-vessel catching capacity, strengthening monitoring, and increasing scientific observers. Writing this history merely as "Taiwan improved management after sanctions" would miss the gap between the speed of industrial expansion and international responsibility.[^1]

Viewed from the history of Southeast Asian fishing grounds, Taiwan's distant-water fisheries is an industry spanning Taiwan's ports and overseas waters. An academic monograph divides Taiwan's Southeast Asian distant-water fisheries from 1936 to 1977 into distinct developmental phases, discussing how fleets entered regional waters and how politics, colonial legacies, and ocean governance constrained subsequent development. After leaving port, vessels remain bound in a net of vessel registration, contracts, ports, fuel supply, and diplomatic relations.[^6] [^7]

## A Fish's Price Contains an Entire Onshore Industry

Distant-water fisheries are often imagined as an extension of work at sea; in reality they depend heavily on onshore manufacturing and services. Vessels need engines, cold-storage equipment, navigation instruments, fuel, repair, and insurance. Once fish land, they need auction, cutting, freezing, transport, and export. The National Museum of Marine Science and Technology notes that postwar fisheries development proceeded in step with fishing ports, ice plants, cold-storage facilities, aquatic experimentation, and education and training. Fisheries simultaneously connect fishers' labor, port-city industry, and food supply chains.[^3]

This also explains why Taiwan's fisheries history cannot be told only through the romantic narrative of "fishers versus the sea." Crews bear risk at sea; onshore families, processing plants, shipyards, fish markets, and local governments also jointly bear the industry's costs. When catches rise, gains do not necessarily distribute evenly across every link. When resources decline or international norms tighten, the first to feel pressure may be small boats, hired crew, and families dependent on a single fishing port.[^5] [^8]

## Aquaculture Ponds Turned the Coast into Another Kind of Factory

Fisheries modernization did not only push boats toward the open ocean; it also kept fish on land. In 1963, Taiwan achieved successful artificial propagation of milkfish and grass carp, enabling mass production of fry and providing the foundation for aquaculture expansion. In the 1980s, grass shrimp farming was dubbed the "Grass Shrimp Kingdom." In 1994, eel exports peaked, and Taiwan was once famous for eel farming. These industries relied on seedstock, feed, disease control, water quality management, cold storage, and export markets — sharing the name "fisheries" with capture fisheries but operating on a different production logic.[^1] [^3]

Aquaculture's problems cannot be solved by technology alone. Limited land and water resources, disease, drug residues, costs, and market prices all determine whether a given pond can stay in business. Ministry of Agriculture materials note that post-2000 aquaculture policy shifted toward marine aquaculture, promoting ornamental fish and grouper as target industries. This shift turned fisheries from chasing wild fish schools into managing water bodies, fry, and market risk.[^1]

## Fishing Ports Began Learning to Host Visitors

![Mituo Fishing Port](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/0/05/Mituo_fishing_harbor_06.jpg)
_Image source: [Mituo fishing harbor 06](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Mituo_fishing_harbor_06.jpg), author Reke, CC BY-SA 4.0._

As coastal and offshore resources declined and traditional capture income became unstable, fishing ports sought new functions. Fisheries Agency records note that after martial law was lifted in 1987, the government expanded marine recreation activities; in 1999 it promoted a fishing port multifunctionalization plan, driving leisure fishing ports and fisherman's wharves. Fishing ports thus simultaneously faced fishing vessels, fish cargo, tourists, and local brands.[^2]

Multifunctional ports do not mean traditional fisheries naturally disappear. Tourism can increase local revenue, but may also raise land prices, alter pier usage rights, and compress working space. For tourists, the port is a place to view the sea, eat seafood, and take photos. For fishers, the port is first and foremost a workplace for unloading, resupplying, repairing, and waiting for the next departure. When these two uses overlap, local governance truly begins.

Port transformation also changes how local memory is presented. Old fish markets, net-drying grounds, and shipyards retain traces of work; tourism design tends to condense complex industrial processes into seascapes, signage, and dining tables. If local tours preserve the narratives of fishers, fishmongers, and shipyard workers, visitors can see the port's industrial pulse and understand how it sustains the city's food supply. Such preservation requires local negotiation; it cannot be completed automatically by a single hardware project.[^2] [^10]

## Fisheries Schools, Experiment Stations, and the Localization of Technology

After vessels were motorized, crews needed to learn engine repair, navigation, gear improvement, and cold-storage operations. The Japanese era's aquatic experimentation and surveys left institutional and equipment foundations. Postwar takeover work had to handle reassignment of personnel, vessel registration, data, and equipment. A National Taiwan Normal University thesis on early postwar fisheries technical talent rightly focuses on this link easily obscured by production figures: industrial recovery depended on people who could operate institutions and equipment, and technical transmission involved educational institutions and administrative appointments.[^4]

Technology localization also occurred in gear and operational methods. Longline, trawl, purse seine, and squid jigging require different vessel types, fish school knowledge, and operational rhythms. As fleets moved from offshore to distant waters, voyages lengthened, making fish preservation and resupply arrangements more critical. Onboard work gradually shifted from family or small partnerships toward corporate-style management. This change raised per-vessel capacity but concentrated capital, equipment, and risk into fewer operators' hands.

## The Time Lag Borne by Fishing Village Families

A single offshore trip may return the same day; a distant-water voyage keeps crew away from home for long periods. Industrial statistics treat the vessel as a production unit; fishing village life revolves around whether family members come home, when income is remitted, and when the boat needs repair. As fleets turned toward distant waters, women's onshore roles in accounting, caregiving, and market work became more important. These labors rarely appear in catch tables, yet they maintain the conditions that allow fleets to sail repeatedly.

The port's local society also forms multi-layered relationships. Boat owners need repair and supplies; fishmongers need stable supply; workers need stevedoring and processing jobs; local governments handle roads, sanitation, shelter, and port land. After port multifunctionalization, food service, tourism, and leisure activities enter the same space. Fishing families' livelihoods comprise sea capture, onshore processing, and local services; port transformation also redistributes job opportunities.

## The Other Side of Production Figures: Resources and Risk

Output-growth policies had their historical conditions. Postwar population growth made fish a key protein source; aquaculture could also compensate for capture's uncertainty. Ministry of Agriculture materials note that aquaculture once formed export advantages in grass shrimp, eel, tilapia, and grouper. When market prices rose, farmers expanded pond area and inputs, and risks from disease, water quality, and feed costs rose in turn. Industrial success itself creates the next stage's management problems.[^1]

Offshore fleet reduction and moratorium policies directly touch income distribution. After old vessels are bought back, resource pressure may ease, but families dependent on a single fishing method must find new income. Moratorium subsidies provide a buffer but cannot replace long-term work in fishing villages. Artificial reefs and stock releases have restorative significance, but effectiveness must be assessed by sea area, species, and enforcement. Historical analysis that only records policy names misses how policies rewrite the timetables of families and ports.[^1] [^10]

Fisheries finance also plays a role in this turning point. Building larger vessels, installing cold-storage and navigation equipment, requires long-term capital. Catch income is subject to season, weather, fuel prices, fish prices, and international quotas. Loans give fleets upgrade capacity; repayment pressure also makes operators more dependent on high-intensity operations. For small vessels, when fixed costs of repair, fuel, and insurance rise, supporting a full year's livelihood on a single good fishing season becomes harder. These financial conditions explain why the "modernization" spoken of in policy arrives at different speeds in different fishing villages.

Market chains likewise changed the distance between consumers and producers. Freezing technology lets fish circulate across seasons and seas; exporters organize supply by specification, weight, and delivery schedule. Fish market auction prices transmit through wholesalers, processors, and retailers to family tables, but price signals do not fully return to the sea. When consumers see only packaging and origin labels, capture methods, crew work, and resource status easily recede from view. Fisheries governance must reconnect this information for sustainability to be more than a market slogan.[^1] [^9]

After supply chain expansion, market participants need new knowledge. Fish markets must identify species and quality; cold-storage firms must master temperature and inventory; exporters must understand different countries' hygiene and labeling regulations; government must place catch data, vessel data, and conservation rules in a single management framework. Modernization's outcomes thus include a data regime. If data remains siloed in separate tables of operators, ports, or competent authorities, consumers can hardly judge a seafood product's origin and environmental cost. Open, mutually verifiable data lets fishers, government, and consumers discuss resource use on the same information basis. This is also the foundational work still needed for today's fisheries transition.[^1] [^5]

## From Production Nation to Responsible Flag State

![Zhengbin Fishing Port](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/88/Zhengbin_Fishing_Port%2C_Keelung%2C_Taiwan_2019.jpg)
_Image source: [Zhengbin Fishing Port, Keelung, Taiwan 2019](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zhengbin_Fishing_Port,_Keelung,_Taiwan_2019.jpg), author bryan..., CC BY-SA 2.0._

Taiwan's fisheries growth in the latter half of the twentieth century left clear industrial capacity. Vessels can operate in three oceans; aquaculture technology can mass-produce fry; ports and cold-storage facilities can send fish to distant markets. But the greater the industrial capacity, the harder external responsibilities are to evade. The flag state must know where its vessels operate; regional organizations' conservation rules cannot remain only in meeting documents; labor and transshipment processes must also be traceable.[^5] [^9]

Thus the core question of Taiwan's fisheries history is whether production, technology, and international responsibility can be sustained simultaneously. Traditional and modern fisheries have long coexisted in many ports, sharing markets, facilities, and policy. The 2005 sanctions reminded Taiwan that the distant-water fleet's global reach is both a capability and a commitment subject to inspection. Offshore fleet reduction and moratoriums remind us that resources do not automatically increase just because equipment upgrades.[^1] [^5]

Today when people buy a fish at the market, they see weight, price, and origin. Unseen are the ports, cold-storage facilities, crew contracts, fisheries organizations, quotas, and monitoring systems that fish may have passed through. Taiwan's fisheries modernization brought the ocean into everyone's daily life, and brought distant ocean governance back to the island. Cianjhen Port's ice, the ship's diesel, the aquaculture pond's water, and the fish on the table were originally different cross-sections of the same history. Understanding this history means understanding how an industry adjusts among national policy, local life, and global markets. Future fisheries policy must still simultaneously handle livelihoods, food safety, resources, and international responsibility — goals that all require transparent data, local participation, stable monitoring, open review, and long-term public discussion, with local fishing villages continuously participating in decision-making.

> **Curator's note:** A maritime nation's maturity lies not in how far its boats can go, but in whether, after going far, it can still clearly explain how it obtains, how it distributes, and how it takes responsibility.
