> **30-Second Overview**
>
> The _Taiwan Minpao_ was not merely a newspaper that mailed news from Tokyo to Taiwan. At its 1923 launch, it absorbed the energy of student organizations, cultural enlightenment, and political reform; later it had to confront the Taiwan Governor-General's censorship, publication permits, and scattered readership. Its significance lies not only in the positions it published, but in how it turned "who can speak, who can understand, what can become a public issue" into a functioning newspaper system.[^1][^2]

# It Didn't Start with a Newspaper, but with an Organizational Network

The _Taiwan Minpao_'s predecessor was not a commercial newspaper that suddenly appeared. In 1920, Taiwanese students in Tokyo formed the Shinminkai (New People's Society), whose goals encompassed both political reform and cultural enlightenment. Thereafter they published _Taiwan Youth_ and _Taiwan_, extending the organization's thinking into fixed textual media. In April 1923, the _Taiwan Minpao_ launched in Tokyo in vernacular Chinese, initially as a semi-monthly; in 1925 it became a weekly.[^1]

This sequence matters. If the _Taiwan Minpao_ is described only as "a progressive newspaper of the Japanese colonial period," it is easy to overlook the school, study-abroad, association, and fundraising networks it relied on. A newspaper does not merely assemble a group of pre-existing readers. It also creates a shared reading time: when a given issue arrives, which article is worth retelling, which local event can become known to people elsewhere on the island. The Academia Sinica newspaper archives therefore record not just titles, but also the shift in frequency from semi-monthly and ten-day period to weekly.[^2]

The _Taiwan Minpao_ is often placed alongside the Taiwan Cultural Association, the Taiwan Parliament Petition Movement, and the Taiwan People's Party. These links exist not because all members consistently followed the same line, but because the paper and the movements shared a pool of personnel, funding, and issues. Lin Cheng-lu's biographical data shows he participated in the Shinminkai, edited _Taiwan Youth_, and later served as the _Taiwan Minpao_'s chief editor. After the paper received permission to print in Taiwan in 1926, he returned to prepare for the move.[^3] This trajectory pulls "editor" back from a desk job to the intersection of political organization, publishing permits, and reader management.

This timeline also shows that newspaper history cannot be summarized with just "launch" and "cessation" dates. Every change in frequency, publishing location, title, and distribution model simultaneously involved readership numbers, printing capacity, administrative permits, and political environment. Researchers who cite only the later newspaper title risk mistaking different periods' editorial systems and speech conditions for the same thing.

# Vernacular Chinese Was Not Ornament, but Reader Policy

Around 1923, Huang Cheng-tsung advocated vernacular Chinese popularization and served as the _Taiwan Minpao_'s general affairs director and publisher. National Library of Taiwan records also note he returned to Taiwan with Wang Min-chuan to promote the paper's mission, recruit readers, and hold island-wide lecture tours. In the paper, he pushed vernacular Chinese and women's suffrage.[^4] These actions show that vernacular Chinese here was not merely a stylistic choice, but an editorial judgment directly tied to "who the newspaper is for."

Readers are not an abstract "Taiwanese people." For a paper to cross regional and educational differences, it must imagine different reading scenarios: some read word-by-word at home, some listen to readings in teahouses or gatherings, some only scan headlines, editorials, or local news. Lecture tours and reader recruitment supplemented the circulation work that print cannot accomplish on its own. A newspaper's influence cannot be measured by circulation alone; one must also see whether it entered lectures, reading clubs, and local organizations.

Therefore, women, farmers, workers, and students were not "society page" topics added later. The National Museum of Taiwan History's _Taiwan Minpao_ facsimile materials note the paper advocated rights for farmers, workers, and women; supported student movements, cultural enlightenment, and the Taiwan Parliament Petition Movement; and promoted vernacular Chinese and Taiwan New Literature.[^5] Placing these issues in one publication presents not a contradiction-free progressive checklist, but the process by which Taiwanese intellectuals of the time tried to translate culture, education, labor, and political reform into each other's terms.

# "Voice of the People" Coexisted with Censorship System

The _Taiwan Minpao_'s public character never formed without resistance. An Academia Sinica Institute of Taiwan History special topic notes the paper positioned itself as "voice of the people," reporting and commenting on political and social events, supporting local autonomy and the Taiwan Parliament Petition Movement. But under the Taiwan Newspaper Ordinance, newspapers had to submit to Governor-General Police Bureau censorship, and critical content was repeatedly banned.[^1]

Here lies a contradiction that cannot be smoothed over: the paper had to obtain institutional recognition while simultaneously making criticism of the system a core content. The Governor-General's permit was not mere administrative procedure, but a power mechanism deciding which texts could enter public circulation. Conversely, censorship did not render the _Taiwan Minpao_ entirely silent. A thesis on journalist Huang Wang-cheng's career notes the paper developed its own practical experience in news gathering, organizational division of labor, and news production. When reporting was restricted, journalists still attempted to reconstruct events through different channels.[^6]

This "constrained but not silenced" state is closer to the paper's actual predicament than the binary "free newspaper versus colonial government." To work in a censored environment, the newsroom had to judge which sentences could run directly, which topics needed reframing, which local news could reach the editorial office via readers or correspondents. Journalistic professionalism here was not neutral technique detached from politics, but a survival technique in colonial society.

# From Tokyo to Taiwan: More Than Printing Presses Moved

In 1926 the _Taiwan Minpao_ received permission to print in Taiwan; Lin Cheng-lu returned to prepare. The Academia Sinica collection special topic further notes that after reorganization as the _Taiwan Shinminpao_ in 1932, it finally received permission for daily publication, becoming the only daily in the market at the time operated by Taiwanese capital.[^3][^7] This shift is often simplified as "the newspaper returned to Taiwan," but it actually involved printing equipment, staffing, reporting lines, distribution channels, and how to sustain newspaper operations closer to the center of power.

After moving to Taiwan, the paper's distance from island readers shrank, and news response speed likely increased. Simultaneously, the newsroom was more directly exposed to Governor-General administrative and police supervision. In other words, geographic proximity was both expansion opportunity and control risk. If the paper only published in Tokyo, it could be seen as an overseas Taiwanese publication. To become an island public medium, it had to enter Taiwan's printing, sales, and communication networks — all subject to colonial government permit systems.

The _Taiwan Minpao_ Weekly issue no. 76 collection page provides a more concrete cross-section than abstract slogans: the October 25, 1925 issue had a weekly table of contents and featured "Immediate Issues of Women's Liberation" as a discussion topic. The collection description places it in the context of farmer, worker, women, student, and cultural movements, calling it indispensable material for studying 1920s–1930s Taiwan.[^7] A newspaper's public character accumulates precisely through these columns, headlines, and recurring reader questions — not through a grand label attached by posterity.

Another Academia Sinica original issue record preserves the _Taiwan Minpao_ no. 152 (April 10, 1927) title, date, and issue topic index. Such issue-by-issue records let researchers ground "the newspaper once discussed politics and society" in concrete pages, not just later generalizations.[^8] The same Taiwan Literature Full-Text Database also preserves four issues of the _Taiwan Minpao_ from September 1926, allowing frequency and publishing rhythm to be verified issue by issue from the original paper.[^11]

# A Newspaper Must Also Operate Like a Company

Political stance cannot pay all printing costs, nor deliver papers to Hualien, Chiayi, or Changhua on its own. A thesis on _Taiwan Minpao_ news gathering and organizational operations notes that to meet the "temporality" of regular publication, the paper had to institutionalize its publishing model, forming clear newsroom division of labor. Reporters' academic backgrounds, writing ability, and publishing experience affected whether they were recruited and how copy was allocated.[^6]

The Academia Sinica special topic further records that after the _Taiwan Shinminpao_ became a daily, island bureaus expanded from Hsinchu to eastern Taiwan, reaching 13 locations at peak. Overseas bureaus once existed in Xiamen, Shanghai, and Dalian.[^1] This expansion should not be understood only as "growing influence." It also meant the paper began handling communication costs, local news credibility, advertising revenue, and page layout — daily problems. From a public intellectual history perspective, the paper's accounting, printing, and distribution are equally conditions for speech to persist.

After becoming a daily, content shifted from commentary-centered to reporting-centered, adding entertainment, arts, and family supplements. Advertising became a major revenue source, using hand-drawn illustrations and slogans to attract readers.[^1] This is not a single trajectory of "ideals corrupted by commerce," but mass media simultaneously facing two temporalities: political events demand immediate response; commercial operations demand fixed publication and stable income. Without the latter, the former is hard to sustain.

From today's data-reading perspective, these page changes have another layer of meaning. Researchers should not just search "Taiwan Minpao" as a title, but separately examine issue numbers, publication dates, language versions, and database image pages. The National Museum of Taiwan History's digital database preserves _Taiwan Youth_, _Taiwan_, _Taiwan Minpao_, and _Taiwan Shinminpao_ separately. Academia Sinica's issue-level records further allow cross-checking single-issue dates and content.[^8][^10] This avoids conflating the 1923 Tokyo semi-monthly, 1925 weekly, and 1932 daily into a single unchanging medium.

# Splits, Reorganizations, and the Newspaper's Multiple Allegiances

After the Cultural Association split in 1927, the _Taiwan Minpao_ left with the right wing, becoming the Taiwan People's Party organ. In 1930 the organization expanded and renamed to _Taiwan Shinminpao_; in 1932 it became a daily.[^3] This history reminds us a newspaper is not a forever-fixed "progressive camp" badge. It changes position with organizational splits, line adjustments, and government permits.

The same paper can be seen by different groups as their tool, and may lose some original readers as editorial and political lines shift. Marking it only by which party it supported misses the more complex relationship between paper and social movements: the paper needed movements for issues and networks; movements needed the paper to translate local events into text the island could collectively discuss.

This multiple allegiance also reflects in personnel mobility. Wang Min-chuan's data shows he worked on _Taiwan Youth_ and early _Taiwan Minpao_, then turned to the New Cultural Association and socialist movements due to internal line differences. Huang Cheng-tsung moved among newspaper work, local organization, sugar policy protests, and religious activities.[^9] The newsroom was not a professional institution isolated from society, but an intersection where people left traces as they moved between organizations.

# From _Taiwan Minpao_ to _Kōnan Shinbun_: Wartime Was Not a Natural Endpoint

The 1932 daily conversion did not bring permanent safety. The National Museum of Taiwan History's facsimile publication notes surviving complete early originals cover April 1923 launch to July 1927, 166 issues total. Some facsimiles show blank pages left by Governor-General censorship and postwar publishing systems.[^7] Blanks are not absence of content, but visible traces left by the joint operation of censorship and preservation systems.

In 1941, the _Taiwan Shinminpao_ was forced to rename as _Kōnan Shinbun_; in 1944 it merged with other papers into _Taiwan Shinpō_. The Academia Sinica special topic places this change under wartime speech control, not as ordinary corporate rebranding.[^1] When a paper must enter the wartime news system, relationships among editorial desk, sources, and readers are rearranged. Pages once available for political criticism were gradually replaced by national mobilization and war propaganda.

Therefore, the _Taiwan Minpao_'s history cannot end with "it was once influential." More worth asking: what content was preserved then, what was deleted. Which papers were saved by whom, which versions only resurfaced later. The National Museum of Taiwan History published eight facsimile volumes precisely because complete early originals fill gaps in facsimiles affected by censorship and publishing systems.[^7] Preservation is not putting newspapers in glass cases, but letting later readers see again how public language was manufactured, constrained, and repaired at the time.

# Why Still Read a Century-Old Newspaper Today

The National Museum of Taiwan History's digital database collects _Taiwan Youth_, _Taiwan_, _Taiwan Minpao_, _Taiwan Shinminpao_, and other publications, covering 1920–1932 Tokyo and Taiwan issues in Chinese and Japanese, with full-text images of political, social, cultural, and literary topics.[^10] Such collections' value lies not only in research convenience, but in restoring "newspaper" as a batch of objects that can be examined page by page.

Readers can compare how the same event appears differently in editorials, news, ads, and supplements; observe which vocabulary recurs, which issues exist only in specific periods. This reading method avoids pouring later historical conclusions back into the past. For 1920s readers, "self-governance," "women's suffrage," and "vernacular Chinese" were not necessarily one ideology. They were juxtaposed and debated in the paper, gradually forming recognizable public issues.

The biggest question the _Taiwan Minpao_ leaves is not whether it fully represented Taiwanese people. Any newspaper has editorial choices, funding sources, reader thresholds, and political positions. It deserves preservation more because it lets us see how a group of people, within colonial institutions, organized a publication, fought for readers, handled censorship, and connected originally scattered local problems into language that could be publicly debated.

This is also why "a newspaper taught an island to speak for itself" cannot be understood as a one-sided enlightenment story. The paper did not speak for everyone, nor automatically bring freedom. It merely provided a public space jointly maintained by editors, reporters, correspondents, fundraisers, sellers, and readers. Some proposed reform there, some criticized the paper's line, some had texts banned, some first learned through reading that other places faced the same problems.

When today's information flows far faster than the 1920s, this history still offers a concrete reminder: publicness does not naturally strengthen with more messages; it requires people to verify, edit, transmit, and people who can recognize which voices are excluded within institutional constraints. The _Taiwan Minpao_'s legacy lies precisely in this speaking method that demands labor, organization, and risk-bearing.
