> **Thirty-Second Overview:** The relationship between Taiwan and North Korea is far more complex than it appears. Before the comprehensive embargo in 2017, Taiwan was North Korea's fourth-largest global export destination, and private economic exchanges between the two sides were extremely vibrant. Pyongyang offices were filled with Acer and Asus computers, while high officials favored brands like Lipton coffee and Pocky; MIT brands had deeply permeated Pyongyang. Although official relations are now frozen, maritime smuggling, high-tech penetration, underground financial laundering, and submarine cable incidents show that this relationship has entered a gray zone, continuously stirring the geopolitical nerves of East Asia. Furthermore, North Korean workers secretly traveled to Taiwan for study tours, and Taiwanese fishing vessels frequently hired North Korean crew members—these little-known deep connections reveal an even more intricate network of interaction between the two regions.

## Official and Economic Relations: From Fourth Largest Export Destination to Comprehensive Sanctions

Although no official diplomatic relations have been established between Taiwan and the Democratic People's Republic of Korea (DPRK), with external affairs managed by a representative office in Singapore, before the tightening of international sanctions in 2017, private economic exchanges between the two sides reached astonishing levels of vibrancy[^1].

According to international trade statistics, in 2016, Taiwan ranked as North Korea's fourth-largest export destination globally, surpassed only by China, India, and the Philippines[^2]. The trade structure at that time showed high complementarity: Taiwanese private entities imported large quantities of North Korea's rich anthracite coal, minerals, and up to 5 tons of premium ginseng annually; simultaneously, Taiwan exported chemical raw materials, precision machinery, textiles, and fishing technology to North Korea[^2]. However, following the international outcry over North Korea's nuclear weapons program, starting in 2017, Taiwan complied with UN Security Council resolutions, completely banning imports and exports to North Korea, bringing official trade value to zero[^1].

In terms of tourism, Taiwanese citizens traveling to North Korea previously often had to transit through China and enter under a "group tour in, group tour out" model[^3]. However, in recent years, due to escalating international sanctions, the lockdown policies implemented by North Korea during the pandemic, and high concerns for national security, Taiwan's Ministry of Foreign Affairs has designated North Korea as a "red" travel warning area.

## Secret Cooperation and Technical Aid: The Taiwanese Presence and Study Traces in Pyongyang Farms

Despite the cold official relationship, Taiwan and North Korea engaged in multiple instances of secret technical and financial cooperation during the 1990s. After the severance of diplomatic ties between Taiwan and South Korea in 1992, North Korea actively sought contact with Taiwan, even proposing to send workers to Taiwan to assist with public works. Liu Tai-ying, a former secretary of the Kuomintang (KMT), revealed that North Korea once sought a loan of $200 million to complete the "Rjong Hotel" and promised to establish a Taiwanese trade zone, but this was abandoned due to pressure from the US side[^4].

During the period of severe food shortages in North Korea's "Arduous March" (1996–1997), the Taiwanese government secretly dispatched agricultural experts to the "Pyongyang Sunan Farm" for technical guidance and provided $1 million worth of agricultural machinery and livestock[^4]. Private exchanges were equally active; Chen Chia-fu, a lobster farmer from Pingtung, was invited to Pyongyang to personally instruct on temperature control technology at the "Datong River Fishery," even receiving approval and hospitality from Kim Jong-un himself.

Notably, in the 1990s, North Korean Workers' Party officials secretly visited Taiwan for study tours, focusing on Taiwan's experience in "processing export zones," such as those in Kaohsiung and Nanzi. They attempted to learn how Taiwan utilized foreign investment and cheap labor to drive economic takeoff, providing a reference point for the later planning of North Korea's Rhoe-seon Economic Zone and Kai-cheng Industrial Zone[^25].

📝 **Curator's Note:** These seemingly scattered unofficial interactions reflect the flexibility and pragmatism with which both sides sought breakthroughs in a harsh international environment. History is not strictly black or white; the gray zone often holds the truest stories.

## Reliance on Daily Necessities: MIT Brands in Pyongyang Offices and Civilian Barter

The degree of affection North Korean society held for Taiwanese goods was beyond imagination. Before the comprehensive embargo, Taiwanese brands were synonymous with "high quality" in North Korea. In government offices and libraries, Acer and Asus computers could often be seen; in civilian life, HCG sanitary ware and drinking water from Taiwan were also favored by locals[^24].

In snack culture, Taiwan's Golden Bear Lipton coffee, Pocky, and Want Want Sanbei were considered the "Holy Trinity" for gifts and afternoon tea among North Korean officials, serving as status symbols in state-run stores like Pyongyang Department Store[^22]. High-ranking North Koreans even attempted to trade their prestigious "Kai-cheng ginseng" for agricultural cultivation techniques for Pear Mountain cold vegetables from Taiwan, demonstrating the strong appeal of Taiwanese consumer technology and refined agriculture.

Beyond official trade, private exchanges between Taiwan and North Korea existed before the sanctions. Public reports and oral testimonies mention a preference in the North Korean market for Taiwanese MSG and other daily necessities, and MIT electronic and civilian brands entered the region through third parties; these fragments resemble "gray civilian circulation under official estrangement" rather than a normal bilateral trade system[^22][^24].

📝 **Curator's Note:** This "brand penetration" proves that commercial power can often pierce the tightest political iron curtain. When North Korean officials used Asus computers and drank Lipton coffee, the border between Taiwan and Korea became blurred in that moment.

## Gray Smuggling, Judicial Verdicts, and High-Tech Penetration: Struggles on the Legal Edge and Underground Finance

As international society tightened sanctions against North Korea, interactions between Taiwan and North Korea increasingly shifted toward hidden gray zones, most notably maritime smuggling, judicial cases, high-tech penetration, and underground finance. These activities not only challenged international regulations but also posed potential threats to Taiwan's national security.

The high seas surrounding Taiwan have become a black market hotspot for North Korea's "shadow fleet" to smuggle refined oil and coal[^5]. These vessels bypass international monitoring through methods like ship-to-ship (STS) transfers, providing essential supplies to North Korea. Judicial bodies in Taiwan have investigated multiple cases involving North Korean smuggling, including several high-profile verdicts:

- **The Shangyuan Fort Case:** A Taiwanese businessman, Huang Wang-gen, was sentenced to 1 year upon conviction for smuggling 3,000 tons of oil to North Korea on the high seas after being photographed by US military satellites[^6].
- **The Legislator's Son Case:** Huang Chong-wei, the son of a former legislator, was sentenced to 2 years and 4 months for allegedly illegally exporting thousands of tons of diesel fuel to North Korea[^7].
- **Smuggling Chains Shielded by Shell Companies:** Taiwanese judicial cases frequently feature verdicts involving shell companies and forged origin documents used to smuggle oil products or controlled materials to North Korea; these cases show that sanctions are not merely diplomatic statements but reach down to the banking, shipping, and intermediary chains[^6][^7].
- **Matsu Sugar Smuggling Case:** In 2018, investigators uncovered "Cheng Yang International" from Matsu using Mongolian-flagged vessels to smuggle sugar to North Korea for industrial alcohol distillation or civilian sustenance[^20].

In terms of underground finance, North Korea often laundered money through shell companies in China and Southeast Asia. These funds sometimes passed through Taiwanese banks before being wired to the US or other regions, exposing the Taiwanese banking sector to high anti-money laundering risks. The Financial Supervisory Commission (FSC) has repeatedly stressed the need for strict investigation and monitoring of all financial transactions involving North Korea, requiring banks to flag abnormal transactions as potential money laundering[^27].

In the high-tech domain, North Korea also continued penetration through gray channels. South Korean investigations indicated that North Korea still smuggled Taiwanese and South Korean semiconductor chips via illicit routes to develop its artificial intelligence technology[^9]. Furthermore, in 2025, the US Department of Justice indicted transnational suspects, including two Taiwanese citizens, accusing them of assisting North Korean IT workers who used American identities to work remotely across the US, thereby earning foreign currency needed for North Korea's weapons development[^10]. These North Korean IT personnel often used "Laptop Farms" in Taiwan and the US as relay stations for remote penetration and fund transfers.

## Military Intelligence and New Security Threats: Taiwan's Role in Geopolitics and Labor in Fisheries

During the Cold War, Taiwan served as a crucial intelligence post for the US and South Korea to monitor North Korea. Listening posts located in Yangmingshan, Taipei, long assisted allies in decoding North Korean radio communications, playing a key role in the regional intelligence network[^4].

In recent years, with shifts in geopolitical circumstances, military intelligence and security issues between Taiwan and North Korea have taken on new forms. Early in the process of Taiwan initiating the Indigenous Defense System (IDS) project, foreign media reported rumors that North Korea was attempting to secretly sell "salmon-class" miniature submarines and torpedo technology to Taiwan; however, this information was firmly denied by the Ministry of National Defense[^11].

Most noteworthy is an international maritime investigation in July 2026. The investigation indicated that the mother company behind a cargo vessel suspected of deliberately cutting Taiwan's submarine cable—the same entity that secretly transported armored vehicles to Kim Jong-un in 2018—was "Do Young Shipping," registered in the Marshall Islands[^12]. This company’s control chain included Taiwanese citizens and shell companies, and some smuggling vessels were even alleged to be directly commanded by the Chinese Navy, making this incident a gray zone vulnerability for Taiwan's national security and highlighting its fragility amid complex international relations[^13].

Furthermore, Taiwanese fishing vessels frequently hired North Korean crew members. Before the tightening of international sanctions in 2017, the number of North Korean crew members on deep-sea Taiwanese fishing vessels reached nearly 300 people. However, to comply with UN sanctions, the Fisheries Administration banned hiring or renewing contracts for North Korean seafarers starting in August 2016, and the employment numbers subsequently dropped rapidly to single digits[^28]. This history of labor in fisheries also reflects Taiwan's policy shift under international sanction pressure.

## Historical Diplomatic Cases and Civilian Legends: From Nuclear Waste to Martial Arts Romance

The history of relations between Taiwan and North Korea includes several unexpected diplomatic cases and civilian legends, stories that not only enrich the aspects of interaction between the two regions but also reflect the changes of the times and the complexity of human nature.

In 1997, Taiwan Power Company planned to transport low-level radioactive waste to North Korea for disposal, but this plan was aborted due to strong pressure from the US and South Korea. However, the North Korean side, having already dug the necessary tunnels, secretly appointed a Taiwanese lawyer to petition the Executive Yuan's Engineering Council, demanding 300 million NTD compensation from Taiwan Power across the sea[^14]. Furthermore, declassified Italian documents suggested an unsolved case where international arms dealers allegedly rerouted North Korean nuclear waste into the waters surrounding Taiwan, adding more mystery to this history.

On the diplomatic front, materials recently declassified by the South Korean Ministry of Foreign Affairs in March 2026 confirmed that in 1995, North Korea had strongly stated its willingness to consider formal diplomatic relations with Taiwan due to dissatisfaction with China and South Korea deepening their ties, attempting to leverage the "Taiwan card" to pressure Beijing[^15]. In April of the same year, China Airlines even successfully operated a charter flight from Taipei to Pyongyang, indicating a moment of subtle change in the relationship between the two regions.

On the civilian level, the romance story between North Korean judoka Lee Chang-soo and Taiwanese judo female athlete Chen Ling-zhen transcends political boundaries. Fearing political persecution and forced labor in North Korea, Lee Chang-soo escaped on an international train from Barcelona to Pyongyang in 1991, successfully defecting and eventually settling in Taiwan, where he naturalized and later served as the head coach of the Taiwanese national judo team[^16]. However, this history is also marked by tragedy. In 1987, North Korean intelligence personnel secretly abducted Shen Jing-yu, a Taiwanese woman, within Japan and forcibly took her to North Korea, representing a rare cross-border human rights conflict between the two nations[^17].

## Geopolitical Strategy and "Two States Theory": A Strategic Warning for Taiwan

In recent years, North Korea's strategic transformation—particularly its constitutional amendment that completely deleted the phrase "one people, one nation" and designated South Korea as an "enemy state"—holds profound geopolitical implications for Taiwan. South Korean President Lee Jae-myung has also publicly stated that "in terms of military security, Taiwan is actually less stable than the Korean Peninsula"[^18].

North Korea's shift toward a "two states theory" proves how fragile "blood ties" are in the face of authoritarian state strategy. This compels Taiwan to accelerate its transition toward a strategic mindset based solely on substantive sovereignty and defense deterrence. Think tank analyses suggest that with North Korea's transformation, Taiwan should proactively develop controls over investment in the DPRK to avoid repeating the mistakes of technology outflow during the early period of advancing into China, thereby safeguarding national security and economic interests[^19].
