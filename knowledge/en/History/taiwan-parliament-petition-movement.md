On January 30, 1921, Lin Hsien-tang, Yeh Rong-chung, and 178 Taiwanese students studying in Tokyo submitted the first petition for the establishment of a Taiwan parliament to the Japanese Imperial Diet. [^1] The document was subsequently submitted fifteen times, and the movement continued until a resolution to discontinue it was passed on September 2, 1934. [^2] It did not result in a Taiwan parliament, but it allowed the question of "who can represent the Taiwanese people" to move from private studies and student dormitories into the center of imperial institutions.

> **30-Second Overview:** On January 30, 1921, 178 people submitted the first petition to the Japanese Imperial Diet. Over fourteen years, the petition group submitted fifteen rounds, experiencing the founding of the Taiwan Cultural Association, the 1923 Police Affairs Incident, the 1927 political line split, and the censorship and relocation of publication of the Taiwan People's Newspaper. [^1] [^2] In 1934, the petition movement did not achieve a Taiwan parliament, but left behind a set of political techniques that wrote the contradictions of colonial rule into legal language, then passed them on to organizations, newspapers, and public discussion.

## Don't Just Abolish the Sixty-Three Law: Lin Cheng-lu Rewrites the Problem as Parliament

At the end of 1920, Taiwanese youth in Tokyo were discussing a seemingly direct goal: abolishing the Sixty-Three Law. This set of laws granted the Taiwan Governor-General broad administrative and legislative discretion, and the petitioners believed it made Taiwan's governance disconnected from substantive oversight by the imperial parliament. [^7]

However, Lin Cheng-lu, a graduate of the University of Tokyo Faculty of Law, proposed a different approach. In an article titled "The Destination of the Sixty-Three Problem" published in the December 15, 1920 issue of Taiwan Youth, he argued that simply demanding the abolition of the Sixty-Three Law might be interpreted as directly incorporating Taiwan into "mainland expansionism." His argument was that, while acknowledging Taiwan's special circumstances, a special parliament elected by the Taiwanese people should be established, so that the Governor-General's powers would be constrained by a representative body. [^2]

This shift was significant. It did not simplify the issue into "whether to be Japanized," nor did it directly portray the petitioners as those who had already put forward a modern national independence plan. The strategy of the petition was to seize upon the empire's own constitutional language. Since Taiwan belonged to the empire, its residents should be able to participate in decisions affecting their lives through a deliberative body. English-language research on the petition also points out that the petitioners mainly criticized the excessive concentration of power by the Governor-General, the lack of representation in advisory bodies, and the absence of effective political appeal channels for the Taiwanese people. [^7]

![Taiwan Parliament Establishment Petition](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/9/97/Petition_Movement_for_the_Establishment_of_a_Taiwanese_Parliament.jpg)

_Photo: Taiwan Parliament Establishment Petition. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain._ [^13]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The sharpest point of the petition movement was not that it placed "Japan" and "Taiwan" as two abstract enemies, but that it used the empire's claimed constitutional principles to turn the question back. If laws only applied locally, whose "unity" was the empire's "unity" uniting?

## One Document, First Passing Through the Hands of Legislators

The first petition on January 30, 1921, was not completed simply by handing the document to the parliament. According to Article 62 of the Diet Law at the time, petitions required sponsorship by Imperial Diet members. Through the introduction by Pastor Toshimune Shigezumi, members of the House of Peers, Jiang Yu-lu, and members of the House of Representatives, Tanegashima Daigorō agreed to serve as sponsors, and the petition entered the review process. [^1]

This detail made the petition movement no longer like an abstract "knocking on Tokyo's door." It was a political task requiring finding intermediaries, preparing legal texts, obtaining signatures, and waiting for parliamentary procedures. The first petition had 178 signatories. In every subsequent round, participants had to find viable paths between the colonial police, the review system, and the indifferent responses of the Imperial Diet. [^1] [^2]

In March 1921, the Japanese Imperial Diet passed Law No. 3, still preserving the appointment legislative powers of the Taiwan Governor-General. The original line demanding the abolition of the Sixty-Three Law thus lost its leverage, and the establishment of a Taiwan parliament became more concentrated as the goal of Taiwan's political movement. [^2]

## Fifteen Rounds Are Not Fifteen Repetitive Submissions

From 1921 to 1934, the petition movement appeared linear. The first round sent documents, the Imperial Diet replied with "not adopted" or "under review," and the petitioners prepared the next round. However, the fifteen rounds of petitions actually passed through different organizations, media, and political environments. The compilation by the Legislative Yuan's Democratic Governance Park points out that between the first and eighth rounds, social support remained strong, and the 1927 petitions even reached their peak. [^2]

During this period, the main bodies of the petitions also changed continuously. Lin Hsien-tang, Lin Cheng-lu, and others provided leadership, resources, and legal arguments. Chiang Wei-shu, Cai Pei-huo, and others connected the petitions with cultural lectures, political societies, and social mobilization. When the participants' generations, classes, and political readings differed, the petition movement could not maintain the same voice forever.

Therefore, "fifteen rounds" were not fifteen times of resending the same document, but fifteen times of re-answering a question: In a colonial system where the Governor-General controlled administrative, legislative, and police powers, what other ways could the Taiwanese people make collective demands? This is also why, even without success, the petition movement is still worth reading as political history rather than just institutional history.

## The Cultural Association Brought Tokyo's Petitions Back to the Island

If the petitions only stayed in Tokyo, they would hardly become a political experience for Taiwanese society. On October 17, 1921, the Taiwan Cultural Association was founded in the auditorium of the Taipei Jing-Shou High School for Girls, with over 300 attendees. The association, with the aim of "promoting culture," pushed for newspaper reading societies, cultural lectures, activity photography, lecture classes, and summer schools. [^4]

Lin Hsien-tang provided resources and reputation, Chiang Wei-shu was responsible for organization and action, and Lin You-chun, Lai Ho, Xie Wen-da, and others connected culture, education, and newspapers with each other. These activities did not necessarily discuss the Taiwan parliament directly, but allowed more people to come into contact with political terms such as "public opinion," "self-governance," "association," and "public discussion." The petition movement thus had echoes on the island, no longer just the Tokyo itinerary of a few students and gentry.

On October 10, 1925, the Taiwan Cultural Association Lecture Team took a photo at the Taiwan People's Newspaper distribution point in the Hsinchu Branch. The people and places in the photo brought the abstract nouns of the cultural movement back to recognizable locations. This was not just a collective photo, but also evidence of the overlap between newspaper circulation, local lectures, and political organizations. [^11]

In December 1922, Cai Pei-huo, Chiang Wei-shu, and others decided to form the Taiwan Parliament Promotion Alliance outside the Cultural Association, giving the petition a more clearly responsible group. In 1923, the Taipei Police Bureau prohibited the alliance from being established on the island. The alliance then reorganized in Tokyo and continued to promote the petition. [^5]

On December 16, 1923, the Government-General launched an island-wide mass reporting, arresting nearly 30 people and detaining them in the Taipei Prison, with Chiang Wei-shu, Cai Pei-huo, and others being arrested and tried. [^5] This "Police Affairs Incident" did not cause the petition to disappear immediately, but clearly drew the boundaries of colonial politics. Documents could be sent to the Imperial Diet, but organizations might be cut off by police bans on associations on the island of Taiwan.

## Newspapers Turn "Not Adopted" into a Public Event

On April 15, 1923, the Taiwan People's Newspaper was founded. It inherited the editorial context of the Taiwan Youth and Taiwan, printed in Tokyo, and then imported into Taiwan after review. The coverage included not only petitions but also social, political, economic, and cultural issues on the island. In 1925, the newspaper changed to a weekly. In August of the same year, the circulation exceeded 10,000 copies. [^6]

![Taiwan People's Newspaper, January 1, 1926 front page](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/be/Front_page_of_The_Taiwan_Minpao_19260101.png)

_Photo: Taiwan People's Newspaper, January 1, 1926 front page. Wikimedia Commons, Public domain._ [^12]

A front page can show how newspapers became objects of political movements. The petition movement was not only present in legislators' introductions and official responses, but also in the pages that readers flipped through every day, the censorship that newspaper offices had to face, and the blanks left after the text was deleted.

On August 1, 1927, the Taiwan People's Newspaper was allowed to be published in Taiwan, but the cost was also written on the pages. The original wording "the only voice of the Taiwanese people" was removed, and the content still had to undergo review in both Japan and Taiwan. [^6] This was not a newspaper that could speak freely, but one of the few media at the time that could place the responses of the Imperial Diet, the disputes of the colony, and the discourse of the Taiwanese people on the same piece of paper.

The petitions were repeatedly met with "not adopted" or "under review." These words looked like procedural terms, but on the newspaper, they became political events that could be discussed. Who was rejected, what were the reasons for rejection, whether to submit again next time, and whether the Taiwanese people had other ways to leave their requests in the public space—all these questions were continuously circulated through the press.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The importance of the Taiwan People's Newspaper was not only that it "supported" the petition, but that it turned each institutional rejection into public materials that readers could see, retell, and argue about. The newspaper did not eliminate censorship, but made censorship itself part of history.

## After the Split, the Petition Did Not End Immediately

In the mid-1920s, there were disagreements within the Cultural Association regarding class, peasant, and labor movements, as well as the limitations of petitions within the system. In 1927, the Cultural Association split. After Lin Hsien-tang, Cai Pei-huo, and others left, they formed the Taiwan People's Party, while the left-wing took control of the New Taiwan Cultural Association. The compilation by the Legislative Yuan's Democratic Governance Park points out that the Taiwan People's Party still supported parliamentary petitions, but the New Taiwan Cultural Association and left-wing figures criticized this approach as too weak. [^2]

This split should not be written as "the united elite suddenly failed later." It was more like the beginning of the divergence of political goals. Some believed that a representative parliament could gradually constrain the Governor-General. Some believed that peasants and laborers must first become the main body of the movement. And some placed the issue of self-governance within a more radical framework of class and national liberation. The supporters of the petition movement thus decreased, but its core goals were still continuously submitted to the Imperial Diet.

What also needs to be preserved is the representativeness limitation of the movement. The early leaders were mostly male gentry, students studying in Japan, and modern-educated intellectuals. They put forward collective political demands for "the Taiwanese people," but this did not mean that every class, gender, and ethnic group at the time could equally participate in decision-making. English-language research points out that the petitioners criticized the non-representativeness of the colonial government on one hand, and were constrained by their own class positions and political resources on the other. [^7] This contradiction did not make the petition lose its value, but reminded us that deliberative politics is not only about asking "whether there is a parliament," but also about asking "who can enter the parliament, whose lives can be represented."

In 1931, the Taiwan People's Party was forced to disband. The petition movement lost an important political support point. At the same time, Japanese militarism and the control of the colonial police intensified, and the space for legitimate political societies became narrower. [^2] After the fifteenth petition failed in 1934, the movement entered its final act.

## Three Hours of Meeting, Ending Fourteen Years

On September 2, 1934, Lin Hsien-tang and more than twenty others met in the conference room of the Da Dong Trust Company in Taichung to discuss whether to continue the petition. Three hours later, they decided to stop the movement. On September 4, Lin Hsien-tang reported to the Governor-General, General Nakano Sadae, accompanied by Chen Shin. [^3]

The stop was not because the petitioners suddenly acknowledged that Taiwan did not need a parliament, but because they judged that the existing political path had already been compressed. The Government-General linked the establishment of a Taiwan parliament with "plotting Taiwan's independence," and demanded that the self-governance movement take a limited reform of local systems. Under these conditions, the political gains of continuing to submit the same petition became increasingly small, while the risks continued to rise. [^3]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The end of this movement did not have a heroic victory scene, only a meeting room, three hours, and a judgment that had to be made by the participants themselves. Political endurance is not only about pushing forward, but also about knowing when to put away a path that can no longer protect people.

## The 1935 Election Is Not a Direct Victory of the Petition

In 1935, the Taiwan Governor-General promoted local system reforms, and semi-elected seats appeared in local councils such as prefectural councils, municipal councils, and village committees. Academic research points out that at that time, the voting rights were still restricted by conditions such as age, gender, residence, and tax payment, and were not universal suffrage. [^8]

This reform was in the same context of self-governance politics as the Taiwan Parliament Establishment Petition Movement, but it cannot be simplified as "the petition movement directly replaced local elections." On one hand, the discourse of representation and self-governance put forward by the petitioners for a long time did make political participation an unavoidable issue. On the other hand, the 1935 system only offered limited seats and limited qualifications, and the prefectural governors and local officials still retained important powers. [^8] [^9]

The 1935 election was thus a narrow doorway. It allowed a portion of male taxpayers to obtain limited local voting rights, but did not turn Taiwan into a democratic society governed by the general population. Writing this narrow doorway as the "achievement" of the petition movement would erase the fact that the colonial system still retained appointed seats and administrative control. Writing it completely as unrelated to the petition would also fail to see how the long-term discourse of self-governance changed the boundaries of political issues.

Therefore, the historical legacy of the petition movement should not be treated as a simple choice between "success" or "failure." It did not complete its original institutional goals, but left behind three kinds of political capabilities that appeared repeatedly later. First, to write inequality into a verifiable claim. Second, to pass the claim on to organizations and media. Third, to re-decide the next step after being rejected. These capabilities also appeared in Taiwan's local autonomy, social movements, and democratization history, but each era had to face its own institutional limitations again.

## Questions Left by One Petition

In 1921, 178 people sent a petition to the Imperial Diet. In 1934, more than twenty people decided to stop in a conference room of a trust company in Taichung. Between the two scenes were fifteen rounds of petitions, the lectures of the Cultural Association, the arrests of the Police Affairs Incident, the censorship and relocation of publication of the Taiwan People's Newspaper, and the split of the political line. [^1] [^3]

This history should not be condensed into "democratic seeds." The seed metaphor easily covers up colonial rule, class differences, and internal struggles within the movement. A more accurate way of saying it is that the Taiwan Parliament Establishment Petition Movement turned self-governance into a public job that could be written, signed, introduced, reviewed, reported, argued, and rejected by the colonial government.

Today, when reading that document, what is most worth pausing for is not that it failed, but that it allowed a governed group to begin repeatedly practicing how to put forward governance demands. That petition did not become a bill of the parliament in the end, but became a syntax that is still in use in Taiwan's political life later. First, make the request clear, then find people to sign together, and then let the rejection leave a name.
