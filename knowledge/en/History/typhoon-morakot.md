On August 8, 2009, Typhoon Morakot brought extreme rainfall, triggering a cascade of floods, debris flows, and landslides in the southern and eastern mountainous regions. The village of Xiaolin was buried by earth and rockslides from Mount Xian-du; official reconstruction bulletins preserved in the Executive Yuan’s Reconstruction Database recorded, based on household registration statistics, that 407 people perished in Xiaolin. [^1]

Yet, the most difficult aspect of Morakot to comprehend lies not in the scale of the disaster, but in where “reconstruction” was meant to lead people after the disaster ended. Houses could be built within months, but the relationships between tribes and land, work, ancestors, and public decision-making do not automatically restore simply because one holds the keys to a new home.

**After a disaster ends, what is hardest to rebuild may not be the houses, but the relationship between people and their homeland, between land and decision-making power.**

## From Torrential Rain to “Permanent Houses”

Following Morakot, the government and civil society quickly shifted relief efforts into resettlement. The Ministry of the Interior’s official Q&A manual explains that permanent houses are provided with land by the government and built using charitable donations, with the primary purpose of solving housing issues rather than serving as compensation for property loss. [^2]

This design seems clear, yet it compresses a complex issue into a single housing question: whether people have a place to live. For Indigenous communities living in mountainous areas, houses are rarely isolated structures; they are connected to farmland, hunting grounds, streams, ritual sites, kinship networks, and tribal assemblies. Moving a house to a safe location may simultaneously distance people from other essential aspects of their lives.

The _Special Provisions for Post-Morakot Typhoon Reconstruction_, announced in late August 2009, transformed post-disaster reconstruction into a national project with special budgets, special procedures, and administrative coordination mechanisms. The Executive Yuan stated at the time that the provisions were intended to accelerate reconstruction, raise funds, and establish a dedicated mechanism for promoting reconstruction. [^3]

Thus, “permanent” ceased to be merely an adjective for housing and became a policy commitment: the government hoped to use a stable, rapid, and manageable space to end the drift for disaster victims. But for many survivors, the real question was not whether they would have a room, but who had the right to decide what counted as safe and what counted as home.

## Xiaolin Village: Memory Cannot Be Confined to a Memorial Park

Xiaolin Village later became the symbol of Morakot reconstruction. Official reconstruction bulletins recorded that on the third anniversary of the typhoon, Xiaolin villagers returned to the Xiaolin Shrine in Wuli-pu to mourn the deceased; three resettlement bases had a total of 276 permanent houses built, along with facilities such as Xiaolin Elementary School, the Pingpu Cultural Park, and a memorial park. The memorial park was selected on higher ground overlooking the original site, allowing rituals and memory to remain close to the disaster scene. [^1]

These facilities are important because reconstruction requires not only bedrooms, kitchens, and roads, but also a place where a community can recognize itself. Shrines, ritual squares, schools, and parks ensure that lost villages do not remain merely statistical figures; they return death, kinship, and history to public space.

But commemorating a place and reclaiming a place are not the same thing. The experience of Xiaolin Village reminds us that resettlement bases can preserve fragments of memory, but they do not necessarily restore original livelihoods and social relations. If houses are far from work, farming, and tribal networks, life may still require crossing an invisible distance every day.

## NGOs Can Fill Gaps, But Cannot Replace Tribal Decision-Making

Morakot reconstruction was not an engineering project completed by the government alone. The Reconstruction Database preserved by the National Museum of Science and Technology lists multiple plans covering infrastructure, homeland, employment, industry, agriculture, and community resource integration, showing that post-disaster work relied simultaneously on central ministries, local governments, and civil organizations. [^4]

Academic research, using two Indigenous disaster-affected communities and three large NGOs as case studies, proposed a four-stage framework for NGO-led reconstruction: preparation, initiation, planning and construction, and monitoring and livelihood recovery. The research also warns that while NGOs can fill gaps in government capacity, experienced and resource-rich NGOs may inadvertently dominate decision-making; trust and organizational philosophy affect whether reconstruction can truly be used by the community. [^5]

The difference here is subtle but decisive for the direction of reconstruction. External groups can bring engineers, social workers, fundraising capabilities, and administrative experience, but they cannot automatically know how a tribe should live simply because they can build houses quickly. If reconstruction pursues only engineering progress, resident participation is easily reduced to mere briefing sessions; if reconstruction treats residents as co-decision-makers, the process may take longer, but the results are more likely to endure.

## Between “Safety” and “Going Home”

Post-disaster, the government must address real geological risks and cannot romanticize returning home. Research indicates that Indigenous communities are often located in areas prone to natural disasters; if reconstruction policies do not fully consider actual needs, they may create new cultural conflicts while protecting residents. Related studies view community participation and local identity as important conditions for effective disaster governance. [^6]

Housing recovery research over a decade later also points out that the off-site housing policy after Morakot was implemented rapidly under time and institutional pressure, resulting in insufficient Indigenous participation; when relocation sites and housing options were predetermined, subsequent improvements often only addressed partial issues, leaving the fundamental difficulties of livelihood and cultural preservation intact. [^7]

This is not about choosing a side between safety and culture, but requiring safety itself to be defined more completely. Housing recovery research indicates that when relocation sites and housing options are predetermined, subsequent improvements often only address partial issues, leaving the fundamental difficulties of livelihood and cultural preservation intact. [^7] Geologists can assess slope, stream, and debris flow risks; Indigenous people know how a specific piece of land connects to family, rituals, farming, and seasons. If only one type of knowledge enters decision-making, the assessment may be reasonable in engineering terms but fail in daily life.

## The Long-Term Bill for “Permanent Houses”

The Council of Inspection’s 2022 investigation turned “permanent houses” from a policy achievement into a bill requiring continued reading. The investigation pointed out that as of April 2021, there were 43 bases and 3,583 households associated with Morakot permanent houses; among Indigenous permanent house relocation households, 2,379 households (85%) were displaced from both disaster zones and their hometowns. The report raised issues regarding land rights, tripartite contracts, housing quality, tribal fragmentation, and safety re-surveys of original residences. [^8]

“Displaced from disaster and hometown” is significant because it describes more than geographical distance. Leaving one’s homeland may mean leaving behind original work, farmland, kinship care, tribal politics, and cultural practices; if new bases lack sufficient transportation, employment, and public services, housing may become a container that places people inside but does not move their lives along with them.

The Ministry of the Interior’s early Q&A manual stated that after accepting permanent houses, residents generally could not return to their original residences to live or build houses; the same Q&A also mentioned that in cases of collective relocation, the government could handle residents who did not meet general qualifications but had factual residence, in order to maintain tribal organization and cultural transmission. [^2] Placing these two regulations together reveals two tensions in the system: on one hand, to move people away from risk; on the other, to acknowledge that tribes cannot be fragmented.

The Council of Inspection’s subsequent investigation therefore required that safety re-surveys of original residences not be completed solely by geological, civil engineering, and soil and water conservation experts, but also incorporate ethnic, cultural, social, and ecological perspectives, and allow Indigenous participation in site selection, decision-making, and negotiation. [^8] This is not about handing professional judgment to a single group, but acknowledging that “habitable” and “worth returning to” require joint judgment.

## Ensuring Reconstruction Is More Than Just “Completion”

The plans listed in the Morakot Reconstruction Database already reveal an important fact: post-disaster reconstruction is never just about building houses. Infrastructure, employment, agriculture, industry, community empowerment, and cultural seeds all need to operate continuously beyond housing engineering. [^4]

Academic observations on NGOs also point out that reconstruction needs to extend from housing to livelihood recovery and long-term monitoring. [^5] If policies declare completion only upon house delivery, the parts that need the most time—rebuilding work, repairing community relationships, children growing up between old and new landscapes, and tribal decision-making functioning again—will slowly occur outside administrative reports.

What Morakot left behind is not a simple disaster story, but a question of state governance: when the government must protect people rapidly, how can it simultaneously preserve people’s right to participate in deciding the future? The Xiaolin reconstruction case recorded by the Taiwan Society of Anthropology and Ethnology shows that villagers once demanded “to rebuild Xiaolin,” striving for space to decide the form of their home through collective discussion, voting, and architectural planning. [^9] When a tribe needs to relocate, how can migration be made not a passive loss, but a shared planning process with choices, resources, and cultural continuity?

**True post-disaster reconstruction is not about erasing people from a danger map, but allowing people to still say “this is our home” on a new map.**

After Morakot, Taiwan accumulated more disaster prevention engineering, early warning systems, and reconstruction institutions; but every time extreme weather arrives, the same question is asked anew: who defines safety, and who decides home? The most important legacy of this typhoon may not be when a batch of permanent houses was completed, but that Taiwan began to see that reconstruction truly begins only after the houses are finished.
