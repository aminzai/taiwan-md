On August 8, 2009, Typhoon Morakot brought extreme rainfall, triggering floods, landslides, and collapses across mountainous areas in southern and eastern Taiwan. Xiaolin Village was buried by a landslide from the sacred mountain; the official reconstruction database maintained by the Executive Yuan records 407 fatalities in Xiaolin Village based on household registration statistics.[^1]

Yet the most difficult aspect of Morakot is not merely the scale of the disaster, but what "reconstruction" is supposed to carry people toward after the disaster ends. Houses can be built within a few months, but the relationships between the tribe, land, livelihood, ancestors, and public decision-making do not automatically restore themselves simply because someone receives a new key.

**After a disaster, the hardest thing to rebuild may not be the house, but the relationship between people and their homeland, between land and decision-making power.**

## From Torrential Rain to "Permanent Housing"

After Morakot, government and civil society quickly shifted rescue efforts into resettlement. The Ministry of the Interior's official Q&A clarifies that permanent housing is provided with government land and built with charitable donations, primarily aimed at solving housing needs rather than compensating for property losses.[^2]

This design appears clear, yet it compresses a complex issue into a housing problem: whether people have a roof over their heads. For Indigenous communities living in mountainous areas, a house is usually not an isolated building; it connects to farmland, hunting grounds, streams, ritual sites, kinship networks, and tribal council meetings. Moving a house to a safe location may simultaneously move away other parts of life.

The _Morakot Post-Disaster Reconstruction Special Act_, released at the end of August 2009, turned post-disaster reconstruction into a national project with special budgets, special procedures, and administrative coordination mechanisms. The Executive Yuan explained at the time that the Act was intended to accelerate reconstruction, raise funds, and establish dedicated mechanisms for promoting reconstruction.[^3]

Thus "permanent" ceased to be merely an adjective describing shelter and became a policy promise: the government hoped to use a stable, fast, and manageable space to end the displacement of disaster victims. But for many survivors, the real question was not whether they had a house, but who had the right to decide what counts as safety and what counts as home.

## Xiaolin Village: Memories Cannot Be Confined to a Memorial Park

Xiaolin Village later became a symbol of Morakot reconstruction. The official reconstruction report records that on the third anniversary of the disaster, Xiaolin residents returned to the Xiaolin Ancestral Hall in Lijia to mourn the deceased; three resettlement sites jointly built 276 permanent housing units, along with Xiaolin Elementary School, the Paiwan Cultural Garden, and a memorial park. The memorial park was chosen at a higher elevation overlooking the original site, allowing rituals and memory to remain close to the disaster area.[^1]

These facilities are important because reconstruction needs more than bedrooms, kitchens, and roads—it needs places where a community can recognize itself. Ancestral halls, ritual squares, schools, and parks prevent the lost village from being reduced to mere statistics; they return death, kinship, and history to public space.

But commemorating a place and re-owning a place are not the same thing. The experience of Xiaolin Village reminds us that resettlement sites can preserve some memories but do not necessarily restore original livelihoods and social relationships. If housing is separated from work, farming, and tribal networks, life may still require crossing an invisible distance every day.

## NGOs Can Fill Gaps, But Cannot Replace Tribal Decisions

Morakot reconstruction was not a project completed by the government alone. The reconstruction database preserved by the National Science and Technology Museum lists multiple plans covering infrastructure, homelands, employment, industry, agriculture, and community resource integration, showing that post-disaster work relied simultaneously on central ministries, local governments, and civil organizations.[^4]

Academic research, using two Indigenous disaster-affected communities and three major NGOs as cases, proposes a four-stage framework for NGO-led reconstruction: preparation, initiation, planning and construction, and monitoring and livelihood recovery. The research also warns that NGOs can fill gaps in government capacity, but experienced and resource-rich NGOs may equally end up quietly taking charge of decisions; trust and organizational ideals influence whether reconstruction is truly driven by the community.[^5]

The distinction here is subtle yet determines the direction of reconstruction. External groups can bring engineers, social workers, fundraising capabilities, and administrative experience, but they cannot automatically know how a tribe should live just because they can quickly build houses. If reconstruction only pursues engineering progress, resident participation is easily relegated to explanatory meetings; if residents are treated as co-decision-makers, the process may take longer but the outcome is more likely to endure.

## Between "Safety" and "Going Home"

After the disaster, the government must deal with real geological risks and cannot romanticize return. Research shows that Indigenous communities often settle in disaster-prone areas; if reconstruction policies fail to adequately consider actual needs, they may create new cultural conflicts while protecting residents. Studies identify community participation and local identity as key conditions for effective disaster governance.[^6]

Housing recovery research conducted more than ten years later also points out that the relocation housing policies implemented after Morakot were rapidly pushed forward under time and institutional pressures, resulting in insufficient Indigenous participation; when relocation sites and housing options are pre-determined, subsequent improvements can only address partial problems, while fundamental difficulties in livelihood and cultural preservation remain.[^7]

This is not about choosing between safety and culture, but about defining safety more completely. Housing recovery research notes that when relocation sites and housing options are pre-determined, later improvements can only handle local issues, leaving fundamental challenges in livelihood and cultural preservation unresolved.[^7] Geologists can assess risks on slopes, streams, and landslides; tribe members also know how a piece of land connects to family, rituals, farming, and seasons. If only one type of knowledge enters decision-making, the assessment may be reasonable on an engineering level but fail in daily life.

## The Long Bill of Permanent Housing

The 2022 audit by the Control Yuan turned "permanent housing" from a policy achievement into a bill that still needs to be read. The audit found that by April 2021, there were 43 permanent housing sites related to Morakot, covering 3,583 households; among Indigenous permanent housing relocation households, 2,379 households—accounting for 85%—were displaced both from the disaster and from their hometowns. The report also raised issues regarding land rights, tripartite contracts, living quality, tribal segmentation, and safety re-surveys of original sites.[^8]

The importance of "displaced from both disaster and hometown" lies in the fact that it describes not just geographical distance. Leaving one's hometown may mean leaving behind original work, farmland, family care, tribal politics, and cultural practices; if new sites lack sufficient transportation, employment, and public services, housing may become a container that houses people without bringing life along.

The Ministry of the Interior's early Q&A once stated that after accepting permanent housing, residents are generally not allowed to return to their original residences to live or build houses; the same Q&A also mentioned that during collective relocation, the government may handle residents who do not meet general qualifications but have actual living needs in order to maintain tribal organization and cultural inheritance.[^2] Placed together, these two provisions reveal two opposing forces within the system: on one hand, people must be moved away from risk; on the other hand, it is recognized that tribes cannot be fragmented.

The Control Yuan's subsequent investigation therefore requires that safety re-surveys of original sites not be completed solely by geology, civil engineering, and soil and water conservation experts, but should also incorporate ethnic, cultural, social, and ecological perspectives, and allow Indigenous peoples to participate in site selection, decision-making, and negotiations.[^8] This is not about handing professional judgment over to a single group, but about recognizing that "livability" and "worth returning to" require joint judgment.

## Letting Reconstruction Be More Than Just "Completion"

The plans listed in the Morakot reconstruction database already reveal an important fact: post-disaster reconstruction has never been just about building houses. Infrastructure, employment, agriculture, industry, community capacity building, and cultural seeds all need to continue operating beyond housing projects.[^4]

Academic observations on NGOs also point out that reconstruction needs to extend from housing to livelihood recovery and long-term monitoring.[^5] If policies declare completion only when houses are handed over, the most time-consuming parts—re-establishing work, repairing social relationships, children growing up between old and new landscapes, and tribal decision-making being re-activated—will instead unfold slowly outside of administrative reports.

Morakot left behind not a simple disaster story, but a problem of state governance: when the government must quickly protect people, how can it simultaneously preserve people's right to participate in deciding the future? The case study of Xiaolin reconstruction recorded by the Taiwan Society of Anthropology and Ethnology shows that villagers once demanded "rebuilding Xiaolin" and, through collective discussion, voting, and architectural planning, fought for the space to decide the form of their own homeland.[^9] When a tribe needs to relocate, how can migration become not a passive loss but a shared plan with choice, resources, and cultural continuity?

**True post-disaster reconstruction is not about erasing people from the danger map, but allowing them to say "this is our home" on a new map.**

After Morakot, Taiwan accumulated more disaster prevention infrastructure, early warning systems, and reconstruction institutions; yet every time extreme weather arrives, the same question is asked again: who defines safety, and who decides home? Perhaps the most important legacy of this disaster is not when a batch of permanent housing units are completed, but that Taiwan began to see that after houses are finished, reconstruction truly begins.
