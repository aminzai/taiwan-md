# US Aid: How a Sack of Flour Became a Development System

In 1951, US economic aid to Taiwan was restarted. This period, commonly referred to as the "US Aid era," lasted until 1965. [^1] In 1954, US President Eisenhower signed Public Law 480, allowing surplus agricultural products to enter Taiwan's relief network through government and private organizations. [^1] Yet in 1956, the US State Department acknowledged in a policy report that although US aid had brought economic progress, Taiwan had not yet reached a point where it could stand on its own. [^2]

Placing these three time points together makes the term "US aid" seem less like a donation sent from Washington. It resembles more a translation machine: the US sent security interests, surplus materials, funds, and technology into Cold War Asia. Taiwan's bureaucrats, businesses, schools, rural areas, and welfare organizations then translated them into factories, roads, milk powder rations, hygiene education, and foreign exchange regulations.

> **30-Second Overview:** After the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950, the US integrated Taiwan into the Asia-Pacific security order to prevent the conflict from spreading southward. From 1951 to 1965, US aid, set against the backdrop of military security, drove capital, technology, trade, education, public health, and social relief. It supported the financial and security needs of the Nationalist government after its relocation to Taiwan, and enabled Taiwan's policy institutions to learn how to allocate scarce resources. The conditions, oversight, and Cold War objectives of the aid were equally inscribed into this history of development.

## The Korean War Pushed Taiwan into the US Asian Defense Line

On June 25, 1950, North Korea crossed the 38th parallel to attack South Korea. The Office of the Historian at the US State Department noted that before the outbreak of the Korean War, US officials had originally prepared to allow the Chinese Communist Army to cross the sea and defeat Chiang Kai-shek. The Korean War changed this judgment. The US sent the Seventh Fleet into the Taiwan Strait, not with the immediate goal of turning Taiwan into a complete ally, but first to prevent the Korean War from spreading southward. Troops prepared to attack Taiwan were redirected to the Korean Peninsula, thereby delaying military conflict in the Taiwan Strait. [^10]

President Truman's statement on June 27, 1950, articulated this turning point directly:

> “Accordingly I have ordered the 7th Fleet to prevent any attack on Formosa.”
>
> ——Harry S. Truman, June 27, 1950 [^11]

This order contained two directions simultaneously. The US Navy was to prevent any party from using Taiwan as a base to attack mainland China, and also to prevent the Chinese Communists from extending the civil war into Taiwan. Truman's statement therefore required the Taiwan side to cease air and sea operations against the mainland, and to leave Taiwan's future political status to be determined by peace arrangements with Japan or United Nations considerations. This was not a treaty permanently incorporating Taiwan into the US defense perimeter, but a temporary military arrangement taken to control the front lines during the Korean War crisis. [^11]

The temporary arrangement quickly became a long-term structure. In 1951, while the Korean War was still ongoing, US economic aid to Taiwan was restarted. After the 1953 armistice, the Seventh Fleet did not return the Taiwan Strait to the state of early 1950. In 1954, tensions rose around Kinmen, Matsu, and Dachen Island, and the People's Republic of China began shelling the offshore islands. The debate within the US was not whether to acknowledge Taiwan, but to what extent to protect the Republic of China government, how to avoid being dragged back into the Chinese civil war, and whether the offshore islands fell within the scope of the US defense commitment. In 1955, the US Congress passed the Formosa Resolution, authorizing the President to defend Taiwan and the offshore islands. [^10]

Therefore, the Korean War context cannot simply be placed in the first paragraph of an article and then disappear. It explains why economic aid appeared simultaneously with military security, and why the administrative system of US aid needed to handle military supplies, food, industrial raw materials, and social stability. For the US, Taiwan could not lose its defense capability due to economic collapse. For the Taiwan government, aid was not just a bandage, but an opportunity to translate military priorities into industrial and fiscal policies. [^2] [^12]

This translation required time. Before 1949, Taiwan already faced administrative dilemmas left by postwar reception, inflation, and material shortages. The Korean War pushed the situation in another direction; the government had to simultaneously maintain the military, restore production, supply urban life, and ensure that rural areas did not lose production capacity due to conscription and price controls. Aid was thus broken down into many different tasks. Raw materials and equipment entered the Industrial Committee's plans, food and surplus agricultural products entered the warehouses of relief organizations, and technical cooperation entered ministries, schools, and rural health work. These tasks were not necessarily executed by the same unit, but all gained priority under the security judgment that "Taiwan must maintain stability." [^4] [^5]

Precisely because of this, the beneficiaries of US aid are difficult to generalize into a single group. The military saw defense capability. Industrial bureaucrats saw foreign exchange, equipment, and loans. Rural workers saw fertilizer, hygiene, and disaster relief. Urban families remembered milk powder, flour, and a ration card. Only by placing these experiences back into the Korean War context can we understand why US aid was simultaneously military, economic, and social, rather than mistaking one aspect for the whole.

A difference of two days is small, but the gap in policy is large. The entry of the Seventh Fleet into the Taiwan Strait turned Taiwan from a retreat point in the KMT-CCP civil war into a security node on the US line in the Western Pacific. The State Department's brief history of the Korean War also noted that the Korean War made the containment policy advocated by NSC-68 the diplomatic foundation of the US government. [^12] Subsequent economic aid was therefore difficult to understand separately from military security. Taiwan needed funds, food, and technology. The US needed a Taiwan that could maintain defense, avoid regime collapse, and not become entirely dependent on endless aid. This connection between security and development is the most important background the Korean War left for the US aid era. It predetermined under what conditions aid would arrive, and who would use it first.

## The Problem Before Yin Zhongrong: Where Should the Money Flow First?

Beside this translation machine, we can first look at Yin Zhongrong. Official historical materials from the Ministry of Finance list his positions as Convener of the Industrial Committee of the Economic Stabilization Commission of the Executive Yuan, Minister of Economic Affairs, and Deputy Chairman of the Executive Yuan Committee on US Aid. The problem he handled was not "whether to accept US aid," but how to connect raw materials, equipment, financing, foreign exchange, and enterprises. [^3]

Official historical materials describe how Yin Zhongrong used US aid resources at the Industrial Committee to develop chemical, food, transportation, telecommunications, and general industries, and also assisted domestic enterprises in applying for financing loans. This list effectively illustrates the nature of US aid: it was not just about putting finished goods into warehouses, but trying to arrange imported raw materials, domestic production, and the next round of investment on the same policy map. [^3]

📝 Curator's Note: **The true difficulty of US aid was not delivering resources to Taiwan, but deciding which industry received the resources, who managed them, and when the market should take over.**

Yin Zhongrong left another reminder. He advocated for light industries for people's livelihood and opposed backward countries blindly chasing heavy industry, stating: "In the process of economic development, backward countries' particular emphasis on heavy industry often leads to economic imbalance and disconnection, inflation, and excessive lowering of people's living standards—chaotic phenomena. Even if successful, the cost outweighs the benefit." [^3]

The point of this statement is not to label Yin Zhongrong as a "visionary," but to reveal the choice space of the recipient government. External resources indeed change what can be done, but they do not determine the sequence of every policy for local governments. The history left by US aid also includes how Taiwan's bureaucrats made trade-offs between security pressure, material shortages, and industrial expectations.

## The US Aid Committee: Turning Aid into Administrative Language

US aid permeated daily life through institutions. After the US Congress passed the Foreign Aid Act in 1948, the government established the Executive Yuan Committee on US Aid, responsible for the receipt, management, and distribution of materials. After the outbreak of the Korean War, the US restarted economic and technical aid to the Republic of China. [^4]

The Archives of the Institute of Modern History, Academia Sinica, extend this line further: Taiwan's initial US aid funds and materials were operated jointly by the US side and the US Aid Committee. In the 1960s, the US Aid Committee was reorganized into the Committee on International Economic Cooperation and Development (IECD). Another important institution, the Agricultural Reconstruction Committee (ARC), participated in rural hygiene and education. [^5] Here, aid was not just a ledger entry, but a meeting, a department, a work schedule, and an administrative process requiring cooperation from local institutions.

📝 Curator's Note: **When an aid truly leaves a mark, it is often not when it is spent, but when it becomes a working method used daily by a certain institution.**

This photo, taken around 1950, cannot directly prove that everyone standing in the frame was related to US aid. It reminds us that as aid restarted, Taiwan was also mobilizing manpower, organizing the military, and rearranging family life. Postwar economic history is not just about factories and ledgers; it also includes the bodily experience of a group of people waiting for the next order on a platform.

The Official Archives Support Teaching Network summarizes the use of US aid in Taiwan's economy into three levels: technology, capital, and trade. [^4] This classification does not mean all projects neatly fall into three drawers. It is more like a reading map: funds can buy equipment, equipment requires technology, and technology relies on trade and foreign exchange to sustain itself.

This also explains why US aid, land reform, agricultural production, and later industrialization often appear simultaneously in postwar economic history. Land reform changed rural ownership and production incentives, with some landlord compensation flowing into industry. US aid provided another set of capital, technology, and policy tools. [^6] The two interacted, but they were not the same thing, and Taiwan's later growth cannot be reduced to a single cause.

## The Second Leg of a Sack of Flour

Public Law 480 best allows us to see the distance between aid and local life. The "Development of Agricultural Trade and Assistance Act," passed in 1954, dealt with US surplus agricultural products on one hand, and also sought overseas markets for US agricultural products on the other. Strictly speaking, these materials did not belong to the Taiwan US Aid plan, but were often included in statistics under economic aid. Research by Zhao Zhenyang estimates that the amount allocated by Public Law 480 accounted for approximately 24% of Taiwan's total US aid. [^1]

After flour, milk powder, butter, and cheese arrived in Taiwan, they did not automatically become "relief." Materials were first sent into meetings by the US Aid Committee according to the distribution plans of welfare organizations, then coordinated by the Ministry of the Interior, local governments, churches, and distribution centers. In a 1955 survey, only 7 of the 18 inspected locations maintained complete distribution records. The study also recorded that some materials were resold, exchanged, or even used to feed pigs because they did not fit local dietary habits. [^7]

These frictions are not trivial details. They illustrate the gap between "aid arriving" and "aid being effective," bridged by institutional design, local knowledge, and power relations. For central agencies, a ration card was a manageable roster. For recipients, it might be an unfamiliar food. For local organizations, it was simultaneously relief work, administrative burden, and a responsibility under supervision.

📝 Curator's Note: **The object that best illustrates a system is often not a grand construction, but a milk powder can that a local family did not know how to use.**

The research leaves sharper questions: The US Aid Committee and the US side required recording, packaging recovery, and prohibited resale, but local distributors did not necessarily believe these rules met on-site needs. In 1961, the Christian Welfare Association shifted from simple relief to cultivation and professional social welfare. A thesis cited a 1962 _New York Times_ report stating that the household物资 plan gradually ceased due to unfair distribution, black markets, and supervision issues, but charitable institutions, hospitals, schools, and disaster relief continued. [^7]

Thus, Public Law 480 was not a straight story of "the US sends food, Taiwan receives it." It was a net pulled out jointly by US agencies, Taiwan's administrative departments, churches, local governments, fishing associations, rural areas, and recipient families. The denser the net, the more likely relief was to reach those in need, and the more easily it exposed who was not seen.

## Whose Security Did This Aid Serve?

The other side of US aid was clearly written in the US State Department's 1956 policy report. The report stated that the Nationalist government's maintenance of military forces sufficient to defend Taiwan and Penghu exceeded Taiwan's economic capacity. Taiwan had made economic progress under US aid, but "self-support is not in sight." The report then put a question on the table: Should future US economic policy and aid also be used to strengthen Taiwan's economy, making it part of political and military objectives? [^2]

This document made the distance between "aid" and "charity" visible. US interests included blocking communist forces, maintaining the military position in the Taiwan Strait, and stabilizing an important Cold War ally. Taiwan needed food, capital, equipment, technology, and foreign exchange. The goals of both sides could intersect, but were not identical.

English-language research also places 1950s aid into the post-Korean War Asia-Pacific containment and regional security, analyzing how aid entered different economic sectors, affected Taiwan's foreign trade, and how Taiwan moved from a recipient status to self-supporting growth. [^8] This international perspective reminds us: Taiwan's postwar development cannot be described simply as "the US helped Taiwan," nor summarized as "the US used Taiwan." What truly happened was the entanglement of security interests, local policies, and social life.

## After 1959, Aid Began to Cultivate "Those Who Were Not Yet There"

The long-term effects of aid appeared in places that did not immediately become factories. Research by Fu Liyu on secondary science education during the US aid era points out that while US aid started in 1951 and ended in 1965, the secondary science education plan was not included until 1959. This plan formed in an environment where US defense science aid, shortages of scientific talent, the long-term scientific goals of scholars like Hu Shih, and Taiwan's existing education system intersected. [^9]

This is a significant time gap: US aid did not only pursue immediately visible outputs upon arrival in Taiwan. As education and science plans entered the aid framework, debates followed, including how American-style science education would coexist with local school culture, whether exam-orientedism would change the plan's goals, and whose legitimacy was greater: "defense needs" or "long-term academic development." [^9]

📝 Curator's Note: **Some aid outcomes are not a factory that can be cut open for ribbon-cutting today, but a group of people who only knew years later that they had been cultivated.**

This also allows us to re-examine the common summary "Taiwan made good use of US aid." It captured part of the fact: Taiwan indeed transformed external resources into industry, administrative capacity, and educational investment. But "making good use" is not a frictionless virtue, but a series of concrete institutional choices. Who received loans, who distributed materials, and which science was prioritized for cultivation would all affect what Taiwan later grew into.

## After 1965, What Was Left Behind?

Research estimates that the total approved execution amount of US aid projects between 1951 and 1965 was $1.433 billion, averaging about $100 million per year, approximately 5%–10% of GNP at the time. [^1] This was a resource sufficient to change policy options, but the numbers themselves cannot answer "why Taiwan was able to grow later." They only tell us to what extent Taiwan at that time placed development on the timeline of external resources.

The end of general economic aid in 1965 can be viewed as an institutional turning point, not a magical period. What remained included the organizational experience from the US Aid Committee to the IECD, enterprise financing and foreign exchange governance, the administrative network of rural hygiene and social relief, and the institutional imagination cultivated by science education plans. Some of these later became Taiwan's own policy capabilities, while others retained the Cold War-era preferences for security, development, and order. [^2] [^5] [^9]

📝 Curator's Note: **The endpoint of US aid was not "never needing others again," but Taiwan beginning to have to answer: after external resources withdrew, which capabilities had truly become our own?**

Looking back at Yin Zhongrong, his story is not suitable for being placed into a binary of "US aid created Taiwan" or "Taiwan was merely shaped by the US." Yin Zhongrong and his contemporaries—bureaucrats, entrepreneurs, teachers, rural workers, and welfare organizations—made real choices under unequal international conditions, and bore the errors and costs. US aid gave Taiwan resources to use, but did not write a single future for Taiwan.

What the sack of flour finally left behind may not be the day it was eaten, but the cards, meetings, records, controversies, and rules people established to distribute it. That system once belonged to the Cold War, and was once used by Taiwan to handle its own scarcity. After 1965, it remained in Taiwan's factories, schools, rural areas, and administrative institutions under different names.
