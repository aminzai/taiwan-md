# US Aid: How a Bag of Flour Became an Administrative System

In 1951, U.S. economic aid to Taiwan was relaunched. This period is generally referred to as the "U.S. Aid Period," extending until 1965.[^1] In 1954, President Eisenhower signed Law 480, which allowed surplus agricultural products to enter the relief network in Taiwan through government and private organizations.[^1] However, in a policy report in 1956, the U.S. Department of State acknowledged that although U.S. aid had brought economic progress, Taiwan was not yet capable of self-support.[^2]

When these three time points are viewed together, the term "U.S. Aid" ceases to sound like a simple donation sent from Washington. It resembles a translation machine: the United States delivered security interests, surplus materials, capital, and technology into Cold War Asia. Taiwan’s bureaucrats, enterprises, schools, rural communities, and welfare groups then translated this into factories, roads, powdered milk rationing, health education, and foreign exchange regulations.

> **30-Second Overview:** Following the outbreak of the Korean War in 1950, the United States incorporated Taiwan into the Asia-Pacific security order to prevent the conflict from spreading southward. U.S. aid from 1951 to 1965 operated against the backdrop of military security, involving capital, technology, trade, education, public health, and social relief. It supported the finance and security of the government after relocating to Taiwan, and it forced Taiwan's policy institutions to learn how to allocate scarce resources. The conditions, oversight, and Cold War objectives of the aid were also written into this developmental history.

## The Korean War Pushed Taiwan onto America’s Asian Frontline

On June 25, 1950, North Korea invaded South Korea across the 38th parallel. The U.S. Department of State Historian Office noted that before the war, American officials had originally planned for the Communist forces of China to cross the sea and defeat Chiang Kai-shek. The Korean War changed this assessment. The United States sent the Seventh Fleet into the Taiwan Strait, not with the immediate goal of making Taiwan a complete ally, but first to prevent the Korean War from spreading southward. Forces prepared to attack Taiwan were redirected to the Korean Peninsula, thus delaying military conflict in the Taiwan Strait.[^10]

Truman’s statement on June 27, 1950, described this turning point very directly:

> “Accordingly I have ordered the 7th Fleet to prevent any attack on Formosa.”
>
> ——Harry S. Truman, June 27, 1950[^11]

This order contained two directions. The U.S. Navy had to prevent either side from attacking the mainland using Taiwan as a base, and it also had to prevent the Communist forces of China from extending their civil war into Taiwan. Therefore, Truman’s statement required the Taiwanese side to halt air and sea operations against the mainland, leaving Taiwan's future political status for peace arrangements with Japan or consideration by the United Nations. This was not a treaty permanently incorporating Taiwan into U.S. defense scope, but rather a temporary military arrangement made during the Korean War crisis to control the front lines.[^11]

The temporary arrangement quickly became a long-term structure. In 1951, while the Korean War was ongoing, U.S. economic aid to Taiwan was relaunched. After the armistice in 1953, the Seventh Fleet did not allow the Taiwan Strait to return to its state at the beginning of 1950. In 1954, as tensions rose around Kinmen, Matsu, and Great Penghu, the People's Republic of China began shelling the outlying islands. The internal debate in the United States was not whether to see Taiwan, but within what scope to protect the government in Taiwan, how to avoid being drawn into the Chinese civil war, and whether the outlying islands fell under the defense commitment of the United States. In 1955, the U.S. Congress passed the "Formosa Resolution," authorizing the President to defend Taiwan and the outlying islands.[^10]

Thus, the Korean War background cannot be relegated only to the first paragraph and then disappear. It explains why economic aid appeared alongside military security, and it explains why the administrative system of U.S. aid needed to handle military supplies, food, industrial raw materials, and social stability. For the United States, Taiwan could not lose its defense capability due to economic collapse. For the Taiwanese government, the aid was not just a patch; it was an opportunity to translate military priority into industrial and fiscal policy.[^2] [^12]

This translation required time. Before 1949, Taiwan already faced administrative predicaments left by post-war reception, inflation, and material shortages. The Korean War pushed the situation in another direction: the government had to simultaneously maintain the military, restore production, supply urban life, and ensure that rural areas did not lose productivity due to conscription and prices. Aid was thus broken down into many different tasks. Plans for raw materials and equipment entering the Industrial Commission, food and surplus agricultural products entering relief group warehouses, and technical cooperation entering ministries, schools, and rural health work. These tasks were not necessarily executed by the same unit, but they all gained priority under the security assessment that "Taiwan must maintain stability."[^4] [^5]

It is also for this reason that the beneficiaries of U.S. aid are difficult to summarize as a single group. The military saw defense capability. Industrial bureaucrats saw foreign exchange, equipment, and loans. Rural workers saw fertilizer, hygiene, and disaster relief. Urban families remembered powdered milk, flour, and a ration card. Only by placing these experiences back into the Korean War context can we understand why U.S. aid was simultaneously military, economic, and social, rather than mistaking one aspect for the whole.

The gap of two days is small, but the gap in policy is large. The entry of the Seventh Fleet into the Taiwan Strait transformed Taiwan from a retreat point in the Chinese Civil War into a security node on America's West Pacific front. The U.S. Department of State’s brief history of the Korean War also pointed out that the war made the strengthening containment policy advocated by NSC-68 the foreign policy foundation for the United States.[^12] Consequently, later economic aid is difficult to understand separately from military security. Taiwan needed capital, food, and technology. The United States needed a Taiwan capable of maintaining defense, preventing regime collapse, yet not relying entirely on endless aid. This link between security and development was the most crucial background left by the Korean War for the U.S. Aid Period. It first determined under what conditions the aid would arrive, and it also determined who could use it first.

## The Problem Before Yin Chung-rong: Where Should the Money Flow First?

Next to this translation machine, we can look at Yin Chung-rong (尹仲容). Official records from the Ministry of Finance list him as a convener of the Industrial Commission and Minister of Economic Stability in the Executive Yuan, as well as an executive committee member of the Executive Yuan U.S. Aid Utilization Committee. The problem he dealt with was not "whether to accept U.S. aid," but how raw materials, equipment, financing, foreign exchange, and enterprises connected.[^3]

Official records describe Yin Chung-rong utilizing U.S. aid resources in the Industrial Commission to develop chemicals, food, transportation, communications, and general industry, while also assisting domestic enterprises in applying for loans. This list clearly illustrates the nature of U.S. aid: it was not just about putting finished goods into warehouses, but attempting to place imported raw materials, domestic production, and future investment on one policy map.[^3]

📝 Curator's Note: **The real difficulty with U.S. aid was not getting resources to Taiwan, but deciding which industry would receive them, who would manage them, and when the market should take over.**

Yin Chung-rong also left another warning. He advocated for civilian industries and opposed developing nations blindly pursuing heavy industry, stating: "In the process of economic development, underdeveloped countries often place too much emphasis on the results of heavy industry, leading to imbalances and disconnections in the economy, inflation, and the decline of people's living standards—a failure that is ultimately unprofitable."[^3]

The focus of this statement is not to crown Yin Chung-rong a "visionary," but to reveal the choices available to the recipient government. External resources could change what was possible, but they could not dictate the sequence of every policy for the local government. The history left by U.S. aid also includes how Taiwanese bureaucrats made compromises between security pressure, material shortages, and industrial expectations.

## The U.S. Aid Bureaucracy: Turning Aid into Administrative Language

The reason U.S. aid permeated daily life was due to institutions. After the U.S. Congress passed the Foreign Assistance Act in 1948, the government established the Executive Yuan U.S. Aid Utilization Committee, which was responsible for receiving, managing, and distributing materials. Following the outbreak of the Korean War, the United States relaunched economic and technical aid to the Republic of China.[^4]

A special exhibition at the Institute of Modern History, Academia Sinica, extended this line even further: in the early period of U.S. aid to Taiwan, funding and materials were jointly managed by the American side and the U.S. Aid Committee; after the 1960s, the U.S. Aid Committee was reorganized into the International Economic Cooperation Development Committee. Another important organization, the National Food and Agriculture Association, participated in rural health education.[^5] Here, aid was not just an account; it was a meeting, a department, a progress report, and an administrative process that required cooperation from local institutions.

📝 Curator's Note: **When an aid program truly leaves a mark, it is often not when it is spent, but when it becomes the working method of a certain institution.**

This photo of a station platform, likely taken around 1950, cannot prove that every person standing in the frame was related to U.S. aid. It reminds us that while aid was relaunched, Taiwan was also mobilizing manpower, organizing the military, and rearranging family life. Post-war economic history is not just about factories and ledgers; it also includes the lived experience of people waiting for the next order on a platform.

Official archives supporting education websites summarize the use of U.S. aid in Taiwan's economy across three levels: technology, capital, and trade.[^4] This classification does not mean that all projects neatly fit into three drawers. It is more like a reading map: funds could buy equipment, equipment required technology, and technology needed trade and foreign exchange to continue.

This also explains why U.S. aid frequently appears alongside land reform, agricultural production, and later industrialization in post-war economic history. Land reform changed the ownership and production incentives of rural areas, with some landlords compensating and moving into industry. U.S. aid provided another set of capital, technology, and policy tools.[^6] The two interacted, but they were not the same thing; nor can Taiwan's later growth be reduced to a single cause.

## A Bag of Flour’s Second Chapter

Law 480 best illustrates the distance between aid and local life. The "Development Agriculture Trade and Assistance Act," passed in 1954, dealt with U.S. surplus agricultural products on one hand, and sought overseas markets for American agricultural products on the other. Strictly speaking, these materials were not part of the Taiwan-U.S. aid program, but they were often listed in statistics related to economic assistance. Research by Chao Chin-yang estimated that the amount allocated by Law 480 accounted for about 24% of Taiwan's total U.S. aid.[^1]

After flour, powdered milk, butter, and cheese arrived in Taiwan, they did not automatically become "relief." The materials were first delivered to the committee according to the distribution plan of welfare groups, then coordinated by the Ministry of the Interior, local governments, churches, and distribution centers. A 1955 survey found that only 7 out of 18 surveyed locations maintained complete distribution records. Research also recorded that some materials were resold or exchanged because they did not suit local dietary habits, or even used to feed pigs.[^7]

These frictions were not minor details. They showed that between "aid arrival" and "effective aid," there existed institutional design, local knowledge, and power relations. To central agencies, a ration card was a manageable list of names. To the recipients, it might be a bag of unfamiliar food. To local groups, it was simultaneously relief work, an administrative burden, and a responsibility to be monitored.

📝 Curator's Note: **The object that best illustrates the system is often not grand construction, but a can of powdered milk that a local family doesn't know how to use.**

Research also raised sharper questions: The U.S. Aid Committee and the U.S. side required records, packaging recovery, and prohibited resale, yet local distributors may not have considered these rules suitable for the on-the-ground needs. In 1961, Christian welfare groups shifted from simple relief to nurturing and professional social welfare. A paper cited a 1962 _New York Times_ report stating that household material programs gradually ceased due to unfair rationing, black markets, and oversight issues, although charities, hospitals, schools, and disaster relief continued.[^7]

Therefore, Law 480 is not a straight story of "America sends food, Taiwan receives it." It is a net cast by U.S. agencies, Taiwanese administrative departments, churches, local governments, fishing associations, rural communities, and recipient families. The tighter the net, the more likely relief was to reach those in need, and the easier it was to expose who went unseen.

## Whose Security Did This Aid Serve?

Another aspect of U.S. aid was clearly stated in a 1956 policy report from the U.S. Department of State. The report indicated that the government in Taiwan maintaining military strength sufficient to defend Taiwan and Penghu exceeded Taiwan's economic capacity. Although Taiwan had made economic progress under U.S. aid, "self-support is not in sight." The report then put a question on the table: should future U.S. economic policy and aid also be used to strengthen Taiwan's economy, making it part of a political and military objective?[^2]

This document made the distance between "aid" and "charity" visible. America’s interests included containing Communist forces, maintaining the military position in the Taiwan Strait, and stabilizing an important Cold War ally. Taiwan needed food, capital, equipment, technology, and foreign exchange. The goals of both sides could intersect, but they were not identical.

English studies also placed the aid of the 1950s within the context of post-Korean War Asia-Pacific containment and regional security, analyzing how aid entered different economic sectors, influenced Taiwan's foreign trade, and how Taiwan moved from a recipient status toward self-reliant growth.[^8] This international perspective reminds us: Taiwan's post-war development cannot be described solely as "America helping Taiwan," nor can it be summarized only as "America exploiting Taiwan." What truly happened was the entanglement of security interests, local policy, and social life.

## After 1959, Aid Began to Cultivate "The Yet-Not-Yet"

The long-term effects of aid were also seen in places that did not immediately become factories. Research by Fu Li-yu on middle school science education during the U.S. Aid Period pointed out that although U.S. aid ran from 1951 to 1965, the middle school science education program was only introduced in 1959. This program emerged from the intersection of American defense science assistance, the shortage of scientific talent, long-term scientific goals by scholars like Hu Shi (胡適), and Taiwan's existing educational system.[^9]

This time lag is important: U.S. aid was not solely focused on output that could be immediately seen upon arrival in Taiwan. When education and science programs entered the aid framework, debates followed, including how American scientific education interacted with local school culture, whether the emphasis on university entrance exams would change program goals, and who had greater legitimacy between "defense needs" and "long-term academic development."[^9]

📝 Curator's Note: **Some achievements of aid are not factories that can be cut a ribbon on today, but people whose cultivation is only realized years later.**

This also forces us to re-examine the common summary, "Taiwan effectively utilized U.S. aid." It captures some truth: Taiwan did transform external resources into industrial capacity, administrative capability, and educational investment. But "effective utilization" was not a frictionless virtue; it was a series of concrete institutional choices. Who received loans, who distributed materials, and which science was prioritized—all influenced what Taiwan would become later.

## After 1965, What Remained?

Research estimated that the total amount of U.S. aid approved between 1951 and 1965 was $1.4433 billion, averaging about $100 million per year, which accounted for approximately 5%–10% of the GNP at the time.[^1] This was a resource capable of changing policy options, but the number itself cannot answer "why Taiwan later managed to grow." It only tells us the extent to which Taiwan placed development on the timeline of external resources.

The end of general economic aid in 1965 can be seen as an institutional turning point, not a magic period mark. What remained included the organizational experience from the U.S. Aid Committee to the International Economic Cooperation Development Committee, corporate financing and foreign exchange management, the administrative network of rural health and social relief, and the institutional imagination cultivated by science education programs. Some later became Taiwan's own policy capabilities; others retained Cold War preferences for security, development, and order.[^2] [^5] [^9]

📝 Curator's Note: **The end point of U.S. aid was not "no longer needing anyone," but rather when Taiwan had to begin answering: which capacities truly became its own after the withdrawal of external resources?**

Looking back at Yin Chung-rong, his story cannot be placed in a binary choice between "U.S. Aid created Taiwan" or "Taiwan was merely shaped by America." Yin Chung-rong and his contemporaries—bureaucrats, entrepreneurs, teachers, rural workers, and welfare groups—made real choices under unequal international conditions, bearing mistakes and costs. U.S. aid gave Taiwan resources to use, but it did not write a single future for Taiwan.

What the bag of flour ultimately left behind may not be the day it was eaten, but the cards, meetings, records, disputes, and rules that people established in order to distribute it. That system once belonged to the Cold War, and it was also used by Taiwan to manage its own scarcity. After 1965, it remained in Taiwanese factories, schools, rural areas, and administrative institutions under different names.
