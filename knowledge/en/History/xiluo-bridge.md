> **30-Second Overview:** The Xiluo Bridge was not built in one go. After construction began in 1937 and 32 piers were completed in 1941, the superstructure work stalled due to war. In 1952, American aid steel arrived in Taiwan, and Taiwanese engineers along with foreign technicians finished the bridge in seven months, opening in January of the following year. It connected Chiashui Township (in Changhua) and Xiluo (in Yunlin), layering piers from the Japanese colonial era, post-war trusses, the Taiwan Sugar Railway, and local petitions into a piece of history that one can walk across.[^1] [^2] [^3]

In 1937, the bridge on the lower Chiashui River had piers, but there was no road to walk on yet. After the completion of 32 concrete piers in 1941, the Pacific War diverted the steel that was meant for the structure for military use. The bridge was truly completed on December 25, 1952, and the opening ceremony was held on January 28, 1953.[^1] [^2]

What is most memorable about the Xiluo Bridge is this time gap. The blueprints first left the piers; war, steel shortages, local petitions, and Cold War transport demands subsequently rewrote the engineering, resulting in a complete truss structure after fifteen years. The piers belong to one era; the trusses belong to another; both were finally connected on the Chiashui River into a single road.[^1] [^2]

## The Chiashui River Separated Two Nearby Places

Xiluo is in Yunlin, and Chiashui is in Changhua. They were separated only by the lower reaches of the Chiashui River, yet at one point, crossing was not possible. Records from the National Archives state that traveling from Taichung to Dounan required a detour through Ji-ji, Zhushan, and Douliu, covering 99 kilometers. If a bridge had crossed the Chiashui River, the route could have been shortened to 58 kilometers. During the rainy season, Xiluo to Chiashui sometimes required a detour of about 80 kilometers.[^1]

This was not merely an issue of roads; it was also a matter of the time cost in local life. When the river water was low, people could wade across or use a makeshift bridge. When the rain came, the markets, schools, relatives, and goods on both banks were separated by a wide waterway. Xiluo and Chiashui looked close, but transportation required a long detour—this is the background for why local residents continuously demanded a bridge.[^1] [^3]

In 1936, the "Chiashui River Pedestrian Construction Alliance" was established locally, turning the desire to cross into an engineering proposal that could be submitted to the government. The Taiwan Governor-General's Office decided to undertake the Chiashui Bridge project the following year. After the residents on both banks framed "there should be a bridge" as a public issue, the project gained momentum. The Governor-General's decision and the engineers' design followed this local demand.[^1] [^3] [^6]

> **Curator's Note:** Before the first steel beam of a bridge appeared, the locals had already built it into their public life through petitions, meetings, and waiting.

## 32 Piers Stuck in War

The construction began in 1937, with piers completed around 1940 or 1941. There are slight differences in official and historical records regarding the year, but they all point to the same structural fact: the piers were finished first; the superstructure was added later. Cultural Heritage registration lists 1938 as the formal start year and notes a halt around 1941 due to the Pacific War.[^1] [^2] [^3]

The piers were not merely unfinished parts. They are the most concrete temporal markers of this bridge today. The substructure is mainly composed of truss-type reinforced concrete piers completed during the Japanese colonial era, while the superstructure is a Warren truss made of steel from the early post-war period. What people see when they stand on the bridge is a red steel structure; what they walk over are the joints between engineering left by two different political eras.[^2]

The war diverted the structural steel away from the Chiashui River, sending it toward military use. After Japan's surrender in 1945, the Taiwan Provincial Administration and local residents attempted to reacquire steel, even contacting Anshan Steel Mill, Japan, Britain, and the United States. The instability of social conditions and materials caused by the February 28 Incident and the Sino-Japanese War meant that construction did not truly resume on the bridge.[^1]

This stagnation was not just a "construction delay." It left a batch of completed piers exposed to the river, time, and political change, forcing local residents to repeatedly explain to new government agencies why the same crossing problem had not disappeared. In 1948, Liao Chung-kuan (廖重光) and Li Ying-chou (李應鏜) established the Xiluo Bridge Resumption Committee, turning local waiting into action.[^6]

## American Aid Steel Arrived After the Korean War

At the end of 1949, the Central Government relocated to Taiwan, and representatives from Changhua and Yunlin petitioned the Provincial Assembly of Taiwan again. In 1950, Li Wan-ju (李萬居) suggested securing American aid to complete the bridge. The language of the project expanded from "local transportation" to include "wartime transport": if north-south transport relied only on the main railway line, there was no other supporting route during wartime.[^1]

Before the arrival of American aid steel, the Xiluo Bridge had already waited for a period in ports and documents. The United States had originally promised to provide steel, but after the Korean War broke out in June 1950, steel exports to Taiwan were temporarily suspended due to battlefield demands. This is another counterintuitive aspect of the Xiluo Bridge: it was delayed by the Cold War situation, yet it became more valued for Taiwan's transportation and military position because of the Cold War, ultimately securing the conditions for resumption.[^1] [^2]

Two batches of steel arrived in Taiwan in April and November 1952. The construction was undertaken by Taiwan Machinery Company, Heavy Machinery Plant, and Taiwan Industrial Company, with assistance from technicians from the United States, Japan, and the Philippines at the construction site. Piling, scaffolding, girder erection, riveting, painting, and deck paving were compressed into a single construction season.[^1]

Girder erection required precise handling of weather, earthquakes, tools, and construction conditions. Records from the National Archives state that the bridge was completed ahead of schedule on December 25, 1952, meaning approximately seven months from resumption to completion. This number should not be glossed over as a mere "miracle." It is closer to a temporary international engineering team that caught up fifteen years of stagnation once materials finally arrived.[^1]

## A Bridge Carrying Both Cars and Five-Ton Trucks

The Xiluo Bridge is approximately 1,939 meters long with a deck width of about 7.3 meters, utilizing a Warren truss structure made of steel framing and concrete piers. The Cultural Heritage website lists it as a historical building of the bridge type and records its registration on October 31, 2001.[^2] [^3]

The deck was not only for cars. Small trains from Taiwan Sugar Railway also ran on the bridge; the sight of trains and cars running parallel placed the sugarcane, materials, people, and vehicles of both banks of the Chiashui River into a single transportation cross-section. Data from Yunlin County Government indicates that the railway tracks were later removed due to accidents caused by the reduced importance of sugar and iron transport, leaving only the road section today.[^3]

This change meant that "transportation construction" was no longer just a story about faster speed. When the Taiwan Sugar Railway was still operating, the bridge was a tool for industrial transport. When the tracks were removed, the bridge left behind a trace of an industrial era receding into history. Today, readers driving over the red truss cannot see the five-ton trucks, but they can imagine how it once shared the same narrow road surface with the highway based on the scale of the structure.[^3]

The opening ceremony on January 28, 1953, finally provided a public moment for this long wait. Photos, documents, and commemorative postmarks from the National Archives record crowds flooding into Xiluo Township, with congratulatory slogans like "Big Bridge is Open, North and South Are One" appearing on streets and walls. This phrase was not a beautiful metaphor added later; it appeared on the local streets and walls on the day of the opening, recording how residents understood this bridge.[^1]

> **Curator's Note:** The bridge once carried cars and trains, and also carried the collective sigh of relief from residents on both banks who could "finally cross directly."

## How a Bridge Became a Road in Seven Months

The resumption of the Xiluo Bridge was not complete just by delivering steel to the riverbank. Engineering narratives preserved by the National Archives break down construction into several interconnected stages: the piers must first be able to support the superstructure; the steel girders must be lifted when river conditions allow; riveting and joint work must be completed on-site; and finally, a deck capable of supporting vehicular traffic must be paved. These tasks cannot be summarized merely as "fast," because any delay in one stage would push the seven-month construction season back to another wait.[^1]

The bridge's span also made it a visible engineering scale. The Cultural Heritage website records a total length of 1,939.03 meters, and the Yunlin County Government records a deck width of about 7.3 meters. This is not a dike that fills both banks; rather, it is a road repeatedly passing through trusses, with weight transferred to the riverbed by the piers. The roughly 31 truss openings seen by readers correspond to an engineering method that divides long distances into multiple structural units.[^2] [^3] [^6]

The deck at the time also had to contend with the natural conditions of the Chiashui River. The waterway was wide and erosive, so pier locations could not be determined solely by extending the lines of the roads on both banks. The project had to find a workable node between transportation needs and riverine environment, which is why the bridge's history cannot just be written as "American aid construction." American aid provided the critical steel; local petitions provided continuous political pressure; and the engineering team turned materials into a structure capable of supporting daily traffic.[^1] [^2]

## From Shortest Path to Longest Memory

The Xiluo Bridge was once called Taiwan's first long bridge in the Far East. Cultural Heritage registration includes "the former longest bridge in the Far East and the second longest in the world" as part of its historical value, while Transportation History describes it as the longest bridge in East Asia at its 1953 commissioning. The ranking contexts from different sources are not entirely identical; therefore, a more stable description is that upon opening, it was an engineering project attracting attention in East Asian and Far Eastern bridges approaching two kilometers.[^2] [^6]

Later, the Zhongsha Bridge and other transportation routes shared its original transport functions. The Cultural Heritage website records that the Xiluo Bridge remains open to traffic but is no longer the sole north-south artery. After the change in its transportation role, the bridge's value did not automatically become purely scenic. It still faces practical issues such as deck maintenance, traffic safety, steel structure anti-rusting, and cross-strait management coordination.[^2] [^3]

A 2025 report by Lianhe News showed that local cultural and historical groups wished to upgrade it to a historic monument, while the Changhua County Cultural Heritage Review Committee decided to maintain its status as a historical building, citing the existence of similar bridges, the uniqueness of the structural form, and the criteria for designating monuments. This decision does not mean the Xiluo Bridge lacks historical value; rather, it means different cultural heritage statuses have different proof thresholds. The preservation of the bridge thus requires simultaneously addressing engineering judgment, historical narrative, and local expectations.[^7]

This debate should not be simplified into "locals wanting tourism" versus "experts not valuing history." The status of a cultural heritage item answers another question: what system should be used to preserve a bridge that is still in use, whose structure needs maintenance, and whose value comes from engineering, transportation, industry, and local memory? The present of the Xiluo Bridge still requires people to make concrete choices between traffic safety and historical preservation.[^2] [^7]

An article in _Taiwan Guanghua_ wrote the Xiluo Bridge as the starting point for Xiluo residents' dreams of moving north, recording how the red steel structure, the Chiashui Riverbed, and local cultural activities re-formed the way it was viewed. The bridge's transportation function can be replaced, but its function as a local landmark has not disappeared.[^4]

At night, the red truss is no longer just an engineering structure; it becomes a scenic spot for walking, drinking coffee, and watching the sunset. Another article in _Taiwan Guanghua_ pointed out that later bridges handled most of the traffic, but the Xiluo Bridge remained in the local view due to its nighttime lighting. The night scenery of an old bridge should not be romanticized. After infrastructure steps back from the forefront, it can still become a vessel for public memory.[^5]

## Piers Did Not Conclude History

The story of the Xiluo Bridge was not completed when it opened in 1953, nor finalized when it was registered as a historical building in 2001. It was first an engineering project demanded by local residents to shorten travel; then it was a facility where post-war governments sought American aid and maintained transport; then it became part of the Taiwan Sugar Railway line; and finally, it became a cultural heritage item requiring cross-county coordination. No layer completely erased the one before it.[^1] [^2] [^3]

If it were only called "Taiwan's first long bridge in the Far East," readers would remember the ranking but not know why the superstructure has two eras. If it were only called a "historical building," readers would know it should be preserved, but might not understand why people on both banks were willing to wait fifteen years. If it were only photographed as a red landscape at sunset, the five-ton trucks and the construction site would disappear from the frame.

A reading closer to the Xiluo Bridge is to see it as a timeline that has been repeatedly connected. The piers started in 1937 were not replaced by the steel girders of 1952; the Taiwan Sugar Railway tracks did not vanish upon removal; and local petitions did not lose meaning after the opening ceremony. They all remain on today's bridge, just seen in different ways.[^1] [^2] [^3]

Standing on the bridge, the Chiashui River still divides Changhua and Yunlin into two banks. Yet, the bridge turned this division into a distance that one can walk across. The most enduring quality of the Xiluo Bridge is not how long it once was. It records in its history both "how to cross the river" and how a group of people managed to keep the act of crossing within public life when war, materials, and systems were beyond their control.
