> **30-second overview:** The controversy surrounding the Xincheng Mountain mine in Shoufeng Township, Hualien County, appears on the surface to be a single administrative document extending mining rights, but beneath it lies three sets of systems operating on different timelines: the mine has been in operation since 1957, the Indigenous consultation and consent system was not formally enacted until 2005, and the EIA system once excluded "existing mines" from its scope. In 2021, the Supreme Administrative Court revoked the permit extension, ruling that the state had not allowed the affected Truku people to participate in the decision before granting another 20 years. However, under the Mining Law at the time, mining rights remained valid during the extension review period. Thus, the ruling nullified one approval but did not press the stop button for residents below the mountain. [^1] [^2]

## The People Below the Mountain See the Changes First

On February 3, 2026, Asia Cement held a public briefing in the Fushih Community Center in Shoufeng Township, Hualien County, regarding the supplementary EIA for the Xincheng Mountain mine. The meeting discussed air quality, noise, vibration, surface water, and slope monitoring, but those seated in the audience redirected the conversation to the roads they take home every day: a rockfall incident in June 2025 that sent debris flying into residential areas, and the ongoing risks of collapse and falling rocks following the April 3, 2024 Hualien earthquake that have yet to disappear from residents' daily lives. [^3]

Shoufeng Township Mayor Wang Meihsia said: "We really don't want our Fushih Village to become Xilinyuan Village." This statement is not a data point in any EIA report, yet it explains why residents are not only asking the company to prove that current operations meet standards, but also demanding that the government answer who will be responsible for clarifying risks when the next strong earthquake, typhoon, or heavy rain hits. [^3]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** The hardest impacts of a mine to measure are often not how many meters the mountainside has lost, but whether residents can still treat "safety" as an everyday reality rather than a promise waiting to be verified by an accident.

The aerial photo pulls the controversy into wider focus: the mining area is not an isolated white cut, but a landscape adjacent to valleys, roads, settlements, and tribal life. This is also why "mining rights" cannot be seen as merely an industrial permit. They simultaneously determine which landscapes will be altered and which people must bear the long-term risks of those changes. [^2] [^3]

The "Asia Cement case" is not a single incident. It layers mining rights, Indigenous land, cultural heritage, geological safety, environmental impact assessment, and local employment onto one mountain in Shoufeng Township, Hualien County. Different stakeholders all acknowledge that the Xincheng Mountain mine has existed for many years; the real dispute is whether the existing facts of extraction can automatically become the legitimacy of the next 20 years. [^1] [^4]

## Protests Bring the Mountain's Problems to the Executive Yuan

On June 25, 2017, Truku people and environmental groups launched the "See Asia Cement, Save Truku" march. The Environmental Information Center reported that the procession was led by Truku people from Fushih Village in Hualien Xincheng, marching from the Executive Yuan to Crescent Road, with over 8,000 participants on site. The demands raised during the march included revoking Asia Cement's Xincheng Mountain mining rights extension, amending the Mining Law, implementing Article 21 of the Indigenous Peoples' Basic Law, re-determining mining land use, establishing a mechanism for resident participation, and making the mining development review data public. [^13]

_Extended reading: [The "Mining Reform, Revoke Asia Cement" protest march hits the streets.](https://e-info.org.tw/images/eaeea256-99cb-4561-ad46-e99988e7093f-w1200.webP)_

_Protest photo: Environmental Information Center news page, caption indicates the June 25, 2017 march, photographed by Lai Pin-yu; confirm usage rights according to the original page and rights holder before official release._

In this photo, the slogans compress the legal points into a few shouts: "Mining Reform," "Revoke Asia Cement." But the protest was not simply an emotional outburst. What the participants demanded was that the permit extension no longer be completed solely within administrative procedures between the supervising authority and the company, but that tribal residents, households below the mine, and environmental risks be brought back to the decision-making scene. [^13]

This joint appearance on stage made the Asia Cement case more than just a Hualien case. Residents from Hsinchu Guansi also appeared in the march, because they too face conflicts between mining development and local life. Residents from different regions brought their own experiences to the same protest, forming a larger question: when mining rights, EIA, and local governance are scattered across different systems, who is responsible for integrating these impacts? [^13]

The protest also raised another demand for "professional review": residents are not rejecting all data, but demanding that data not flow only between the government and the company. The scope of the mine, geological sensitive areas, land rights, extraction plans, and safety measures should all be understandable and answerable to those affected, and alternative proposals should be submitted before decisions are made. This forms a clear institutional connection with the prior consultation and consent emphasized later by the Supreme Administrative Court. [^1] [^13]

## From Mining Rights in 1957 to 2017

According to the official press release of the Supreme Administrative Court, Asia Cement acquired the Xincheng Mountain marble mining rights from Eastern Industrial in 1973. The original mining rights period was from November 23, 1957 to November 22, 1977, followed by two 20-year extensions in 1977 and 1997, with the second extension expiring on November 22, 2017. [^1]

The importance of this timeline lies in the fact that "existing mines" does not mean there were no systems in place. After the Environmental Impact Assessment Law was enacted in 1994, whether existing mines needed to undergo EIA during subsequent extensions was long handled according to the then-current laws and administrative interpretations. After the Indigenous Peoples' Basic Law was promulgated in 2005, Article 21 established consultation and consent participation requirements for developments involving Indigenous lands and natural resources. [^4] [^5]

On March 14, 2017, the Ministry of Economic Affairs approved Asia Cement's Xincheng Mountain mining rights extension, valid until November 22, 2037. Environmental groups and tribal representatives questioned the application, which was submitted on November 25, 2016 and approved in about three and a half months, coinciding with the heated discussions on amending the Mining Law. Asia Cement argued that the application was submitted in accordance with the law and that relevant extraction, water conservation, and safety data had already been provided. [^1] [^6] [^7]

The 2017 investigation by the Control Yuan did not simplify the controversy into "support or oppose mining." It pointed out that the Ministry of Economic Affairs, during the review of the extension, already knew that three potential debris flow streams passed through the mining area, but did not require a thorough investigation of the potential impacts on debris flows, landslides, exposed surfaces, and public safety over 20 years of extraction. It also did not complete the verification that the Control Yuan deemed necessary for geological sensitive areas. [^2]

The same Control Yuan statement also pointed out that the Xincheng Mountain mining area includes the Fushih Historic Site designated by Hualien County, and the Ministry of Economic Affairs used Asia Cement's promise not to mine toward the historic site as the basis for handling, but the Control Yuan believed that such promises leave long-term protection uncertain. This made "whether the mountain will slide" and "whether the historic site will be affected" reasons why the mining rights extension cannot be judged solely by whether the documents are complete. [^2]

## One Timeline, Three Institutional Shifts

| Time               | What Happened                                                                                                                          | Why It Matters                                                                                                                    |
| ------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1957               | Xincheng Mountain mining rights begin to be calculated, later transferred from Eastern Industrial to Asia Cement. [^1]                 | The history of existing mines predates the EIA law and Indigenous consultation and consent system.                                |
| 1994               | The Environmental Impact Assessment Law is enacted. [^4]                                                                               | Whether "existing mines" need to undergo supplementary EIA during extensions becomes part of the later institutional controversy. |
| 2005               | The Indigenous Peoples' Basic Law is promulgated, and Article 21 establishes consultation and consent participation requirements. [^5] | The state's procedural obligations regarding Indigenous lands and natural resource development are codified.                      |
| March 14, 2017     | The Ministry of Economic Affairs approves Asia Cement's Xincheng Mountain mining rights extension until 2037. [^1] [^6]                | The existing facts of extraction and the legal legitimacy of a new 20 years are placed on the same document.                      |
| July 2019          | Taipei High Administrative Court revokes the extension decision. [^8]                                                                  | The court first examines this extension case through the lens of Indigenous consultation and consent procedures.                  |
| September 16, 2021 | The Supreme Administrative Court rejects Asia Cement's appeal and upholds the revocation. [^1]                                         | Procedural rights become a legal issue that cannot be bypassed in mining rights extensions.                                       |
| February 3, 2026   | Public briefing on the supplementary EIA for the Xincheng Mountain mine. [^3]                                                          | The controversy extends from "whether the extension is valid" to "how future extraction will be supervised."                      |

This timeline is not just about cutting events into several dates. It shows that each system entered at different times: mining rights existed first, the EIA law came later, and Indigenous consultation and consent were codified even later. When the state understands extensions as "continuation of old rights," tribal groups and environmental organizations ask: if the next 20 years will generate new geological, cultural, and living impacts, why should it only be treated as an extension of the previous 20 years? [^1] [^2] [^4] [^5]

## The Court Asks: Who Is Protected by Law

In July 2019, the Taipei High Administrative Court revoked the Ministry of Economic Affairs' approval of the extension decision and the Executive Yuan's remonstrance decision. After Asia Cement filed an appeal, the Supreme Administrative Court rejected the appeal on September 16, 2021, in the 2020 Supreme Administrative Court Civil Division Case No. 894, upholding the revocation. [^1] [^8]

The key point of the Supreme Administrative Court's ruling is not that the court decided the mine must immediately shut down for the tribe, but that it confirmed the procedural rights themselves have legal significance. The court held that the Mining Law's extension provisions simultaneously protect the personal interests of the mining area and nearby residents. Article 21 of the Indigenous Peoples' Basic Law also has the nature of safeguarding the individual rights of specific Indigenous peoples and tribes, so residents within 500 meters of the mining area, whose identity and land rights are directly related to the mining area, have the eligibility to file administrative lawsuits. [^1]

The court then pointed out that mining is a land development activity that affects Indigenous groups and traditional culture. Before the state makes a decision on extending mining rights, it should first complete the consultation and consent procedures. Compensating with employment, feedback, or soil and water conservation plans after the extension is approved cannot replace the opportunity for the tribe to influence the decision beforehand. [^1]

There is an easily overlooked counterintuitive point here: the Truku people won the lawsuit to revoke the extension, but this does not mean that mining stopped the day the ruling was made. The court's press release explains that mining rights are calculated in 20-year periods, and during the extension review period before the supervising authority makes a decision, mining rights are still considered valid under the Mining Law. The ruling changed the procedures that the state must follow when approving the next period of extraction, not all the legal relationships of the existing mine. [^1]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** What this lawsuit truly revoked was the assumption that "since we've been digging for so long, we can keep digging"; what remains is the responsibility that every next step must be explained anew.

## Two Safety Narratives on the Same Mountain

The Control Yuan's argument focuses on whether the government's review was sufficient: potential debris flow streams, geological sensitive areas, the Fushih Historic Site, and the safety of nearby tribes should all be included in the judgment before the extension decision. [^2] Asia Cement's public Q&A instead argues that data on potential debris flow streams is mainly used for disaster prevention warnings, and that the mine operates according to the soil and water conservation plan, with slope monitoring, rockfall barriers, and siltation facilities in place. The company also stated that the area overlapping the Fushih Historic Site is not included in the mining plan. [^7]

These two narratives do not necessarily negate each other. The company can present monitoring data, and the government can require more complete pre-investigation. Residents still have the right to question whether the data coverage includes their own homes, tribes, and extreme weather conditions. At the 2026 briefing, residents questioned that the presentation map did not mark the ten neighborhoods of Fushih Village, and requested that geological weak zones, fault fracture zones, and post-earthquake scenarios be included in the assessment. [^3]

The supplementary EIA is therefore not just filling in a formal chapter for the 2017 extension controversy, but transforming "how we mined in the past" into "under what conditions we can continue to mine in the future." After the 2023 amendment to the Mining Law, existing mines approved before October 20, 1995, that have never undergone EIA and have a mining land area exceeding 2 hectares must conduct supplementary EIA in accordance with Article 76. Xincheng Mountain Mine therefore enters this procedure. [^3] [^9]

## The EIA Must Answer More Than Just "Whether Current Standards Are Exceeded"

At the 2026 briefing, the assessment scope proposed by Asia Cement and its EIA consultant focused on the currently and future planned extraction area of 35.2 hectares, with a maximum extraction height of approximately 120 meters. The company explained that the current investigation of air quality, noise and vibration, surface water quality, and traffic conditions complies with current laws and regulations, and recorded the presence of yellow-throated marten, crab-eating mongoose, Mikado duck, formosan clouded leopard, and protected species of yellow-spotted phengodes. The company also proposed dust suppression, noise reduction, water conservation, slope monitoring, and post-mining restoration measures. [^3]

These are necessary basic data in the EIA, but the questions raised by residents are closer to risk governance: if rockfall incidents have already occurred, how can the early warning system prove its effectiveness? If there are still collapses and falling rocks after the April 3 earthquake, does the assessment boundary cover the affected living space? If the mine's presentation map does not mark the ten neighborhoods where residents live, is public participation truly allowing all affected parties to see the same map? [^3]

Therefore, the supplementary EIA has at least three levels. The first level is legal compliance, confirming whether the monitoring data and development conditions meet current standards; the second level is cumulative risk, placing blasting, earthquakes, typhoons, geological sensitive areas, and existing mining traces in the same scenario; the third level is procedural credibility, allowing tribes to express their opinions when the data is formed and alternative solutions are discussed, rather than only expressing consent or opposition to the conclusion after the report is completed. The first two levels require professional investigation, and the third level involves the prior consultation and effective participation confirmed by the court. [^1] [^2] [^3]

> 📝 **Curator's note:** The EIA is not about translating the mountain into a table, but about confirming whether every number on the table truly corresponds to a place where people live at the foot of the mountain.

## Land Restoration, Work, and Closure — None Can Answer Alone

The land issue of Xincheng Mountain is not something that can be concluded with a single sentence of "legally rented." The Control Yuan pointed out that the relevant mining land use includes Indigenous reserved land, and Hualien County government has repeatedly failed to handle rental payments and land return issues due to policy changes and delays. The Control Yuan also believed that the Executive Yuan and the Ministry of Economic Affairs previously excluded mining rights extensions from Article 21 of the Indigenous Peoples' Basic Law, which effectively weakened the protection of Indigenous land and natural resource rights. [^2]

Asia Cement stated in its public explanation that early land use involved compensation, cultivated land rights cancellation, and government rental procedures, and the company denied obtaining land through deception or forgery, and claimed to have continuously provided local feedback, employment, and safety communication over the years. [^7] These claims must be placed on the same table as the tribe's land experience, and cannot be spoken for the other party by either side's documents alone.

The 2022 investigation report on the truth of the Executive Yuan's Indigenous land use pointed out that after the mining land use is completed, land restoration and disaster prevention should be carried out in accordance with the soil and water conservation plan, and recommended that the government, the tribe, and Asia Cement jointly consider the transformation and land reuse of the mine area four years before the end of mining. [^10] At the 2026 EIA briefing, the Earth Citizens Foundation estimated the existing reserves and annual extraction volume based on the report, and requested that the company propose staff arrangements, land restoration, and tribal transformation closure plans early. Asia Cement responded that the closure plan has long been part of mine management in accordance with the law, and does not mean the company is about to exit the market. [^3]

This is also the most realistic difficulty of the case: if we only talk about stopping mining, we may not see the families that depend on work and local feedback. If we only talk about industry and employment, we may push land rights, tribal safety, and cultural continuation to the future. The truly feasible answer must simultaneously explain who bears the risk, who gets the decision-making power, and how the mountain and people's lives continue after the mine closes. [^1] [^3] [^10]

## Tribal Participation Is Not a Single Vote, But a Traceable Relationship

After the 2017 extension controversy erupted, the government, the tribe, and Asia Cement held a three-party dialogue; in 2020, Asia Cement stated that it would promote consultation in the spirit of Article 21 of the Indigenous Peoples' Basic Law; in 2021, the tribe put forward conditions and Asia Cement responded; in 2022, the Boshi'an tribe held a consultation and consent vote, with the reported result being 294 votes in favor, 45 votes against, and 14 invalid votes. [^4]

This process cannot be simply condensed into "final consent" or "final opposition." There may be different living needs within the tribe: some care about land return and traditional territories, some depend on the jobs provided by the mine, and some are most concerned about the risks of blasting, falling rocks, and water disasters. Meaningful consultation should allow different opinions to obtain the same data before the vote, put forward conditions, track commitments, and know how to seek relief when commitments are not fulfilled. This is exactly why the Supreme Administrative Court emphasized prior procedures: post-feedback and compensation cannot fully replace the opportunity to influence decisions. [^1]

The 2022 investigation report on the truth of the Executive Yuan's Indigenous land use has already placed land restoration, disaster prevention, and mine transformation within the same long-term framework, recommending that the government, the tribe, and Asia Cement jointly consider land reuse and transformation four years before the end of mining. The key to this recommendation is not to pre-determine the answer for any party, but to bring up "what to do after closure" to the negotiation stage when there is still time. [^10]

## One Revocation Document Has Not Yet Settled the Lives of Those Below the Mountain

The Asia Cement Xincheng Mountain mine case went from the 2017 extension approval to the 2021 Supreme Administrative Court revocation, and then to the 2026 supplementary EIA briefing. The timeline has been stretched out, and the issues have shifted from "whether the extension is legal" to "how the next decision can be believed." The court has already made it clear that prior consultation and consent cannot be replaced by post-feedback; the Control Yuan left a gap in the government's review of geology, cultural heritage, and public safety; and residents brought rockfalls, falling rocks, and homes not marked on the map back to every meeting. [^1] [^2] [^3]

The cliffs of Xincheng Mountain are still there, and the terraced mining scars left by extraction are still visible. What has not yet been completed is not finding a slogan of "can mine" or "cannot mine" for this mountain, but returning safety, land, work, culture, and the closure timetable to those who must live at the foot of the mountain to decide together. When the government picks up that document next time, the people below the mountain should already be in the document, not notified after the document is finalized.
