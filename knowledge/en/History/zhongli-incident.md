# The Zhongli Incident: How a Altered Ballot Box Burned an Outlet for Local Democracy

> **30 Second Summary:** On November 19, 1977, Xu Shin-liang ran for Taoyuan County mayor against the KMT nominee Ou Hsien-yu. Allegations of spoiled ballots from Zhongli Elementary School spread to the adjacent Zhongli precinct in the afternoon; the crowd's siege at night resulted in a police station fire. The event involved overturning vehicles, tear gas, casualties, and arson, but also left behind a consequence harder to capture in photographs: the ballot transformed from internal party paper into public evidence that had to be displayed before everyone.

## Xu Shin-liang Left the Party Before Entering This Polling Place

Xu Shin-liang did not start by opposing the KMT. In 1977, he was a provincial councilor affiliated with the KMT, but his relationship with the party rapidly deteriorated after publicly criticizing its policies and publishing _The Sound of Wind and Rain_ (風雨之聲) and _When It Is Our Turn Not to Yield_ (當仁不讓). In October, the KMT expelled him from the party. On November 19, he ran for Taoyuan County mayor as an independent candidate against Ou Hsien-yu, who was nominated by the KMT.[^1] [^2]

Xu Shin-liang had grown within the party but left it with familiarity regarding local governance and elections. He used the ballot to ask the residents of Taoyuan to judge him, which gave the Zhongli Incident a background different from a mere street conflict. Reporter's English special reports record that during his campaign, Xu Shin-liang gathered young workers, local acquaintances, and independent figures, designing campaign songs, evening rallies, and intensive vote monitoring. These practices later became familiar scenes in Taiwanese elections, yet at the time, they were an experiment mixing campus clubs, local temple fairs, and modern campaigning.[^2]

Xu Shin-liang's campaign team organized Taoyuan as a place needing reorganization. They proposed "New Spirit, New Figures, New Taoyuan," rewriting campaign songs using Tâi-gí (Taiwanese) folk tunes, making campaigning more than just candidate photos and policy leaflets. Academic research reminds us that this election campaign cannot be understood solely through the dichotomy of independent vs. KMT. The split voting among the KMT nomination process, local Taoyuan factions, and the county/provincial councilor elections were all conditions in Xu Shin-liang's political success.[^4]

**Curator's Note:** The Zhongli Incident entered the streets through a campaign song. The song brought politics out of the conference room into the familiar sounds of residents, meaning local participation was no longer dependent only on candidate policy leaflets.

## Zhongli Elementary School: Controversy Began with One Ballot

On the morning of November 19, Taoyuan County held elections for five public offices, including county mayor, provincial councilor, Taipei city councilor, county/city councilor, and township mayor. Zhongli Elementary School was one of the polling places. Fan Jiang-xinlin (范姜新林), a poll worker at the time, was accused of spoiling ballots cast by two elderly individuals for Xu Shin-liang. Details from different sources are not entirely consistent. Some recorded traces left on the ballot with ink, while others recounted the poll worker explaining the spoiled ballots according to voting regulations. What is certain is that the controversy was not settled peacefully inside the polling place but quickly spread out of the school gate.[^5] [^6]

Zhongli Elementary School was located directly opposite the Zhongli Precinct. When investigators took the elderly individuals and witnesses to the police station, but left the accused poll worker to continue working at the polling place, what the crowd saw was no longer just "is this ballot valid?" They saw an asymmetrical process: those pointing out the problem were taken away, while the person in charge of the ballots remained on site. Considering the memory preserved by documents, describe this space as a very short but critical political route. The school gate, the road, and the police precinct—three places became connected within a few hours.[^6]

This spatial relationship also explains why the event did not stop at the polling place. If Zhongli Elementary School and the police precinct were far apart, the news might have remained among a few witnesses. Facing each other, the crowd could directly bring their suspicion of the poll work to the gate of public authority. A summary of the police case preserved by the National Archives Information Network records scenes of protesting election fraud that night, citizens overturning vehicles, using megaphones to speak, and military/police blocking crowds. The archives also hold 170 pages of investigative photos, meaning this event exists not only in later oral histories.[^7]

## From the Precinct Gate to the Entire Street

In the afternoon, the number of people gathered outside the Zhongli Precinct increased. Historical reports by the Taipei Times describe citizens overturning police cars and rushing into the precinct to damage property, while police discharged tear gas, eventually leading to the burning of the police station building. A program produced by Hakka TV for its 40th anniversary described that night as a massive siege and fire at the Zhongli Precinct, presenting local memory with two deaths and one serious injury. These narratives collectively point to a chaotic night, but regarding whether the police fired, the number of casualties, or the identity of the arsonists, no single version can be accepted as the final answer.[^5] [^8]

In 2025, the Control Yuan released an investigation into the Zhongli Incident. The investigative team reviewed 72 volumes and 8,050 pages of records and interviewed related individuals, including Xu Shin-liang and Chang Chun-hung (張俊宏). The Control Yuan pointed out that several records from agencies like the Ministry of National Defense were inconsistent with interviews and other materials, and confirmed that there was indeed ballot manipulation during the 1977 election. The investigation also noted that Jiang Wen-guo (江文國), a 19-year-old who died after suffering a penetrating head wound and whose body was quickly cremated, still has not received sufficient justice.[^9]

This report added a new reading thread to the Zhongli Incident, but it cannot force later verification results back onto 1977. The crowds that night did not know how many volumes the Control Yuan would review 48 years later. They only knew that the procedure before them did not answer the questions, and the power dynamic at the police precinct gate was harder than the polling place. Today's reader must see two things simultaneously: the procedures of that night failed to answer the crowd's questions, and the records left by the authoritarian government are contradictory.

**Curator's Note:** The most difficult aspect of the Zhongli Incident is that different agencies preserved inconsistent versions. Transitional justice must first acknowledge that archives are not naturally transparent windows; sometimes they are smoke left behind by power.

## Xu Shin-liang’s Victory Does Not Mean All Street Actions Were Justified

Xu Shin-liang ultimately defeated Ou Hsien-yu with a margin exceeding one hundred thousand votes. Reporters, the Taipei Times, and _Xiangxiang_ (想想) all viewed this victory as an undeniable outcome of the event. Xu Shin-liang did not gain his votes because the crowd burned the police station; he had already established a campaign organization and proposed local policies before the incident, and he became a familiar candidate to the people of Taoyuan through long street campaigning. The Zhongli Incident caused poll workers at polling places across the county to handle ballots with greater caution, leading a local election to have institutional consequences beyond the term of the mayor.[^2] [^5] [^6]

However, writing the event as "the people burned the police station, so democracy won" is equally dangerous. The burning of public buildings, overturning vehicles, and causing casualties cannot automatically become legitimate because of later democratic outcomes. The anger preserved in local memory and the violence that the state must investigate are not two offsetting accounts. The Zhongli Incident deserves to be remembered because it simultaneously contained the political reasons for electoral fraud, the destructive consequences of crowd action, the coercive response from the police and military, and the government's subsequent control over records and news.

The fact that domestic media did not provide a complete report the day after the incident is part of this history. The Control Yuan's article compared the gaps between international and domestic reports, noting that foreign correspondents interviewed overseas media, while major domestic newspapers only published more complete pages several days later. This information gap meant that the people of Zhongli knew what they experienced, but it was not easy for other parts of the island to know simultaneously. The event did happen, but it was fragmented into local residents' memories, international media reports, and official archive versions.[^9]

## Why It Became a Watershed Moment for Democratization

Zhongli in 1977 was not a national revolution. It occurred during the Taoyuan County mayoral election, starting at a local polling place, with participants who were not pre-assembled by a single political organization. Academic research places it within local politics, reminding us to pay attention to the factional alliances, candidate nominations, and split voting in Taoyuan. This locality expanded the participants of democratization; intellectuals in Taipei and central politicians were no longer the sole drivers.[^4]

The Taipei Times cited research indicating that the Zhongli Incident led independent figures to begin cooperating more systematically, and it reduced the fear of some intellectuals participating in politics. The independent support groups at the end of 1978, the subsequent Beilidi incident, and the street and electoral mobilization in the 1980s all show a preceding form in Zhongli. The evening rallies, megaphones, campaign songs, and leaflets used by Xu Shin-liang's team later became the basic grammar of Taiwanese elections.[^5]

The KMT government also learned another lesson from Zhongli. Central elections and political activities were postponed in 1978 due to diplomatic and political circumstances; the authoritarian system did not immediately open up because of a county mayor's victory. Zhongli was not a straight line of democratic progress; it was more like a fissure. The fissure allowed people to see the power that was previously hidden, and it gave rulers time to patch things back with new forms of control.

This is the relationship between the Zhongli Incident and later democratization experiences. It did not directly create party rotation, nor did it immediately make elections fair. First, it let local residents know that ballots could be defended, poll workers' decisions could be questioned, and the police precinct was not necessarily the last place that could speak. Once this awareness emerged, subsequent elections could no longer maintain order solely through internal nominations and closed ballot procedures.

## After One Ballot, Who Was Not Written In?

The photos left by the Zhongli Incident are powerful. Crowds stood before the police station, vehicles were overturned, glass was shattered on the ground, and megaphones sent voices across the streets. But photos do not automatically tell us who took them, who was photographed, or who did not appear in the frame. They also cannot answer how the family of Jiang Wen-guo spent years, or whether those investigated and listed in archives ever had a chance to correct official records.

In 2025, the Control Yuan suggested making the Zhongli Incident a site for education on the rule of law and transitional justice through the preservation of sites of injustice, reconstruction of historical memory, and spatial reuse. This suggestion is not just about erecting a memorial plaque in Zhongli. It requires the government to acknowledge that conflicts between state power and people's rights occurred at the police precinct, polling place, and streets, and allows later residents to understand this conflict on site.[^9]

Today's front station of Zhongli Station looks like an entrance for daily traffic. People buy tickets, wait for trains, and pass through underpasses without usually thinking that news from 1977 once spread across the station square and main roads. The buildings do not speak history unless someone connects the place, the people, and the system again. Local democracy is the continuous action of a group willing to see every ballot clearly at the polling place, question the procedures on the street, and finally demand that the government correct errors in the archives.

The Zhongli Incident cannot be reduced to a heroic poster, nor can it be left as just a burned police precinct. What it truly leaves is a difficult democratic problem: when people no longer believe ballots will be counted fairly, must the government rely on more police force to maintain order, or on more transparent procedures to regain trust? The answer in 1977 was not beautiful; it even carried fire and wounds. Taiwan's subsequent choice to gradually allow ballots to be publicly displayed, allow opponents to run, and subject archives to inquiry is precisely because Zhongli proved that order without procedure will eventually burn out on the streets.

## How a Local Event Casts a Shadow Over National Systems

The locality of the Zhongli Incident can also be seen through electoral organization. The 1977 Taoyuan County mayoral election was not an isolated contest between mayors; on the same day, there were elections for provincial councilors, county councilors, and township mayors. Candidates from different levels shared the same local organizations, kinship ties, and polling places, but voters did not necessarily cast all their votes for the same camp. These differences mean that the mayoral election cannot be simply seen as a projection of central parties onto the locality. Luo Guochu's research analyzes this split voting within local factions and nomination politics, reminding us that Xu Shin-liang's victory cannot merely be described as the central regime suddenly being defeated by street anger. Taoyuan society originally had its own interests, figures, and choices.

This judgment also explains why Xu Shin-liang's campaigning methods were so crucial. He did not wait for a national opposition party to build him a stage; instead, he set up his campaign headquarters locally, sent young workers into the countryside, and turned propaganda into repeatable sounds and actions. Folk songs, megaphones, leaflets, and evening rallies caused voters to encounter the election repeatedly in markets, on roads, and at their homes. This coexistence of modern campaigning methods with traditional local relationships and networks was not detached from Taoyuan's social network, but transformed the local network into a mobilized political organization. This organizational experience can still be found in various elections and social movements.[^2] [^4] [^6]

Electoral transparency is also a systemic shadow left behind here. The Taipei Times cited Hu Hui-ling's compilation, pointing out that after the Zhongli Incident, poll workers handled and read each ballot more carefully during counting. This change seems like a procedural detail, but it actually rearranged power. When every ballot had to be publicly displayed, poll workers could not just demand that voters believe them. The procedure began requiring institutions to prove to the voters how ballots were determined, counted, and preserved.[^5]

The media blackout at the time shows that voting transparency and information transparency are two different things. Even if the ballots in the polling place were read aloud one by one, if the event disappeared from newspapers, people in other regions could not judge whether the local government had handled the controversy properly. The time lag between foreign reports and domestic news compiled later by the Control Yuan made this issue clearer: a democratic system requires not only observable voting but also society's ability to discuss what problems the vote produced. Without public memory from the media, election controversies easily remain as private legends among locals.[^9]

The archival work of the Zhongli Incident therefore could not only serve scholars. The police cases, photos, and investigative materials preserved by the National Archives Information Network allowed researchers to compare how agencies described the crowds at the time, and it gave later official investigations a chance to point out which records were inconsistent with interviews. The Control Yuan's suggestion to preserve sites of injustice is precisely about reconnecting archives and locations. The polling place explains the poll procedures; the police precinct explains how the state responded to protests; and the train station and roads explain how news traveled through the city. Only by putting these three types of spaces together can one see the complete scale of the event.

Local memory must also preserve uncomfortable parts. Supporters of Xu Shin-liang might view Zhongli as a successful defense of votes, while memories of the police and public order would remember overturned vehicles, burned buildings, and injured personnel. Transitional justice must address the responsibilities of different roles one by one; it cannot leave behind only the version most favorable to the contemporary position. Election fraud must be investigated, destruction by crowds must be explained, whether the police and military used excessive force must be investigated, and Jiang Wen-guo's death and his family's requests cannot be overshadowed by the outcome of the election.

Zhongli in 1977 allowed Taiwan to see a problem that recurs later: can institutions provide a trustworthy appeal route before a crisis occurs? If the polling place could resolve doubts in real-time, if investigators could treat witnesses and the accused equally, and if the media could show the scene nationwide, then the crowds would not have to bring their anger to the police precinct gate.

After the Zhongli Incident, Taiwan still experienced the Beilidi incident, the lifting of martial law, and multiple election controversies. Democracy was not completed in one go; institutions learned how to let people see them working from wounds, time and again. This is why the Zhongli Incident is still worth preserving. Local archives, family memories, and electoral systems must be preserved together so that history does not only consist of the voices of winners and rulers. Those who look at later results may easily forget that systemic change often begins with local injustice and controversy. It places election procedures, street protests, press freedom, and archival responsibility in one place, forcing subsequent generations to acknowledge that democracy must be maintained, not just praised and recounted on an anniversary.
