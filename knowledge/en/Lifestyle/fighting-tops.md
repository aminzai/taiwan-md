**30-Second Overview:** Beyblade isn't a toy you "stop playing with once you grow up." After the fourth-generation Beyblade X launched in 2023, the manufacturer repositioned the toy line as "GEAR SPORTS" gear-based competition, and in 2025 held the first World Championship encompassing a Taiwan division. Taiwan now hosts over 600 tournaments annually, with scenes of adults lining up for limited-edition tops making television news. This is the history of a toy sport that began with a TV Tokyo anime and evolved through four generations of part systems, and a case study in nostalgia economy and competitive reinvention fermenting simultaneously.

### Dawn Queues: Beyond the Sneaker Market, Tops Take Their Place

In the early hours of May 23, 2026, crowds of Beyblade players surged outside New Taipei's Xinzhuang Honghui Plaza, queuing for that day's national championship "Xtreme Cup G2." Many arrived before dawn to secure limited-edition launchers and tops; the day's top three finishers would walk away with gold, silver, and bronze launchers. That same weekend, the Central Taiwan Open offered NT$6,000 for first place and NT$5,000 for runner-up, with the organizer publishing the champion's build configuration on social media for players to study.

Breaking down the "600 tournaments" figure reveals the concrete skeleton behind this heat: Taipei, Taichung, and Kaohsiung all have fixed venues; channel retailers and department stores host events weekly, totaling over 600 per year. What was once a ten-minute recess toy for elementary students now has adult competitors queuing at dawn, referees adjudicating matches, and build analyses published openly on social media.

### From Beigoma to Plastic Tops: The Birth of a Promotional Campaign

Beyblade's lineage traces back to the traditional Japanese toy "beigoma" (ベイコマ), a wooden spinning top passed down since the Edo period. Players would wind string around their fingertips and whip it to spin the top, then crash tops against each other to determine a winner.

_Beigoma: a Japanese top passed down since the Edo period, spun by whipping a string and crashed against opponents—the inspiration for Beyblade (Photo: Σ64, CC BY 3.0, via Wikimedia Commons)_

In 1999, toy company Takara (which merged with Tomy in 2006 to become Takara Tomy) launched disassemblable plastic battling tops inspired by beigoma, simultaneously releasing a namesake manga and anime to build hype for the toy.

This model found exceptional success in Taiwan, owing much to the Taiwanese-dubbed anime. _Beyblade_ aired on TV Tokyo in Japan starting in 2001, running 51 episodes, produced by Madhouse. The Taiwanese dub featured voice actors He Shih-fang, Chien Hsin-yi, and Chan Ya-ching, broadcast on China Television (CTV) and GTV Variety Show, with dubbing director Kuan Chih-hung. The anime imbued tops with "Sacred Beast" personas—Azure Dragon, Vermilion Bird, White Tiger, Black Tortoise—each with a human partner. Every child's top transformed from homogenized plastic into a named companion. The stadium bowl outside Taiwanese toy shops became the after-school jianghu.

### Four Generations of Parts: Every Revision a "Hard Reset"

Parts across the four Beyblade generations are mutually incompatible; each generation is a standalone reboot. This deliberate "hard reset" design clears inventory for the toy industry and forces a blood transfusion for players:

Beyblade Generational Timeline
| Year | Generation | Key Developments |
|------|------------|------------------|
| 1999 | Generation 1 (Bakuten Shoot Beyblade) | Takara launches plastic battling tops and namesake anime; Hasbro acquires international license in 2002 |
| 2008 | Metal Fusion | Metal parts introduced, collision sounds and spark effects enhanced. Anime airs in 2009 |
| 2015 | Burst Generation | Layered part system introduced. Tops "burst" apart under heavy impact, adding a new win condition |
| 2023 | Beyblade X | Gear rail system and Xtreme stadium; manufacturer positions as GEAR SPORTS competition |

Sources: Hasbro Newsroom, Wikipedia, 2025

The Metal Fusion generation (2008 toys, 2009 anime) gave tops the sound of metal collisions and visual sparks. The Burst generation (from 2015) introduced a layered structure; tops would "burst" apart under heavy impact, adding a sudden dramatic tension to win determination. Notably for Taiwanese players, the official Asian Championship titles in both 2017 and 2018 were won by Taiwanese competitors; Taiwan was already a competitive market of substance in that generation.

The fourth-generation Beyblade X launched in 2023; its biggest innovation was adding gear rails along the stadium rim: when a top's bit engages the rail, it suddenly accelerates into a sprint, visibly faster to the naked eye. The manufacturer formally defined this toy line as "GEAR SPORTS"—selling toys, but also running a sport.

### Scoring Mechanics Inside the Stadium

In official matches, two players launch their tops into a bowl-shaped stadium. There are four win conditions: Spin Finish (out-spinning the opponent) scores 1 point; Over Finish (knocking the opponent out of the stadium) scores 2 points; Burst Finish (causing the opponent to burst apart) scores 2 points; and the X-generation-exclusive Xtreme Finish (opponent's top falls into the pocket zone and cannot return to the battle area) scores the maximum 3 points.

Tops are categorized into four types—Attack, Defense, Stamina, and Balance—with a rock-paper-scissors counter logic: Attack beats Stamina, Stamina beats Defense, Defense beats Attack.

The player community's arms race goes far finer than these basic rules. Every individual top of the same model has minute physical variations; advanced players use digital scales to select the heaviest parts. Launch force, angle, and landing position all affect the outcome; the official mobile app even records RPM after each launch. This toy now has data, training, and builds—far removed from "leaving it to fate."

### The World Stage and Hasbro's Ledger

The manufacturer's competitive push isn't just sloganeering. On March 21, 2025—"Beyblade Day"—Hasbro announced the global launch of the BEYBLADE X World Championship, with regional qualifiers starting in April and the final settling at Tokyo Tower in early October. The official press release named participating regions including the United States, Canada, France, Taiwan, and Brazil. The same release cited market research firm Circana data: the Beyblade X Xtreme Battle Set was the #1 bestseller in the action figure category in 2024.

At the same time, the broader toy industry faced headwinds. In the first half of 2025, Hasbro's toy division revenue declined due to tariff impacts and weak retailer orders, with traditional toy lines under pressure overall. Yet reviewing that year's financial report, Hasbro listed Beyblade as one of the Consumer Products division's growth highlights. Amid an entirely downward-trending curve, a 27-year-old top grew against the current. In this business, nostalgia is one of the few categories still expanding—not a comfort pill for a declining era.

### Trends and Nostalgia: How the Top Returned to Adult Pockets

In 2024, actor Timothée Chalamet appeared on camera holding a Beyblade at the premiere of _A Complete Unknown_; the image went viral on social media. In Taiwan, the resurgence's ignition point was more everyday: a Threads post asking about Beyblade during this year's Lunar New Year period turned into a massive "getting-into-the-hobby exchange," with stand-up comedian and host Bowen appearing in the comments to share his own build.

**📝 Curator's Note**

Nostalgia economy is often described as "selling memories," but Beyblade's resurgence exposes a more interesting mechanism: **memories are just the admission ticket; competition is the retention mechanism.** Pokémon cards' revival rides the collector market; Tamagotchi leans on reissue nostalgia. Only Beyblade redesigned a childhood object into a sport with data, schedules, and rankings—turning consumers from "buy to collect" into "buy to compete," lifting both average transaction value and repeat purchase rate simultaneously. Taiwanese players winning the 2018 Asian Championship, Taiwan being named a World Championship division in 2025, and players queuing at dawn in 2026—these three events linked together show Taiwan happens to be one of the markets where "competitive reinvention" has shown the clearest results.

### Gray Areas: Adult Seriousness and Children's Toys

Competitive reinvention reaching this stage has also brought problems the toy industry isn't used to. News reports have exposed players in official tournaments secretly adding weight to illegally modify their builds, even disguising themselves to register and compete multiple times. When adults' desire to win overrides children's play spirit, referee systems and rule loopholes become a necessary growing pain. These irregularities are themselves evidence: the top has become important enough to cheat for.

**📝 Curator's Note**

Note the generational dislocation in this renaissance: **toy makers are selling a children's toy spec to decidedly un-childlike adults.** The official recommended age is 8+, but the ones queuing at dawn for limited launchers are adults. The World Championship established a Regular Class for ages 8–12 and an Open Class for 13+, yet couldn't stop adults from crowding into the adult division. The toy's "age ceiling" failed in the face of competitive reinvention—an ending probably no one wrote into the slide deck at Takara's 1999 planning meeting.

### Epilogue: The Next Launch

Back to that morning at Honghui Plaza. Among those queuing, many had also queued twenty-odd years ago—except back then they were lining up for the Four Sacred Beasts series: Dragoon, Dranzer, Driger, Draciel, the starter tops they'd scraped together pocket money to buy. Now they use adult incomes to research bit gear counts, weigh parts on digital scales, and queue at dawn for limited launchers.

The top itself hasn't changed; the people launching them have grown up. The toy maker didn't resist the tide—instead, they turned it into a tournament. The next time you hear "3, 2, 1, GO SHOOT," listen closely—that may be a group of adults finally holding the national championship their ten-minute recess deserved, twenty years late.
