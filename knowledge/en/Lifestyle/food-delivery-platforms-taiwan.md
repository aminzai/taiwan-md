On May 1, 2021, delivery riders joined laborers in marching along Ketagalan Boulevard. At the same time, meals in Taipei continued to leave restaurants, traverse roads, and arrive at homes where people could not or would not go out. That year, the number of food delivery riders in Taiwan approached 88,000. [^1]

A few years ago, ordering a meal was like handing over your life to the phone of a nearby shop. During the pandemic, the proportion of restaurants offering home delivery and delivery services rose from 43.3% in April 2019 to 67.0% during the Level 3 lockdown in June 2021. Even as indoor dining gradually resumed in May 2022, 64.6% of restaurants continued to offer delivery services. [^2]

> Caption: According to CNA reports, if the Fair Trade Commission’s review is approved, Grab originally planned to complete the transaction in the second half of 2026. Image and event information are subject to the CNA article. [^10]

Delivery is therefore more than just "someone bringing you food." It connects the restaurant’s kitchen, the platform’s algorithms, the rider’s phone and motorcycle, the city’s roads, and the door where the meal is received into a single line. This line shortens wait times but also concentrates costs, originally scattered across different places, onto an invisible person.

📝 Curator’s Note: The most noteworthy aspect of delivery is not how convenient it is, but who actually completes the act of "convenience."

## The Bed on the Youth Hostel Bunk Rack Catches Delivery Income

In 2021, the Social Innovation Platform interviewed a youth using the pseudonym "Ah Xiang." He worked odd jobs at a restaurant during the day and delivered food by bicycle at night. His residence was not a private room with a door, but an upper bunk approximately 300 centimeters off the ground in a youth hostel in Taipei city. The bunk cost about 6,000 to 8,000 NTD per month, required no rental deposit, and was close to the city center where he received orders. [^3]

This scene materializes "flexible work" into something tangible: a bed, a bicycle, a phone, and more than two hours online after work. For Ah Xiang, delivery income was once a means to repay student loans, save money, and even move out of the upper bunk. After the platform changed its salary calculation method, he encountered empty waits, fewer orders, and shrinking income, causing his original flexibility to narrow. [^3]

This case does not represent all delivery riders, but it reminds us that "freedom" on platforms is never suspended in the air. It attaches to rent, student loans, transportation, and the next order. When one of these links changes, so-called freedom may become an obligation to continue accepting orders.

## A Meal Actually Travels Through an Entire City

A 2023 study on Taipei delivery riders interviewed riders, restaurant owners, and platform personnel, and also involved the researcher in actual delivery labor. The study describes delivery as a configuration composed of digital platforms, phones, motorcycles, delivery equipment, roads, buildings, users, and restaurants. [^4]

This observation is crucial because delivery riders do not work solely within the "platform." They must find the restaurant entrance, judge whether a building allows parking, identify addresses and floors, and handle friction between elevators, rain, motorcycles, and thermal bags. Gaps not pre-processed by the system are often filled by people in motion. The study therefore points out that the piece-rate system and the acceleration structure of motorized vehicle cities may push more burdens and risks back onto delivery riders. [^4]

Restaurants are also placed within this configuration. The Department of Statistics, Ministry of Economic Affairs (MOEA) notes that the pandemic and changing consumption habits have accelerated the restaurant industry’s adoption of social media, mobile payments, and online ordering. Delivery has gradually evolved from a temporary contingency into an operational method for expanding sales reach. [^2]

## Taipei’s Roads See Risks First

Delivery brings meals to doorsteps and work to roads. Cross-agency audit data released by the Highway Bureau, Ministry of Transportation and Communications, shows that from January to September 2021, there were 1,567 motorcycle accidents involving delivery platforms in Taipei, resulting in 1,212 injuries or deaths. During the same period, police enforced 7,113 violations against delivery platforms. These numbers only cover specific areas and periods and cannot be expanded into a full-year national statistic, but they suffice to show that road safety is not a problem solvable solely by riders’ caution. [^5]

In the same press release, the Highway Bureau noted that delivery platform motorcycles use public roads for delivery, and operators should bear supervisory and management responsibilities. Subsequent measures include physical safety driving training for motorcycles and requiring riders involved in accidents to undergo retraining. The key turning point here is: delivery is no longer viewed solely as a transaction between the platform and consumers, but is placed back into the context of public roads and public safety. [^5]

A 2021 reader submission in _The Reporter_ compiled another set of labor site data: the author cited a 2020 survey of delivery riders, indicating that 24% of respondents delivered for 50 hours per week, and 81.7% feared being in a car accident. The article also pointed out that the actual coverage of safety education, protective equipment, and prevention of high/low-temperature hazards was inconsistent. This is the compilation and judgment of advocates and should be read separately from official statistics, but it gives shape to the work pressure behind "hurry up a bit." [^1]

## The Word "Contractor" Cannot Contain the Whole Road

In 2019, an article published by the Taiwan Labor Front recorded that the Department of Occupational Safety and Health, Ministry of Labor, had launched special labor inspections against foodpanda and Uber Eats, determining that an employment relationship existed between delivery riders and the platforms. The platforms, however, claimed they were contractors or merely matchmakers. The article also compiled how uniforms, standardized thermal bags, scheduling, and non-refusal rules became points of contention in determining command, supervision, and organizational subordination. [^6]

This is not merely a dispute over terminology. If a person can ostensibly go online independently but is actually influenced by order acceptance rates, scheduling, remuneration algorithms, service ratings, and suspension mechanisms, then "being one's own boss" and "work being controlled by the platform" may coexist. The 2016 Judicial Yuan Interpretation No. 740, which addressed labor contract judgments, also emphasized that one should not look only at the contract name, but observe the method of labor provision and business risks. [^7]

The Taipei study collected by Airiti describes this contradiction closer to the street: delivery riders indeed require on-site judgment, movement, and gap-filling, thus possessing agency. But this agency may also be absorbed by the piece-rate system and the accelerating city, ultimately becoming a system cost borne by individuals. [^4]

📝 Curator’s Note: The real question is not whether delivery riders have freedom, but who must pay when freedom fails.

## Grab Acquires foodpanda: A Different Company Behind the Button

In March 2026, Grab announced the acquisition of foodpanda’s Taiwan business for approximately USD 600 million (approx. TWD 19.3 billion). On March 27 of the same year, Grab submitted an application to the Fair Trade Commission (FTC). The FTC officially accepted the case in mid-June, stating that the review would focus on post-merger market impact, monopoly concerns, and whether new competitors can enter the market. [^10]

This transaction is relevant to daily life not just because the logos of two brands might swap positions. The partner restaurants, users, delivery riders, and order data controlled by the platform will be integrated with the platform. Restaurants may face new commission rates and exposure rules; delivery riders may face new dispatch and remuneration systems; consumers may have to rechoose in a more concentrated market. The latter three are life impacts requiring review, not already-occurred results; speculation cannot be written as fact.

On July 22, 2026, the FTC officially announced it would extend the review period by 60 working days to October 27. The FTC stated that the post-merger market structure still maintains the "duopoly" status quo from before the merger. Meanwhile, Uber holds 13% of Grab’s shares and 3.7% of voting rights. The FTC needs to further analyze whether this equity link affects Uber Eats and Grab’s incentive and ability to compete, potentially forming unilateral or coordinated effects. [^11] [^14]

Grab stated to CNA that if the transaction is approved, it expects to complete the transaction in the second half of 2026 and complete platform integration in the first half of 2027, fully migrating original foodpanda users to the Grab application. This is a plan proposed by the enterprise, not a schedule already approved by the FTC. Before the review concludes, consumers cannot treat "definite migration" as an occurred result. [^13]

The same CNA report also compiled Grab’s statements on governance and equity: Grab is headquartered in Singapore and listed on the US Nasdaq. Uber holds approximately 13% of common stock, but voting rights are below 4%. Uber does not participate in Grab’s daily operations, and Uber-nominated Grab directors recuse themselves from all board decisions related to Taiwan. These are Grab’s public statements; the FTC still needs to make independent judgments based on substantive control and competitive effects. [^13]

Another key to the transaction is not "whose App color will change," but how data, partner restaurants, delivery riders, and consumer relationships are rearranged. CNA reports indicate that the integration plan between Grab and foodpanda involves original foodpanda users moving into Grab. Regarding how commissions, dispatch, remuneration, and restaurant contracts will change, existing sources do not provide confirmed plans; thus, this article does not write potential impacts as established facts. [^13]

> Caption: A transaction illustration photo attached to the CNA report on March 30, 2026, directly corresponds to the event of Grab applying to acquire foodpanda’s Taiwan business. [^13]

This case cannot be conflated with the 2024 case of Uber Eats directly acquiring foodpanda. In a 2024 public article, the FTC stated that the anti-competitive disadvantages of the Uber Eats and foodpanda merger outweighed the overall economic benefits, thus prohibiting the merger. The Grab case was still under review as of August 15, 2026, and cannot be pre-written as having the same result. [^12]

📝 Curator’s Note: "Choice" in the delivery market appears not only on consumers’ phone screens, but also in whether there is sufficient competitive distance between platforms.

## Only After Platforms Become Daily Infrastructure Does the System Catch Up

The Government Information Portal compiled labor rights for delivery riders and freelancers in June 2026, placing labor and health insurance, laborers’ occupational disaster insurance, and the food platform delivery riders’ rights zone within the same service context. This compilation itself reveals institutional change: delivery riders are no longer just vague identities in the startup industry, but workers who need to be placed into social insurance, occupational disaster, and labor protection systems. [^8]

But the fact that institutions are written down does not mean risks have disappeared. A _Reporter_ article mentioned that after 2019, the government successively issued safety guidelines for food delivery operations, covering traffic accident prevention, heat hazards, dispatch, and insurance. The same article questioned the gap between actual education and protection using questionnaire data. Norms, execution, and the streets delivery riders encounter daily still need to be examined together. [^1]

Public compilations of Legislative Yuan issues also show that delivery platform governance involves not only labor rights, but also market competition, restaurants, consumers, and the impact of platform mergers. The issue page records legislators’ questions about market concentration and the influence of transnational platforms, as well as the proposal to establish a dedicated delivery law. These are public claims by politicians and do not equal established judicial or economic conclusions. [^9]

Delivery platforms thus become a peculiar type of life infrastructure: when we use it, we only see the meal status change from "preparing" to "delivered." But between these two states, there are restaurant output speeds, platform dispatch logic, riders’ routes, road safety, and whether the system truly allows people to obtain protection after accidents.

## Three Types of Waiting Not Shown on Phone Screens

Consumers see a simple progress bar: restaurant accepts order, meal preparing, rider picks up, arriving soon. But Airiti’s research reminds us that behind these statuses is not a single process, but the coordination of many objects and people. An address on a phone must be translated into the actual positions of the restaurant entrance, elevator, stairs, security, and recipient. [^4]

Restaurants see different waiting. The MOEA lists the proportion of delivery services alongside online ordering, mobile payments, and social media management, showing that the restaurant industry faces not a single choice of "whether to join the platform," but how to connect the kitchen, orders, payment, and delivery. [^2]

Delivery riders face a third type of waiting: waiting for platform dispatch, waiting for restaurants to output meals, waiting for elevators, waiting for gaps in the road to pass safely. These times are not necessarily visible in a single order, but they affect how many orders can be completed in a day, and also affect income and fatigue. In the profile report, Ah Xiang’s empty waits and few orders are precisely this interface-hidden time suddenly surfacing. [^3]

Therefore, the efficiency of delivery platforms cannot be measured solely by "how many minutes from order to delivery." Only when research, statistics, and profile interviews are viewed together do we discover that part of the efficiency is actually completed by riders’ on-site gap-filling. [^2] [^3] [^4]

## The City Begins to Take Responsibility for the Button

The Highway Bureau’s audit places delivery platforms into road management, rather than attributing accidents solely to the rider. The Government Information Portal places delivery riders into the service context of labor/health insurance and occupational disaster insurance. Two institutional languages, one handling roads and one handling work, together come closer to the true position of delivery. [^5] [^8]

The merger and market concentration discussions presented in the Legislative Yuan issue compilation also show that platforms are not neutral screens. As platform scale, algorithms, and the dependency relationships among restaurants, consumers, and delivery riders grow together, competition policy and labor policy meet within the same life service. These political figures’ claims still need to be separated from formal regulations and competent authority decisions, but they point to the direction public discourse is questioning. [^9]

A 2019 article by the Taiwan Labor Front condensed the problem into an institutional dilemma: workers appear to gain scheduling freedom, but may simultaneously lose employer responsibility and occupational disaster protection. This question remains worth retaining today, because every time the platform changes dispatch, remuneration, or rating methods, it rearranges who bears uncertainty. [^6]

## Before Pressing "Send Order" Next Time

Taiwan’s delivery life does not need to be written as "technology makes everything better," nor simplified as "platforms only exploit." MOEA statistics show the restaurant industry indeed expands services through delivery. Research and profile reports explain that this convenience must arrive through specific people, vehicles, roads, and residences. Both sides are true; only together do they approach the full picture of life. [^2] [^3] [^4]

This also explains why a good delivery article cannot only cite the platform’s own reports. Government statistics are suitable for answering the proportion of restaurants offering delivery; transportation authorities are suitable for answering accidents and audits; academic research is suitable for deconstructing labor configurations; profile reports return institutional numbers to a person’s bed, bicycle, and night. They cannot replace each other, but can mutually limit overly simplified conclusions. [^2] [^3] [^4] [^5]

Therefore, next time you press "Send Order," what is worth remembering is not whether to order delivery, but where that convenience comes from. When a city shrinks waiting to a few minutes, it should also have the ability to put reasonable remuneration, safe roads, and accountable responsibility back onto the same road.

This division of labor also reminds readers not to write "consumers, restaurants, platforms, delivery riders" as four unrelated roles. Research interviews originally placed delivery riders, restaurants, and platform personnel in the same research site. The Ministry of Transportation and Communications’ audit also places operator responsibility and road usage together. [^4] [^5]

When institutions separate responsibility management, life still binds them to a single order; problems will repeatedly appear at the intersection. Seeing the intersection is the starting point for understanding Taiwan’s delivery daily life. [^4] [^6]

This is also the reason institutions must continuously update, rather than only filling gaps after accidents occur. [^5] [^8]

In the 2021 photo of the labor march, delivery riders were not abstract "platform manpower." They wore their own clothes, stood on their own roads, and demanded this city acknowledge: what is delivered to the doorstep is not just a meal, but the time someone left after crossing the city.
