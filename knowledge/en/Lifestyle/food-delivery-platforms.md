On May 1, 2021, delivery drivers marched along Crescent Boulevard alongside the labor contingent. At the same time, meals in Taipei still traveled from restaurants, across streets, to homes that couldn't or didn't want to step outside. By that year, the number of food delivery drivers in Taiwan had approached 88,000.[^1]

A few years ago, ordering a meal was like handing your life over to a nearby restaurant by phone. During the pandemic, the share of restaurants offering delivery and takeout rose from 43.3% in April 2019 to 67.0% in June 2021 during the third-level alert. Even as dine-in gradually recovered in May 2022, 64.6% of restaurants still offered delivery services.[^2]

> Caption: According to a report by The Central News Agency, if the Fair Trade Commission approves the review, Grab originally planned to complete the acquisition by the second half of 2026. Photo and event information are based on the CNA article.[^10]

Delivery thus became more than "someone bringing you food." It connected the restaurant kitchen, the platform algorithm, the delivery driver's phone and scooter, the city's roads, and the recipient's doorstep all on one line. This line shortened wait times and concentrated costs—previously scattered across different places—onto a single invisible person.

📝 Curator's note: The most worth writing about delivery isn't how convenient it is, but who actually completes that "convenience."

## A Bed on the Hostel Iron Frame, Catching Delivery Income

In 2021, the Social Innovation Platform interviewed a young person surnamed A-Hsiung. He worked odd jobs at a restaurant during the day and rode a bicycle to deliver meals at night. His residence wasn't a gated apartment but a youth hostel in downtown Taipei, sleeping on a bunk bed about 300 centimeters off the ground. The bed cost about NT$6,000 to NT$8,000 per month, requiring no deposit for renting, and was close to the city center where he received orders.[^3]

This scene made "flexible work" tangible: a bed, a bicycle, a phone, and two more hours online after getting off work. For A-Hsiung, delivery income once covered student loans, savings, and even the possibility of moving out of the bunk bed. After the platform changed its pay calculation, he faced idle waiting, fewer orders, and reduced income, and his original flexibility narrowed along with it.[^3]

Individual cases can't represent all delivery drivers, but they remind us that the "freedom" on the platform was never floating in the air. It hung on rent, student loans, transportation, and the next order. When any of these links changed, so-called freedom might turn into a necessity to keep accepting orders.

## A Single Meal Travels Across an Entire City

A 2023 study on Taipei delivery drivers interviewed drivers, restaurants, and platform staff, with researchers also participating in delivery work themselves. The study described delivery as a configuration composed of digital platforms, phones, scooters, transport equipment, roads, buildings, users, and restaurants.[^4]

This observation is important because delivery drivers don't just work "on the platform." They have to find the restaurant entrance, judge whether a building allows parking, identify the address and floor, and deal with friction between elevators, rain, scooters, and insulated food bags. Gaps not pre-processed by the system are often filled by people on the move. The study thus pointed out that piece-rate systems and the accelerated structure of motor vehicle cities might push more burden and risk back onto delivery drivers.[^4]

Restaurants were placed into this configuration as well. The Ministry of Economic Affairs' statistics bureau noted that the pandemic and changes in consumption habits accelerated restaurants' adoption of social media, mobile payments, and online ordering. Delivery, originally an emergency response, gradually became a way to expand sales channels.[^2]

These numbers don't mean everyone orders delivery every day, nor can they directly lead to the platform's market share. They only show a narrower but more reliable fact: for the restaurant industry, delivery is no longer just an occasionally used add-on service.

## Taipei's Roads First Saw the Risks

Delivery brings meals to doorsteps and work to the roads. Cross-agency inspection data released by the Ministry of Transportation's Road Administration showed 1,567 scooter accidents involving delivery platforms in Taipei from January to September 2021, with 1,212 casualties. During the same period, police cracked down on delivery platform violations 7,113 times. These figures only cover specific areas and timeframes and cannot be generalized to nationwide annual statistics, but they are enough to show that road safety is not something that can be solved by delivery drivers' carefulness alone.[^5]

In the same press release, the Road Administration mentioned that delivery platform scooters using roads for transport should take responsibility for supervision and management. Subsequent measures included practical safety driving training for scooters and requiring delivery drivers involved in accidents to retrain. The key turning point here was that delivery was no longer just seen as a transaction between the platform and consumers but was put back into the context of public roads and public safety.[^5]

A 2021 reader letter in The Reporter compiled another set of workplace data: the author cited a 2020 survey of delivery drivers, pointing out that 24% of respondents delivered for 50 hours per week, and 81.7% worried about getting into accidents. The article also noted that the actual coverage of safety education, protective gear, and high and low temperature hazard prevention was inconsistent. This was an advocate's summary and judgment and should be read separately from official statistics, but it gave shape to the work pressure behind "hurry up a bit."[^1]

## The Word "Chartered" Can't Hold the Whole Road

In 2019, an article published by the Taiwan Labor Front recorded that the Ministry of Labor's Occupational Safety and Health Administration launched a special inspection of foodpanda and Uber Eats, and determined that there was an employment relationship between delivery drivers and the platform. The platform argued that they were either chartered or simply intermediaries. The article also summarized how uniforms, standard insulated food bags, scheduling, and the inability to refuse orders became points of contention in determining command supervision and organizational subordination.[^6]

This wasn't just a simple noun dispute. If a person appears to be able to go online independently but is actually affected by order acceptance rates, scheduling, pay algorithms, service evaluations, and suspension mechanisms, then "being one's own boss" and "being controlled by the platform at work" might exist at the same time. The 2016 Judicial Yuan Interpretation No. 740 on labor contract judgments also emphasized that one shouldn't just look at the contract name but should observe the method of providing labor services and business risks.[^7]

The Airiti Library's Taipei study described this contradiction in terms closer to the street: delivery drivers indeed need on-site judgment, mobility, and gap-filling, and thus have initiative. But these initiatives might be absorbed by piece-rate systems and accelerated cities, eventually turning into individuals bearing the cost of the system.[^4]

📝 Curator's note: The real issue isn't whether delivery drivers have freedom, but who has to pay when freedom fails.

## Grab Acquires foodpanda, a Different Company Behind the Button

In March 2026, Grab announced it would acquire foodpanda's Taiwan business for approximately US$600 million (about NT$19.3 billion). On March 27 of the same year, Grab submitted an application to the Fair Trade Commission. The Fair Trade Commission officially accepted the case in mid-June and stated that the review would focus on the market impact after the merger, monopoly concerns, and whether new competitors could enter the market.[^10]

This transaction was related to daily life not just because two brand logos might change positions. The data on partner restaurants, users, delivery drivers, and orders held by the platform would also be merged with the platform. Restaurants might face new commission and exposure rules, delivery drivers might face new order dispatching and compensation systems, and consumers might re-choose in a more concentrated market. The latter three are living impacts that need to be reviewed, not results that have already occurred, and speculation should not be written as fact.

On July 22, 2026, the Fair Trade Commission officially announced it would extend the review period by 60 working days to October 27. The Fair Trade Commission pointed out that the market structure after the merger still maintained the "duopoly" situation before the merger. At the same time, Uber holds 13% of Grab's shares and 3.7% of voting rights, and the Fair Trade Commission needs to further analyze whether this shareholding link affects Uber Eats' and Grab's incentives and capabilities to compete, and may form unilateral or joint effects.[^11] [^14]

Grab told The Central News Agency that if the transaction is approved, it is expected to be completed in the second half of 2026, and the platform integration will be completed in the first half of 2027, transferring all original foodpanda users to the Grab app. This was a corporate plan, not a schedule approved by the Fair Trade Commission. Before the review is over, consumers still can't treat "will definitely be transferred" as an already occurred result.[^13]

The same CNA report also compiled Grab's statements on governance and equity: Grab is headquartered in Singapore and listed on the US Nasdaq. Uber holds about 13% of ordinary shares but less than 4% of voting rights, Uber does not participate in Grab's daily operations, and the Grab director nominated by Uber recuses himself from all board decisions related to Taiwan. These were Grab's public statements, and the Fair Trade Commission still needs to make independent judgments based on actual control and competitive effects.[^13]

Another key point of the transaction was not "whose app color will change" but how data, partner restaurants, delivery drivers, and consumer relationships are rearranged. The Central News Agency reported that Grab's merger plan with foodpanda involves transferring original foodpanda users to Grab. As for how commissions, order dispatching, compensation, and restaurant contracts will change, the existing sources have not provided confirmed plans, so this article does not write possible impacts as established facts.[^13]

> Caption: The transaction illustration photo attached to the Central News Agency's March 30, 2026 report, directly corresponding to Grab's application to acquire foodpanda's Taiwan business.[^13]

This case also cannot be conflated with Uber Eats' direct acquisition of foodpanda in 2024. The Fair Trade Commission stated in a 2024 public article that the anti-competitive interests of the Uber Eats and foodpanda merger were greater than the overall economic interests, and thus prohibited the merger. The Grab case was still under review on August 15, 2026, and could not be written as the same result in advance.[^12]

📝 Curator's note: The "choice" in the delivery market doesn't only appear on the consumer's phone screen but also in whether there is still enough competitive distance between platforms.

## After the Platform Becomes Daily Life, the System Starts to Catch Up

In June 2026, the government's portal website compiled the labor rights of delivery drivers and freelancers, placing labor health insurance, labor occupational accident insurance, and the special zone for food delivery drivers' rights in the same service context. This kind of organization itself reveals a change in the system: delivery drivers are no longer just a vague identity in the emerging industry but workers who need to be placed in the system of social insurance, occupational accident insurance, and labor protection.[^8]

But the system being written out doesn't mean the risks have disappeared. The Reporter mentioned that after 2019, the government successively released food delivery operation safety guidelines, covering traffic accident prevention, heat hazards, order dispatching, and insurance issues. The same article also questioned the actual gap between education and protection with questionnaire data. Regulations, enforcement, and the streets delivery drivers encounter every day still need to be checked together.[^1]

The public compilation of legislative questions also showed that the governance of delivery platforms involves not only labor rights but also market competition, restaurants, consumers, and the impact of platform mergers. The issue page recorded legislators' questions about market concentration and the influence of transnational platforms, as well as the advocacy for establishing a special law for delivery platforms. These were public statements by political figures, not established judicial or economic conclusions.[^9]

The delivery platform thus became a very special living infrastructure: when we use it, we only see the meal status change from "preparing" to "delivered." But between these two states, there are still the restaurant's meal preparation speed, the platform's order dispatching logic, the delivery driver's route, the safety of the roads, and whether the system really allows people to be protected after an accident.

## The Three Waits Not Shown on the Phone Screen

Consumers see a simple progress bar: restaurant accepts order, meal is prepared, delivery driver picks up the meal, and is about to arrive. But the Airiti study reminded us that behind these statuses is not a single process but the coordination of many objects and people. An address on the phone must be translated into the actual location of the restaurant door, elevator, stairs, security, and recipient.

The wait seen by restaurants is also different. The Ministry of Economic Affairs' statistics bureau listed the share of delivery restaurants alongside online ordering, mobile payments, and social media operations, showing that the restaurant industry is not facing a choice of "whether or not to join the platform" but how to connect the kitchen, orders, payments, and delivery.

Delivery drivers face the third kind of wait: waiting for the platform to assign orders, waiting for the restaurant to prepare the meal, waiting for the elevator, and waiting for a safe gap in the road to pass through. These times are not necessarily visible in a single order but will affect how many orders can be completed in a day and will also affect income and fatigue. In the human report, A-Hsiung's idle waiting and fewer orders were exactly the time hidden by the interface suddenly surfacing.[^3]

Therefore, the efficiency of delivery platforms cannot be measured only by "how many minutes from ordering to delivery." Only by looking at research, statistics, and human interviews together can we discover that part of the efficiency is actually completed by delivery drivers filling gaps on-site.[^2] [^3] [^4]

## The City Begins to Take Responsibility for the Button

The Road Administration's inspection put the delivery platform into road management, rather than attributing accidents solely to the riders. The government's portal website put delivery drivers into the service context of labor health insurance and occupational accident insurance. The two kinds of institutional language, one dealing with roads and the other dealing with work, together come closer to the true position of delivery.

The compilation of legislative questions on mergers and market concentration also shows that the platform is not a neutral screen. When the platform's size, algorithms, and the dependence of restaurants, consumers, and delivery drivers all grow, competition policy and labor policy will meet in the same living service. These political figures' propositions still need to be separated from formal laws and regulations and the decisions of the competent authorities, but they point to the direction of the public discussion being questioned.[^9]

The Taiwan Labor Front's article in 2019 condensed the problem into a system issue: workers seem to obtain scheduling freedom but may simultaneously lose employer responsibility and occupational accident insurance. This issue is still worth keeping today because every time the platform changes order dispatching, compensation, or evaluation methods, it will re-arrange who bears the uncertainty.[^6]

## Before Pressing Send Next Time

Taiwan's delivery life doesn't need to be written as "technology makes everything better," nor does it need to be simplified as "the platform only exploits." The Ministry of Economic Affairs' statistics show that the restaurant industry indeed expands services through delivery. Research and human reports show that this convenience must reach specific people, cars, roads, and homes. Both are true, and together they are closer to the full picture of life.[^2] [^3] [^4]

This also explains why a good delivery article can't just cite the platform's own report. Government statistics are suitable for answering the proportion of restaurants offering delivery, transportation authorities are suitable for answering accidents and inspections, academic research is suitable for disassembling labor configurations, and human reports let the system numbers return to a person's bed, bicycle, and night. They can't replace each other, but they can limit overly simplified conclusions.[^2] [^3] [^4] [^5]

So, the next time you press "submit order," it's worth remembering that it's not about whether to order delivery but where the convenience comes from. When a city shrinks waiting time to a few minutes, it should also have the ability to put reasonable compensation, safe roads, and accountable responsibility back on the same road.

This division of labor also reminds readers not to write "consumers, restaurants, platforms, and delivery drivers" as four unrelated roles. Research interviews originally put delivery drivers, restaurants, and platform staff in the same research site. The Ministry of Transportation's inspection also put business responsibility and road use together.[^4] [^5]

When the system separates responsibilities for management, life still binds them to a single order, and problems will keep appearing at the intersection. Seeing the intersection is the starting point for understanding Taiwan's delivery daily life.[^4] [^6]

This is also the reason why the system must continue to update, rather than just filling gaps after accidents occur.[^5] [^8]

In the 2021 photo of the labor parade, delivery drivers were not abstract "platform labor." They wore their own clothes, stood on their own roads, and demanded that this city acknowledge that what arrives at the doorstep is not just a meal but also the time left by someone passing through the city.
