# Hot Spring Culture: From Military Recuperation to a Healing Empire

In March 1896, when Japanese ronin Hirata Genwu built "Tengukan" beside Beitou Creek to treat his injured leg and beriberi, this former police officer with a bullet wound on his knee could never have imagined that this crude hot spring inn would open the curtain on Taiwan's hot spring civilization. Even less did he foresee that this industry, accidentally born from war and healing trauma, would eventually elevate Taiwan to a healing empire possessing one of the world's only three natural mud hot springs and one of the world's only three underwater hot springs.

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's hot spring culture originated from Japanese colonial military medical needs, evolving from an accidental personal discovery into a world-class density distribution of 128 hot spring areas. Both Guanziling's muddy hot springs and Green Island's underwater hot springs represent rare geological miracles—only three such places exist globally—while Taiwanese hot springs uniquely blend Japanese healing traditions with local social culture, creating an incomparable "hot spring+" compound experience.

**Taiwan's hot spring culture was not born from tourism, but from the unexpected interweaving of war and healing.**

Today, Taiwan hosts 128 hot spring areas distributed across the entire island with an astounding density of one spring per 281 square kilometers—far exceeding Japan (roughly 3 per 1,000 square kilometers). From Beitou's sulfuric acid springs to Guanziling's black mud baths, from Jiaoxi's beauty waters to Green Island's underwater hot springs, Taiwanese people have created a uniquely "Taiwanese hot spring civilization"—blending Japanese ritual sensibility, Taiwanese warmth, and modern urban convenience into an exceptional experience.

## A Healing Empire Born from War

Taiwan's hot spring development's true origin story begins with the Japanese military's medical crisis in 1895.

In the early years of Japanese colonial rule, tropical diseases and medical scarcity inflicted devastating losses on Japanese forces. The Governor-General's Office urgently needed to establish a military convalescence system, beginning systematic surveying of hot spring resources across the island. With every hot spring discovery, they immediately conducted water quality analysis and assessed the possibility of establishing military sanatoriums.

**Hirata Genwu was an accidental trailblazer in this context.** This former police officer who participated in the anti-shogunate movement came to Taiwan originally hoping to strike it rich mining gold. When his application was rejected and he fell injured while prospecting, coupled with beriberi afflicting him simultaneously, he heard rumors of hot springs in the foothills of Mount Datun and decided to try his luck in desperation.

On November 25, 1895, Hirata lodged with a local Taiwanese Chen family in Beitou and began soaking in the creek to heal his wounds. After a month of recovery, he decided to establish a simple hot spring inn here—Tengukan, becoming Taiwan's first hot spring operator.

**But the real catalyst for Beitou's hot spring development was systematic government investment.** In August 1896, Taipei Military Administration's financial chief Matsumoto Kametaro constructed "Shotoen," exclusively for military official convalescence. Subsequently, Houyoen and Beitou Hall were established in succession, transforming Beitou from an accidental personal medical discovery into an officially certified convalescence base.

**This military-medicine-oriented development model accidentally established Taiwan's hot spring industry's quality genetic foundation.** To meet military convalescence needs, the Japanese government imposed strict requirements for water quality, facilities, and service standards—standards that persist to this day, becoming Taiwan's hot spring industry's competitive advantage.

> **Did You Know?** Without war, Taiwan might not have developed today's hot spring culture. The historical entanglement of healing and killing created the purest spaces for body and mind restoration.

## Earth's Exclusive Gift to Taiwan

Taiwan's world-class hot spring status stems from extraordinarily fortunate geological conditions.

Located at the junction of the Eurasian Plate and the Philippine Sea Plate, Taiwan possesses the world's highest density of hot spring distribution. With 128 hot spring areas distributed across 36,000 square kilometers of land, the average density reaches one spring per 281 square kilometers—a density far exceeding hot spring nations Japan (roughly 3 per 1,000 square kilometers) and Iceland (roughly 1 per 1,000 square kilometers).

Even more remarkable is the rarity of spring water types. Within Taiwan's 400-kilometer north-south range, you can experience virtually all major hot spring types found on Earth: from Beitou's acidic sulfate springs (pH 1.5-3) to Jiaoxi's alkaline sodium bicarbonate springs (pH 8-9), with temperatures ranging from 40°C to 75°C, and mineral compositions encompassing sulfur, sodium bicarbonate, chloride, sulfate and other major types.

**Guanziling's muddy hot springs are Taiwan's world-class signature.** Globally, only three places possess natural mud hot springs: Sicily in Italy, Kagoshima in Japan, and Guanziling in Taiwan. Guanziling's gray-black spring water, rich in minerals and fine mud particles at around 75°C, was praised during the Japanese colonial period as "the world's most miraculous spring." This mud forms through long-term interaction between limestone rock layers underground and hot spring water, possessing unique therapeutic effects.

**Green Island's Asahi Hot Spring is another terrestrial miracle.** Only three places worldwide possess underwater hot springs: northern Italy, Japan's Yakushima Island, and Taiwan's Green Island. These hot springs emerge directly from intertidal zones, allowing visitors to soak while listening to ocean waves and experiencing the world's only sunrise hot spring therapy at the ocean's edge.

> **Data Source:** According to the Ministry of Economic Affairs Geological Survey and Mining Management Agency 2024 statistics, among Taiwan's 135 natural hot springs, 52% are sodium bicarbonate springs, 31% are sulfur springs, and 17% are other special spring types. This diversity within the same land area is globally rare.

## From Zen to Entertainment: Taiwan's Hot Spring Cultural Evolution

Taiwan's hot spring culture's most enchanting aspect is "the Taiwanese evolution of Japanese genes."

Traditional Japanese hot springs emphasize the concept of "toji" (hot spring cure)—prolonged soaking to achieve therapeutic effects, pursuing pure physical and mental healing. But Taiwanese people transformed hot springs into social platforms and family entertainment centers.

Taiwanese hot spring resort halls typically operate on a grand scale, featuring not just basic hot spring pools but also spas, fine dining restaurants, karaoke, and game rooms. This "hot spring resort village" model transformed hot spring soaking from individual therapy into multi-person social activities. You can rent a private spring room as a family for intimate bonding, chat and laugh with friends in public pools, or even sing karaoke in hot spring-side private rooms.

**Private spring room culture is Taiwan's original creation.** Unlike Japanese hot springs' traditional emphasis on public bathing, Taiwan developed sophisticated private spaces—from simple indoor spring rooms to luxurious outdoor open-air baths, with prices ranging from NT$800 to NT$3,000. This design respects Taiwanese preferences for privacy while creating new options for couples' dates and family gatherings.

Beitou's Tangse Hot Spring Hall perfectly exemplifies Taiwanese hot spring characteristics. Spring pools here are categorized by function: 38°C beauty and skincare pools, 42°C stress-relief massage pools, alternating hot-cold "five layers of ice and fire" experiences. Visitors freely choose based on their needs—this customization represents the essence of Taiwanese hot springs.

Yet Taiwanese hot springs preserve healing's core value. Many hot spring areas offer professional spa treatments combining aromatherapy, massage, and beauty care, creating "hot spring+" compound experiences.

> **Curatorial Perspective:** Taiwan's hot springs reflect island society's characteristics—valuing relational connection over solitary cultivation, preferring liveliness over solitude, pragmatism over formalism.

## The Jiaoxi Phenomenon: An Urban Hot Spring Miracle on the Plains

Jiaoxi represents another innovation in Taiwan's hot spring culture: the "hot spring city."

Unlike most hot springs located in mountainous areas, Jiaoxi sits in the Yilan Plain with hot springs emerging throughout the urban area. This rare geological condition enabled Jiaoxi to develop an "urban hot spring" concept—enjoying quality hot springs without leaving convenient city amenities.

**Jiaoxi's sodium bicarbonate springs earn the nickname "beauty springs."** This colorless, odorless spring water (pH 7-8) rich in sodium, potassium, and calcium ions is gentle and non-irritating to skin. After soaking, skin becomes remarkably smooth and silky, especially beloved by female visitors. According to a 2024 internet temperature survey, Jiaoxi won the "Taiwan's Most Popular Hot Spring Area" title for three consecutive years, surpassing the historically more significant Beitou.

Tangweigou Hot Spring Park showcases Jiaoxi's democratic spirit. This free public hot spring park uses bamboo screens to create privacy, LED lighting to generate romantic ambiance, enabling everyone to experience hot spring culture without barriers. Weekend Tangweigou bustles with activity: elderly people soak their feet while chatting, couples experience fish therapy in hot spring fish pools, and families cook hot spring eggs together in designated cooking areas.

Since Hsuehshan Tunnel opened, the journey from Taipei to Jiaoxi takes merely one hour, creating a "commuter soaking" phenomenon. Weekend Jiaoxi streets overflow with Taipei license plates, hot spring hotels fully book, and many Taipei residents treat Jiaoxi as their "hot spring living room."

**Jiaoxi's success stems from democratization.** Price options abound: NT$100 public foot baths, NT$150 fish therapy sessions, NT$600 public spring rooms, NT$2,000 private wind baths, NT$8,000 luxury hot spring suites. Regardless of budget constraints, everyone finds appropriate hot spring experiences.

Yilan County Government statistics show that Jiaoxi Hot Spring Area attracted 3.5 million visitors in 2024, with hot spring-related industries generating over NT$4.5 billion in annual output value, making it Taiwan's single hot spring region with the highest economic benefit.

## Hot Spring Cuisine: Creative Geothermal Cooking Experiments

Taiwan's hot spring culture has also developed unique "geothermal cooking" innovations.

**Hot spring eggs represent the classic geothermal cooking showcase.** Utilizing 60-70°C hot spring water for extended heating, egg whites coagulate while yolks remain semi-liquid, creating special textures that, paired with Japanese soy sauce and seven-spice powder, become must-taste hot spring area common foods. In Beitou's Thermal Valley and Jiaoxi's Tangweigou geothermal cooking areas, visitors can personally cook hot spring eggs, corn, and sweet potatoes, experiencing the joy of "Earth's kitchen."

**Guanziling bamboo tube chicken is a classic hot spring cuisine.** Free-range chicken slowly roasted using natural geothermal heat develops crispy exterior and tender interior, with the unique cooking method imparting subtle mineral aromas to the chicken meat. Paired with Guanziling specialties like mountain pepper and pobu-zi (preserved bitter fruit), it creates irreplicable local flavors.

Innovative operators even experiment with "hot spring ramen"—using hot spring water for soup stock, claiming special sweet flavors. Though scientifically difficult to verify hot spring water's actual taste influence, this conceptual combination generates buzz, becoming hot spring area specialties.

**Hot spring agriculture represents another fascinating experiment.** Guanziling utilizes geothermal heat to cultivate tomatoes, while Beitou employs hot spring water to grow water spinach. Though results vary, these attempts showcase Taiwan's hot spring industry's innovative spirit while exploring new agricultural possibilities.

> **Culinary Culture Perspective:** Hot spring cuisine transcends mere gastronomy—it represents concrete practice of human-land connection. Using Earth's thermal energy for cooking represents the most ancient yet contemporaneous cooking method.

## Smart Hot Springs in the Digital Age

Technology is redefining Taiwan's hot spring experiences.

**Smart water quality monitoring systems** make soaking more reassuring. High-end hot spring resort halls in Taipei's Beitou district install real-time monitoring equipment allowing visitors to view current hot spring water pH levels, mineral content, and temperature through screens. This transparency builds consumer confidence while driving industry-wide quality improvement.

**Online reservation systems** solve hot spring peak season supply shortages. Through apps, visitors can advance-reserve spring rooms, select preferred time slots and room types, avoiding on-site waiting. During the pandemic, these contactless services proved invaluable, helping numerous operators weather difficulties.

**Themed hot springs** attract younger generations. Petal springs, milk springs, red wine springs, coffee springs—while departing from tradition—generate social media buzz. These Instagram-friendly designs attract young visitors, infusing traditional hot spring industries with renewed vitality.

**VR hot spring experiences** represent the next frontier. Some operators experiment with providing virtual reality experiences during soaking, enabling visitors to tour world-famous landscapes while bathing or experience guided meditation courses.

Smart hot springs don't aim to replace traditional experiences but rather to lower barriers, enhance quality, and expand clientele. The core remains physical and mental healing—technology simply provides better vehicles.

## Sustainability Challenges: Protecting Earth's Gift

Taiwan's hot spring industry faces critical sustainable development challenges.

**Overdevelopment poses the greatest concern.** Extensive construction in popular hot spring areas may affect natural hot spring flow rates and water quality. Nantou's Lushan Hot Springs banned all construction area-wide due to geological disasters—a sobering warning about overdevelopment. Environmental organizations estimate roughly 30% of Taiwan's hot spring areas face excessive development risks.

**Climate change** brings new threats. Extreme rainfall may affect hot spring water quality, while prolonged droughts could reduce water volume. Some hot spring areas already exhibit decreased water temperature and reduced flow volume, requiring more precise monitoring and protective measures.

Current hot spring laws have established basic development controls, yet face new industry forms (urban hot springs, hot spring bed-and-breakfasts, smart hot springs) requiring more nuanced regulations. Particularly, hot spring development in Indigenous areas requires balancing economic development with cultural preservation.

Japan, Korea, and Iceland actively promote hot spring tourism; Taiwan must enhance international competitiveness while maintaining quality. Developing ecological hot springs, cultural hot springs, and health-focused hot springs as differentiation strategies may provide breakthrough pathways.

> **Sustainability Thinking:** Hot springs are Earth's gift to Taiwan—we must learn cherished use rather than wasteful consumption. Future generations equally deserve rights to enjoy this warmth from Earth's core.

## Hot Spring Etiquette: Balancing Modern and Traditional

When experiencing Taiwan's hot spring culture, basic etiquette remains important.

Thoroughly shower before entering pools—this shows basic respect for water quality maintenance and fellow visitors. Use soap to completely cleanse your body, ensuring personal hygiene. Control soaking duration, with 10-15 minute sessions followed by rest recommended, avoiding excessive soaking causing physical discomfort. Should you experience dizziness, heart palpitations, or other discomfort, immediately leave the hot spring pool for shaded rest areas.

Maintain quiet, avoiding loud conversation disturbing others. Hot springs are healing spaces; quiet environments facilitate physical and mental relaxation. Silence phones, avoiding poolside phone conversations.

Respect facility regulations, including swimwear requirements and towel policies. Private spring rooms have time limits; please return punctually, enabling smooth use for subsequent guests.

Taiwan's hot spring culture evolved from accidental military medical needs a century ago into today's multifaceted life experiences combining healing, leisure, and socializing. Through this process, we preserved Japanese hot spring refinement while integrating Taiwanese creativity and human warmth, creating the world's unique "Taiwanese hot spring civilization."

When you next soak in hot springs, feel not merely geothermal warmth but also the healing symphony composed through a century of cultural fusion, geological blessings, and humanistic innovation—this represents Taiwan's hot spring culture's true value.
