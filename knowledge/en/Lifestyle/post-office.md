> **30-Second Overview:** On March 22, 1888, Liu Ming-cheung established the Taiwan Post Office Bureau in Taipei Prefecture City, issuing stamps and beginning to accept private mail. Subsequently, the postal system passed through Japanese administration, post-war takeover, the revival of postal savings, and corporatization in 2003; the post office seems merely a place for sending letters, but it actually preserves traces of national governance, financial services, and local mutual aid simultaneously.

![Exterior view of Taipei Beimen Post Office, Wikimedia Commons hotlink](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8d/Taipei_Taiwan_Beimen-Post-Office-01.jpg)

_Image source: [Wikimedia Commons file page: Taipei Taiwan Beimen-Post-Office-01.jpg](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taipei_Taiwan_Beimen-Post-Office-01.jpg); Author: CEphoto, Uwe Aranas; License: CC BY-SA 3.0._

Taiwanese people rarely regard the post office as a historical site. It usually appears within a more urgent itinerary: sending a document, collecting money, buying stamps, applying for insurance, or waiting with a numbered ticket at the counter. However, this seemingly ordinary place opened in Taipei Prefecture City on **March 22, 1888**. Liu Ming-cheung promulgated sixteen articles of the "Taiwan Postal Regulations," establishing the Taiwan Post Office Bureau and issuing stamps, which also "accepted private mail."[^1]

There is an easily overlooked contrast in this starting point: modern postal systems in China are often marked by the establishment of the Grand Qing Post Office Agency on **March 20, 1896**, yet the new postal system in Taiwan appeared eight years earlier. This is not a competition to rank two historical periods, but a reminder: Taiwan's postal history was connected from the beginning to local governance, imperial systems, and cross-strait communication. [^1]

## Not Convenience, But Nationhood Encountered First in a Letter

The 1888 Post Office Bureau was not the green sign familiar today. It was a new system: it had stamps, rules for acceptance, and a counter that integrated private correspondence into official order. The post office allowed people far away to send letters, but it also enabled the nation to know more stably who was where, how they were moving, and which areas were included in the same set of regulations.

After Japan took control of Taiwan in 1895, a field postal service was first established. A regular postal system followed the next year. Academic research describes this institutional establishment as an engineering project where "civilization and enlightenment" advanced alongside colonial rule: the Governor's Office introduced Japanese domestic laws and management methods, while simultaneously establishing third-class post offices and post collection points locally to deepen the reach of the post office into daily life.[^2] Thus, the post office was not just a symbol of modernization but also a tool used by the colonial government to incorporate the island into its administrative network.

The postal system in the early Japanese period was not smooth. Research indicates that conditions such as transportation and sanitation, mail robbery, competition from private agencies and foreign shipping companies, and insufficient numbers and capabilities of postal staff required constant repair of the system. The public was also not accustomed to using it initially: official statistics show that in the early civil administration period, the proportion of mail usage was less than one percent, only later increasing to about 30%, with parcels at around 25%. [^2]

Behind these numbers is a specific person. Diaries from local gentry Zhang Li-chun (張麗俊) in Fengyuan show that the postal system gradually became integrated into the daily life network: writing letters, mail shopping, correspondence with literati in other regions, and even reaching Japanese territories in Asia. The post office was not "used" by the public after the completion of a system. It slowly became part of life through one letter and one parcel.[^2]

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The modernization of the postal service was not a road paved from top to bottom. It had to pass through local transportation, public distrust, and existing communication methods before it became what we take for granted today: "if you send it, it will arrive."

## Teaching an Unfamiliar System into Daily Habit

For the post office to become widespread, simply building a post office was not enough. Research from the early Japanese period indicates that the Governor's Office improved delivery procedures and taught people how to use this new communication system through official propaganda and correspondence education in textbooks.[^2]

This detail is important. A modern system is not naturally accepted just because it was designed. It requires a new life grammar: how to write an envelope, where to affix a stamp, what is worth sending, and how long is reasonable to wait. Only when these grammars entered schools and homes did the post office cease to be merely an official agency and become a predictable part of people's daily routines.[^2]

The postal service also made "far away" more concrete. The correspondence of local gentry Zhang Li-chun connected local life, mail shopping, literati exchanges, and Japanese territories in Asia.[^2] The value of a letter was not just in its content but also in the timetable it opened up for someone in one place relative to another.

Commemorative stamps, postcards, and special postmarks added an element of collectible interest to the postal service. Research abstracts point out that these items promoted philatelic interests across Taiwan. The post office thus served as both a communication gateway and a place where people observed the island, remembered events, and exchanged interests.[^2]

Today we view the postal service as a technology for "getting things delivered," but history reminds us that it was also a social engineering effort to "teach people how to maintain relationships with those who are absent."

## Stamps Are Small, Yet They Affix an Era to an Envelope

The stamps reduced the nation into a small piece of paper affixed to an envelope. Summaries from exhibitions on Taiwan's postal history at the British Philatelic Society cover everything from Imperial Courier Post to the French naval blockade in 1884–85, the elevation of Taiwan to a province and postal reform in 1885, through the cession to Japan in 1895, and the period using Japanese overprinted stamps in 1945.[^3]

![Taiwan postage stamp from 1888, 10 cents overprinted on 20 cents, Wikimedia Commons hotlink](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/27/Taiwan_%28Formosa%29_postage_stamp_1888_-_10_cents_on_20_cents.jpg)

_Image source: [Wikimedia Commons file page: Taiwan (Formosa) postage stamp 1888 – 10 cents on 20 cents](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwan_(Formosa)_postage_stamp_1888_-_10_cents_on_20_cents.jpg; Image tagged as Public domain._

These collections are not a complete timeline of Taiwan's postal history, but they reveal the material nature of postal history: the system exists not only in regulations and institutional names but also on paper, overprints, postmarks, and envelopes that have been sent. The image printed on the stamp, the script used, or the place name stamped often tells people more directly than an abstract historical narrative: which regime was managing this letter at this moment.

The official collection page of the Taiwan Post Office Museum also lists categories such as Cloud Dragon stamps, Empress Dowager Cixi anniversary commemorative stamps, Tiger stamps, Taiwan postage and postal commercial stamps, Red overprinted provisional stamps, and "Temporary Neutral" stamps.[^4] Stamp collectors are not just pursuing images; they are tracing how paper passed through regime changes, wars, transportation routes, and communication regulations, finally being preserved as a slice of time that can be re-examined.

## The Post Office Did Not Just Send Letters; It Also Held Money for the Nation

Perhaps what truly changed daily life in postal history was not the letters, but the savings. During the Japanese administration, postal services expanded from mail delivery to deposits, transfers, currency exchange, telegrams, telephones, and simple insurance. Researchers therefore summarized it as having multifaceted, colonial, and modern operating aspects.[^5]

The post-war takeover did not sever this function from Taiwanese life. Historical records in the National Cultural Memory Bank state that although the Postal Savings Agency was relocated to Taiwan at the end of 1949 and subsequently dissolved, related operations were continued by the post office without interruption. The Postal Savings Agency resumed operations on June 1, 1962.[^6]

The postal savings after resumption were not just another bank deposit sign. Historical records state that savings could not be directly disbursed but had to be managed by the Central Bank and invested in long-term capital for power generation, transportation, industry, agriculture, public housing, and small businesses. In other words, the money many people deposited into a post office passbook was systematically directed toward Taiwan's post-war infrastructure.[^6]

This gave the post office a second contrast: on an individual level, it seems small—a passbook, a transfer, an insurance policy. On a national level, however, it connected scattered savings to the pipeline of public construction. The post office did not just hold people's money; it once turned "small-scale, dispersed, and stable" into usable capital for the nation.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The story of postal savings cannot be told merely as "conveniently allowing citizens to save." It must also ask: who concentrated these small savings, under what financial rules were they constrained, and which public projects were they directed toward? The post office counter and national capital allocation actually met within the same system.

## After 2003, the Post Office Is Still Not an Ordinary Company

On January 1, 2003, the Postal Bureau of the Ministry of Transportation was reorganized into China Post Co., Ltd., changing its organizational form from a government agency to a state-owned enterprise.[^1] The name gained "Co., Ltd.," but this does not mean the postal service withdrew from public service.

Article 2 of the current Postal Law stipulates that the competent authority is the Ministry of Transportation. Article 3 states directly: "The Ministry of Transportation establishes China Post Co., Ltd. to provide postal services."[^7] This sentence places both sides of the company on one line: it must operate, and it was established to provide postal services. The post office faces competition from the market, efficiency, and digital communication, yet it cannot simply keep only the most profitable locations.

The name itself has become a political stage. In 2007, the government of Chen Shui-bian attempted to change the name from "China Post" to "Taiwan Post." A commentary by the National Policy Research Foundation at the time suggested that the name change involved legal amendments and administrative authority issues. This was the policy stance of that foundation, not a neutral legal judgment, but it did leave an important clue: the post office sign is not just for identification; it is also used to discuss national names, historical memory, and who has the right to name things.[^9]

The later restoration of the name to "China Post" did not erase this debate. Instead, it showed that the public nature of the postal service has two layers: one layer is the daily service of sending letters, collecting money, and buying stamps. The other layer is under which national name these services operate. The former allows the system to be used; the latter subjects the system to debate.

English reports from Taiwan Panorama documented how this transformation manifested in specific spaces. The Ren'ai Road Post Office in Taipei lowered its counter height, added interactive computers, and implemented a numbering system and seating areas. The Fangshan Post Office in Pingtung was transformed into a giant mailbox, with onion, mango, and local bird images on the exterior walls, and solar panels installed on the roof.[^8] This was not turning the post office into a theme park, but an attempt to answer a practical question: if people no longer write letters every day, will they still be willing to walk in?

The same report also pointed out that there are postal branches in Taiwanese cities, towns, and outlying islands, and mail carriers sometimes deliver rice, medicine, or care packages to elderly individuals during mail delivery. These were service examples in the report, not meaning every route involved a carrier providing care. But it made "universal services" more than an abstract word in regulations; it was the action of one person cycling to another household's door.[^8]

## How One Counter Passed Through Different Names

The continuity of the post office does not mean there was no rupture in history. The Taiwan Post Office Bureau in 1888 was replaced by the Japanese postal system after 1895. The Post Office Bureau, relocated to Taiwan in 1949, rearranged its position within the post-war system. In 2003, it changed from a government agency to a state-owned company. Different regimes, different regulations, and different names have all redefined who the post office should serve and how to manage people and money.[^1] [^2] [^5]

But for most people, history is usually not reached by institutional names. It might be a stamp sent from another region, a date stamp affixed at the post office, a passbook that has accompanied a family for many years, or perhaps the mail carrier remembering which door an elderly person lives behind.

Therefore, what is most worth writing about in the post office is not the statistic "Taiwan has many post offices," but how it brings together several things that are usually separate: national governance and private correspondence, savings and public construction, efficiency reform and local care, and stamp collecting and regime memory. It seems unchanged to Taiwan, yet with every institutional renaming, every financial shift, and every letter delivered, it incorporates change into the daily life of Taiwan.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The post office is not important because it is old; it is important because it turns "the nation" into a place where one can walk in, take a number ticket, and hand over a letter. Regimes may change names, but this action still needs someone to complete it.
