> **30-Second Overview:** During the morning rush hour, the underpass approach of Taipei Bridge, which connects Sanchong District in New Taipei City and Datong District in Taipei City, sees tens of thousands of densely packed motor scoopers pouring down the slope, creating the internationally stunning "Scooter Waterfall." This unique landscape, captured multiple times by foreign media and photography competitions, is not only the ultimate manifestation of Taiwan's motor scooter density of 14 million vehicles, but also profoundly reflects the housing price gap in the Taipei-New Taipei metropolitan area, the survival momentum of cross-river commuters, and the unique traffic resilience developed by Taiwan's cities in the process of modernization.

If you are looking for an image in Taiwan that can instantly condense the visual impact of "grassroots economy and collective labor," the motor scooter approach of Taipei Bridge at 7:30 AM is definitely the first choice[^10]. The moment the traffic light turns green, the engine sounds of tens of thousands of motor scooters intertwine into a low hum. Riders wearing raincoats or windbreakers of various colors resemble an airtight river of steel, pouring from the Sanchong end down the elevated approach toward downtown Taipei. This spectacular sense of flow has been vividly named the "Scooter Waterfall" by photographers and media at home and abroad[^11].

This scene has not only appeared multiple times on the pages of international news and in the selected images of National Geographic photography competitions, but has also become the best slice for foreigners to understand contemporary urban life in Taiwan[^12]. However, behind this astonishing visual spectacle lies Taiwan's urban development trajectory over the past few decades, the dilemma of housing justice, and the collective footprints of the working class seeking survival in the concrete jungle.

## Evolution of a Century-Old Traffic Artery: From Wooden Bridges to a Dragon of Motor Scooters

The history of Taipei Bridge (commonly known as Taipei Bridge) can be traced back to the Japanese colonial period or even the Qing Dynasty. As a vital thoroughfare crossing the Tamsui River and connecting the core of the Taipei Basin with satellite towns to the west (such as Luzhou, Sanchong, and Xinzhuang), its fate has always been closely linked to the modernization of Taiwan's transportation[^10].

In the early days, the Taipei Bridge carried hand carts, pedestrians, ox carts, and early bicycles. With the post-war economic takeoff in Taiwan and the rapid concentration of population in the Taipei metropolitan area, the demand for cross-river commuting exploded exponentially. After the local assembly of motor scooters began in Taiwan in the 1960s, motor scooters quickly became the most affordable and highly mobile civilian vehicle for ordinary families[^1]. By the 1990s, to accommodate the massive cross-river traffic flow, the government expanded the lanes and elevated approaches of Taipei Bridge, but inadvertently created a bottleneck with excellent slope and viewing angles at the Chongqing North Road end—when a large number of motor scooters gathered on the elevated approach and waited to cross the bridge, the high-angle perspective created a visual illusion of vertical drop for the "sea of vehicles," creating the world-famous waterfall spectacle.

## The Push of Housing Prices and Cross-River Commuting: Sociological Causes of the Scooter Waterfall

Why do so many people have to ride across the Tamsui River bridges every morning? The answer lies in the stark differences in the real estate market and housing costs between Taipei and New Taipei.

Statistics show that the total number of registered motor scooters in Taiwan has long exceeded 14 million, with an average of nearly 700 motor scooters per 1,000 people, making it one of the places with the highest motor scooter density in the world[^2]. In the Greater Taipei metropolitan area, areas such as Sanchong, Luzhou, and Xinzhuang, due to earlier development, complete living functions, and relatively more advantageous prices compared to the core of Taipei City, have long become the first stop for countless "drifters" (young people moving to the city for work) and young families.

However, when job opportunities are concentrated in the core commercial districts of Taipei City such as Zhongshan, Zhongzheng, and Xinyi, while residences are on the other side of the Tamsui River, cross-river transportation becomes a daily ordeal. Although the Taipei Metro system (such as the Zhonghe-Xinlu Line) provides rail transit options, for many workers in field services, the service industry, small and medium-sized enterprises, or those seeking to save on transfer times and high rent, motor scooters remain the lowest-cost, most flexible "last-mile" solution. Each "drop" of the scooter waterfall is actually a commuter soul calculating carefully for a living.

## A Spectacle in a Global Perspective: When Foreign Media Lenses Focus on Taipei Bridge

The "Scooter Waterfall" has sparked heated discussion globally in recent years through the lenses of foreign media and photographers. International mainstream media such as _The Guardian_, _The New York Times_, and Reuters have multiple times featured this unique urban landscape in special reports[^12].

In the eyes of many Western urban planners, the motor scooter river at Taipei Bridge was initially seen as a symbol of a "traffic nightmare"—dense carbon emissions, potential traffic accident risks, and a challenge to car-centric thinking. However, as reports deepened, foreign media gradually saw the astonishing sense of order displayed by these scooter riders. Without excessive intervention, tens of thousands of riders demonstrated a high degree of tacit understanding on the narrow approach: starting simultaneously when the green light lit up, braking neatly before red lights, and yielding lanes to each other. This traffic operating order shaped by grassroots tacit understanding amazed many Western metropolises accustomed to orderly but somewhat cold environments.

Many foreign backpackers visiting Taiwan even specially go to the pedestrian overpass under Taipei Bridge at 7:00 AM, just to witness this commute ritual hailed as "the most shocking on the earth."

## The Growing Pains of Traffic Transformation: Struggling Between Electrification and Human-Centric Traffic

However, standing at the crossroads of contemporary urban governance, the traditional motor scooter culture symbolized by the scooter waterfall is facing a comprehensive test of transformation.

As Taiwan moves toward the goal of net-zero carbon emissions, improving air quality and human-centric traffic (such as enhancing pedestrian rights and expanding bus-only lanes) have become core issues in urban planning. In recent years, the government has actively promoted subsidies for electric motor scooters, phase-out mechanisms for fuel-powered motor scooters, and has attempted to add or optimize motor scooter lanes and diversion guides on cross-river bridges to reduce accident rates and reduce exhaust gas retention.

But transformation is not easy. How to improve traffic safety and environmental quality while guaranteeing the right of low-cost commuting for grassroots laborers remains a sustainable puzzle that the traffic authorities of both Taipei and New Taipei cities constantly tug at. The scooter waterfall is not just a byproduct of the economic miracle of the past few decades; it will continue to find a new balance point in the future wave of green transformation.

## Conclusion: The Flowing Image of Taiwan

Every morning, when the first ray of dawn spills on the surface of the Tamsui River, the Taipei Bridge approach welcomes that familiar river of steel on time. It has no flashy slogans and no precise rituals, only the solid feeling of countless Taiwanese pressing the throttle for their livelihoods.

The scooter waterfall is the concrete embodiment of the vitality of the common people in Taiwan, the most authentic pulse in the Taipei Basin. As long as the housing costs and labor structure of this city do not change, this waterfall woven by motor scooters and helmets will continue to flow endlessly in the morning light, showing the world the resilient and vibrant daily scenery of the Taiwanese people.
