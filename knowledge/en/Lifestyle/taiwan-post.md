> **30-second overview:** In 1888, Liu Ming-chuan established the Taiwan Postal Administration in Taipei Prefecture. After 1895, the postal service was incorporated into Japan's colonial transportation and telecommunications network. Today's Chunghwa Post simultaneously delivers mail, manages savings, sells insurance, handles logistics, and reinvents itself through 24-hour iPost boxes and mobile post offices. This article argues that the post office's most important function has never been merely delivering letters, but giving a distant person, a small deposit, or a public service the chance to reach the specific individual at that address.

## A Letter Without a Street Address

![Red and green post boxes on a Taipei street.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c1/Taipei_Taiwan_Post-boxes-01.jpg)

_Image: CEphoto, Uwe Aranas／[Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taipei_Taiwan_Post-boxes-01.jpg), CC BY-SA 3.0._

In 2015, the Yunlin Shuilin Post Office received a letter mailed from Taoyuan. The envelope bore only a postal code and the words "Miss Lin," with no address. By regulation this letter could have become "dead mail," but postal worker Lin Hung-chi judged from the handwriting that the sender was young, and recalled a young woman surnamed Lin in his delivery zone who had married into the area from Taoyuan. He attempted delivery based on memory and found the recipient on the first try.[^7]

A few years earlier, another letter from Yokohama, Japan also lacked a street number, leaving only an English road name and a name. Douliu postal worker Lee Shang-hsuan first checked the phone directory, then went door-to-door in the delivery sector comparing envelopes, and finally spent over ten hours finding the person on Facebook. The letter contained the recipient's employment certificate from their time working in Japan; the sender's omitted street number nearly left a life chapter stuck in the post office's return process.[^7]

These stories read like cinema, yet they are not the postal system's standard procedures. They reveal the layer behind standard procedures: an address is a rule on paper, but the letter carrier uses familiarity with place to translate rules into "I know where this person lives."

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The most moving aspect of the post is not the four characters "mission accomplished," but that when an address fails, someone is still willing to treat a name as a clue rather than an error.

## Sixteen Articles in Taipei Prefecture

On February 21, 1888, Taiwan's first Governor Liu Ming-chuan issued the "Taiwan Postal Regulations" in sixteen articles. One month later on March 22, the Taiwan Postal Administration was established in Taipei Prefecture, issuing stamps and accepting civilian mail. Chunghwa Post officially regards this history as the starting point of Taiwan's modern postal system, but also specifically notes that this innovation did not extend to the mainland territories under Qing rule at the time.[^1] [^2]

Here lies a contrast easily obscured by timeline tables. Taiwan's modern postal imagination did not arrive from a complete state apparatus descending from above, but began as a system trialed by a local governor within Taipei City. Eight years later, on March 20, 1896, the Qing court formally approved the Imperial Chinese Post based on regulations drafted by Robert Hart. The Postal Museum regards this day as the date when the modern state-run postal service obtained legal status.[^1] [^3]

Therefore, 1888 and 1896 are not two ways of writing the same event. The former is the modern postal service Liu Ming-chuan established in Taiwan; the latter is the legal beginning of the modern postal service confirmed by the Qing court at a national scale. Compressing both into "postal service began in a certain year" erases Taiwan's special position in institutional history.

## Beside the Post Box: Telecommunications and Savings

After Japanese troops landed in Taiwan in 1895, they first established field post offices in occupied territories. The Taiwan Historical Culture Map of Academia Sinica records that 23 such field post offices were established in succession. In April 1896, the military post was abolished and ordinary postal service continued; post and telegraph were merged into the Post and Telegraph Bureau. By 1902, postal-telegraph institutions were divided into four tiers: first-class, second-class, third-class, and branch offices.[^4]

This was not merely a change of signage. For the postal service to operate on mountain roads, in ports, and in newly established streets and villages, it had to establish layers of acceptance, dispatch, delivery, telegraphy, and personnel management. Based on the Taiwan Fort Maps and related historical materials, Academia Sinica compiled 92 postal-telegraph institutions, of which 52 were Post and Telegraph Bureaus, with additional post offices, telegraph offices, and various forms of branch offices. The small symbols on the map are in fact a diagram of how the colonial government divided space into manageable nodes.[^4]

From Chunghwa Post's official organizational history and postal films preserved in the National Cultural Memory Bank, one can see this system did not serve only the rulers. It simultaneously developed savings, remittance, insurance, and telephone services; after the war it incorporated mechanization, rural postal affairs, and postal savings into construction plans. The post office thus entered local life, becoming the same window for mailing letters, saving money, sending remittances, and awaiting news.[^1] [^6]

In other words, the post office was both the conduit through which the state sent out orders, and the conduit through which ordinary people sent family letters, goods, and money into their lives. The simultaneous existence of these two directions is the first contradiction the postal service left in Taiwan.

## 1968: Machines Begin Handling Letters

The National Cultural Memory Bank preserves a film titled "Today's Postal Service" screened in 1968. The footage captures the exterior of Taipei Post Office, staff processing mail, and letter carriers departing on motorcycles to deliver letters. The narration describes the postal service as one of the public utilities most closely connected to people's lives. At the time, Taiwan already had 7,218 postal institutions at various levels, and the postal department was listing mechanization, postal savings, and rural postal affairs as construction goals.[^6]

The machines in the film were not mere efficiency tools. On March 20, 1970, the postal service began implementing postal codes. In November of the same year, an automatic electronic letter sorting machine purchased from Japan arrived in Taipei, completing the final step of mail processing mechanization. The few digits on an envelope thus became the common language between human sorting and machine recognition.[^6]

The Postal Museum's "Postal Chronicle" exhibition places this history alongside post boxes, basket carts, mail transport tool models, and sorting equipment. Postal modernization occurred not only in regulations, but in every action of a letter being accepted at the window, cancelled, dispatched, transported, and finally handed to the delivery worker.[^3]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** A postal code looks like cold numbers, but behind it lies the moment when Taiwan entrusted "I know where you live" to both humans and machines to complete together.

## The People in Green Become the Eyes of the Place

A letter carrier's work appears to be a fixed route, but in practice accumulates a kind of local knowledge. Yunlin Shuilin letter carrier Wu Chen-hsiung says: "The letter carrier always comes to ring the bell." In villages without doorbells, he must knock first, then call out "Anyone home!" Over time, elderly residents ask him to mail letters, pick up medicine, or buy lunch boxes on their behalf. This passage appears in local reports compiled by the Chunghwa Post Yunlin Post Office, preserving the local tone verbatim.[^7]

This familiarity can also function in critical moments. Official compiled reports record postal workers discovering elderly residents who had not appeared for days while delivering mail, and checking on them. Another letter carrier discovered a collapsed woman on the delivery route, helped call an ambulance, and visited her at the hospital. These are not delivery steps required by postal regulations, but the sensitivity to "why haven't I seen this person today" accumulated over years of fixed routes.[^7]

Starting in 2008, Chunghwa Post encouraged staff to check on solitary elderly during delivery rounds. In official compiled Yunlin cases, postal workers would note whether elderly residents appeared on their fixed routes, assist with reporting, and even help handle daily postal matters beyond delivery. These stories cannot represent every letter carrier route, but they illustrate how the carrier's route was institutionally enlisted to catch gaps in local society.[^7]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** When a letter carrier is remembered by the elderly as "the person who comes five days a week," the post office is no longer just a delivery system. It also becomes an expected visit in rural life.

## A Company Still Carrying Universal Service

In 1949, the Directorate General of Posts and the Postal Savings and Remittance Bureau relocated to Taiwan. On January 1, 2003, the Directorate General of Posts was reorganized into Chunghwa Post Co., Ltd., 100% owned by the Ministry of Transportation and Communications. The corporate form changed, but postal affairs, savings, remittance, simple life insurance, and philately remained in the same service network.[^1] [^2]

This "three-in-one operation" makes the post office unlike a courier company doing only one business, or a bank handling only deposits. It must face private delivery and financial competition in metropolitan areas while maintaining universal service in outlying islands, mountain areas, and small towns. Chiayi Post Office local historical materials show that Chiayi was designated as a main mail station in 1888, and the old post office building completed in 1939 was designated a historic building in 2004. What the post office left in the locality is both a service base and the city's material memory.[^6]

Chunghwa Post's own corporate profile acknowledges that market and digital trends have changed postal operating conditions. On one hand the company maintains postal affairs, savings/remittance, and life insurance; on the other it promotes smart logistics, digital finance, and AI fraud prevention. The question is not only "whether to digitize," but whether a state-owned system bearing universal service can simultaneously achieve low thresholds and high speed.[^1]

## A Post Office Is Also a Map

The post office exists not only in the letter carrier's route, but in the city's streetscape. The Chiayi Post Office was completed in 1939, originally a first-class bureau during the Japanese colonial period. Today it is preserved as a historic building, with its original interior spaces largely intact. The value of this architectural preservation lies not only in whether the facade looks good, but in how it once concentrated acceptance, dispatch, delivery, and local administration at a single address.[^6]

Taipei's postal history similarly overlaps with urban space. Liu Ming-chuan planned the Taipei Postal Administration; during the Japanese colonial period post and telegraph were merged; after the war they were managed separately. The post office thus always simultaneously connects streets, power lines, motorcycles, counters, and waiting people.[^1] [^2] [^5]

When a post office is preserved, what is preserved is actually a scale of public life: people need not first become large enterprises or understand financial terminology; they need only walk through the door to mail letters, send remittances, save money, or ask a question. This is also where post office architecture differs from ordinary commercial storefronts.

## Another Kind of Arrival in the Passbook

The post office is often remembered as "the place that sends letters," but savings, remittance, and simple life insurance have always been important parts of it. Official materials list postal affairs, savings, and life insurance as core businesses, and note that the post office network extends throughout townships, urban districts, and outlying islands. For many people, what the post office delivers is not distant news, but financial services available nearby.[^1]

This function continues to change today. A 2023 Ministry of Transportation and Communications press release shows that Chunghwa Post cooperated with the Taiwan Depository & Clearing Corporation to allow postal depositors to query postal account balances, transaction details, and time deposit balances through mobile post office authorization. Financial services no longer remain only in passbooks and counters, but begin connecting with other institutions' apps.[^9]

This extension simultaneously brings a question that must be preserved: digital finance makes things faster for those familiar with smartphones, yet cannot assume everyone can equally smoothly log in, authorize, and identify risks. The post office's public nature ultimately returns to whether it is willing to preserve a path where those unfamiliar with new tools can ask clearly.

## From Letter Carrier Routes to iPost Boxes

In 2021, Chunghwa Post launched a more comprehensive digital transformation, proposing three goals: improving operational efficiency, optimizing customer experience, and innovating business models. iThome reported that the new Mobile Post Office App launched in 2022 exceeded 5.4 million downloads in just over a year. The iPost Box launched in 2016 extended parcel mailing and pickup hours from counter business hours to 24 hours.[^8]

Transformation does not mean driving everyone into phones. A 2023 Ministry of Transportation and Communications press release shows that postal depositors can authorize through the Mobile Post Office to use the TDCC e-Handhold to query passbook savings balances, transaction details, and time deposit balances. The post office still does finance, but the entrance has extended from the counter to between different institutions' apps.[^9]

In 2025, Chunghwa Post announced iPost Boxes exceeded 2,400 units, and listed cross-border B2C mail, free trade port zone cargo transshipment to mail export, and international e-packets as smart logistics directions. These services make the post office more like a logistics node, and let the word "postal" leave the envelope and enter e-commerce, cross-border trade, and self-service pickup.[^10]

But the costs and limits of transformation are also clear. When the post office must simultaneously maintain nationwide postal affairs, savings/remittance, and life insurance services while introducing smart logistics and digital finance, efficiency, universal service, and usage thresholds cannot be solved by a single app alone. New machines can accelerate certain processes, but cannot automatically eliminate every friction left by old institutions.[^1]

In an iThome interview with Chunghwa Post Deputy President Chen Tung-liang, he said: "Facing challenges brought by macro-environment trends, if the post office still uses PC and physical location operating models without changing, it will eventually be eliminated sooner or later."[^8] This sounds like a tech transformation manifesto, but placed back in postal context, it reads more like a dilemma: the post office must become faster, but cannot leave behind those who need counters, need paper, need a person to explain.

Official practice in recent years has not been to withdraw counters entirely, but to split services into different entrances: iPost Boxes handle parcels, Mobile Post Office handles accounts and insurance, post office counters continue to handle matters requiring face-to-face verification. Whether this diversion can truly reduce frontline pressure still depends on whether systems are integrated, data interoperable, and those unfamiliar with digital tools have been left behind.[^1] [^9]

## Who the Address Is Ultimately Written For

From the sixteen articles of Taipei Prefecture, to the 1970 postal codes, to today's iPost Boxes, the postal service has continuously changed the technology of "arrival." The Liu Ming-chuan era post office first had to establish rules; the Japanese colonial Post and Telegraph Bureau coded localities into the administrative network; postwar mechanization allowed mass letter sorting; digital services moved parcels and accounts into phones and self-service machines.[^1] [^4] [^5] [^10]

Every change made the system more efficient, and every change re-decided which people are easily served and which people need to walk an extra stretch of road. The letter without a street address could be delivered not because technology was lacking, but because a postal worker was willing to supplement the institutional gap with local experience. The solitary elderly know someone will come ring the bell not because the post office is inherently gentle, but because a fixed route stacked human time upon human time.

The post office looks old-fashioned precisely because it places state service into the smallest unit of daily life: an address, a post box, a passbook, a single ring of the bell. The real problem it faces is not whether letters will disappear, but whether after everything can be done by app, Taiwan still wants to preserve someone who can walk to your doorstep and know who is missing today.
