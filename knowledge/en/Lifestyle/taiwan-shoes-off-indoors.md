# Taking Shoes Off Upon Entering Taiwan: No Genkan, But Still a Boundary of Home

> **30-Second Overview:** In Taiwan, leaving outdoor shoes at the entrance when entering a home is often unnecessary to remind anyone; however, "taking shoes off upon entry" is not a rigid rule in every household, but rather a living script adjusted according to housing layout, room function, flooring material, and family history. It is influenced by Japanese residences and the concept of the _genkan_ during the Japanese colonial period, and it has re-evolved amidst post-war housing changes.[^1][^2][^9] After reading this article, you will see how tatami mats, religious venues, schools, and exhibition spaces assign different meanings to the same action, and you will see a Taiwanese entrance boundary—one without fixed height differences, perhaps drawn only by shoe cabinets, tiles, or a spoken word from the host.[^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

For venues, taking shoes off might also be designed to protect tatami mats, maintain quietness, or complement sound and tactile exhibitions.[^4][^5][^6][^7][^8]

## The First Boundary at the Entrance: Is This Indoors?

In many Taiwanese homes, after opening the door, one may not see the clearly sunken _genkan_ characteristic of Japanese residences. Guests might first step on a small section of tile before seeing a shoe cabinet. Or perhaps the living room is immediately visible upon entering. Some people remove shoes outside the door, some stand at the threshold to change shoes, while some families only require that outdoor shoes not be worn in bedrooms, _washitsu_ (Japanese-style rooms), or rooms with wooden floors.

This is where the phrase "Taiwanese people must take off their shoes upon entering" often becomes distorted. It describes a high-frequency life expectation, not a rigid rule identical in every household. Life observations also record differences between Taiwanese families: some change outdoor shoes for indoor slippers, some only change shoes in specific rooms, and some still maintain the practice of walking in outdoor shoes at their ancestral homes.[^2]

The Japanese _genkan_ incorporated shoe removal into the residential structure. The entrance to a Japanese residence often clearly demarcates the exterior from the interior using earthen floors (_doma_), thresholds, and differences in floor levels. This structure serves the functions of storing shoes, sitting down to put on shoes, and blocking dust.[^3] Taiwan may not replicate the same height difference, but it retains the act of "changing shoes" and reincorporates it into tiles, shoe cabinets, covered walkways (_kikura_), or narrow entry traffic flows.

The boundary in Taiwanese residences is therefore often completed by a few small items: shoe cabinets concentrate shoes, tiles signal "not yet in the living room," and indoor slippers extend the act of wearing shoes indoors. These items do not necessarily appear together, nor is there a fixed configuration. Housing studies and life observations both point to the entrance space being re-used according to family traffic flow.[^1][^9]

> **29 Cases of Modern Residences in Taiwan and Japan:** Housing research explains that the _genkan_ is not merely a decorative term but a living space where shoe removal, storage, and public/private demarcation intersect through comparison, interviews, and observation.[^1]

## It Was Not Always This Way: The Housing History of Genkan and Shoe Removal

Ko Yu-chun's research on the housing types in Taiwan and Japan reviewed residential characteristics around 1945, conducted fieldwork, interviews, and observations, comparing 29 modern residence cases from both Taiwan and Japan. The study summary indicated that the entrance spaces of traditional Taiwanese courtyard houses and townhouses were not the same as the _genkan_ later found in Japanese-style residences. It was only when Japanese-style residences appeared in Taiwan during the colonial period that the concept of the entrance as a demarcation between public and private domains, and the practice of shoe removal, clearly entered domestic life.[^1]

The significance of this conclusion is that it does not support the simple narrative that "ancient Taiwanese people always removed their shoes at the door." Instead, it suggests to us that **space teaches how to act, and long-term practices in turn demand changes in space.** The research explains this interaction clearly: the _genkan_, a space for entry, reception, and passage, gradually gained storage and buffering functions, which residents then re-used according to their own life flows.[^1]

The Story Museum of Zhongping Road in Taoyuan is a specific place where this history can be seen. This Japanese double-occupancy civil servant dormitory built in 1930 retains Japanese spatial characteristics such as tatami mats. Today, when open for historical tours, official tourism information requires visitors to remove shoes and wear socks.[^4] The shoe removal here is not an abstract "Taiwanese custom," but an action produced by building materials, preservation methods, and visiting rules combined.

## Indoor Slippers: A Compromise Between Cleanliness and Awkwardness

What is common at Taiwanese entrances is not bare feet, but another pair of shoes. Outdoor shoes are left outside; guests wear communal slippers; family members wear their own indoor slippers. Different pairs of shoes divide the outdoor area, the living room, the bathroom, the balcony, and even the bedroom into several non-identical zones.

Indoor slippers solve a practical problem: tile floors can be cold, damp, or require keeping the soles clean. They also create another small social awkwardness: is the guest willing to wear communal slippers? Should one worry about odors after taking shoes off? Are the slippers for guests only, or are they acceptable if worn by the previous guest?

The life observation in _Crossing_ writes this contradiction very directly: in Taiwan, outdoor shoes are seen as bringing dirt and grime inside. But from another lived experience, communal slippers themselves can be viewed as a hygiene concern.[^2]

This is not a question of who is more civilized. It is where two imaginations of "cleanliness" meet: one views the soles as a source of pollution, while the other views bare feet or shared slippers as an uncomfortable source. Climate, flooring material, family habits, and bodily sensations all combine to determine which set of rules is ultimately adopted.[^2]

| ---------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Outdoor shoes left at the entrance | Reduces bringing outdoor dirt inside                                 | Is the entrance considered indoors? How neatly should shoes be placed?     |

## From Family Rules to Public Regulations

Shoe removal does not only happen in private residences. Public places codify it into notices, institutional information, or event preparations, so an action that was originally demonstrated by family members becomes an entry procedure that strangers must also understand. This transformation is particularly noteworthy: a family can supplement exceptions with one sentence, but a public place must write the shoe cabinet, seating, traffic flow, and reminders more clearly.

The official institutional information for the Yilan Museum explicitly states that shoes must be removed indoors and requires visitors not to make noise or run around.[^5] The official religious cultural map of Hualien Tzu Chi Cultural Park explains that one must remove shoes and speak softly when entering the meditation hall.[^6] These regulations transform a private family action into an entry requirement for public cultural venues.

The "Echoism" exhibition in the Ministry of Culture's Accessibility Services information also explicitly states that visitors must remove their shoes and suggests wearing shoes that are easy to take off.[^7] The shoe removal here is not just a cleanliness requirement; it directly affects how visually impaired attendees and companions prepare, how they enter the exhibition hall, and how the organizer arranges a space focused on sound and touch.

Schools turn this custom into teachable and enforceable rules. The computer lab usage guidelines at Dongfang Elementary School in Changhua County explicitly state that students must remove their shoes before entering the computer lab and place them neatly in the shoe cabinet; they must also not push each other while removing or putting on shoes.[^8] This regulation is noteworthy: it requires not just "taking off shoes," but also the order of storage and entry. Lifestyle habits are broken down here into a sequence of actions that can be taught to children.

📝 Curator's Note: When "taking shoes off" is written into school rules, it is no longer just etiquette learned at home, but a public traffic flow that many bodies must complete simultaneously. The location of the shoe cabinet, the order of waiting, and whether one can sit down determine whether this rule is understood or merely rushed through.[^7][^8]

## Exceptions Are Not Loopholes: Five Scenarios of Taiwanese Entrances

**Not every room requires shoes to be removed.** Life observations point out that some families only change shoes in specific rooms or use indoor slippers in zoned areas; this practice shows that "indoors" can also be divided into different cleanliness zones like the living room, bedroom, bathroom, and balcony.[^2][^9]

**Not every house has a formal _genkan_.** Taiwanese residences may only have a small section of tiles or space next to a shoe cabinet, and sometimes no entrance suitable for sitting down to change shoes. In these cases, shoe removal may still exist, but it relies on family cues and indoor slippers, not fixed architectural height differences.[^1][^9]

**Not every public venue uses the same reason.** Old tatami houses, religious venues, museums, and sound exhibitions may all require shoes to be removed, but the reasons involve preservation, quietness, site etiquette, sensory experience, or event design; they cannot all be explained by the single word "hygiene."[^4][^5][^6][^7][^12]

**Not every user can complete it with equal ease.** The Ministry of Culture's activity page suggests wearing easy-to-remove shoes, while school rules even write down shoe storage and not pushing; these details remind us that shoe removal is not just an action performed by the feet, but also involves bodily actions like sitting, bending, storing, and waiting.[^7][^8]

**Not all "not taking shoes off" means disrespect.** If a residence originally uses outdoor shoes or semi-barefoot methods, or if guests cannot remove their shoes due to mobility, wounds, or work requirements, the most appropriate action is to first ask the host and seek an alternative solution, such as shoe covers, clean indoor shoes, or staying only in the entrance area. This article did not find statistics supporting a "nationwide consistent alternative," so it only proposes practical negotiation principles, without framing them as national standards.

## The Cultivation of Shoe Removal Rules

A family's rules are usually not formed through long explanations. A child forgetting to change shoes for the first time might be called back to the entrance; a guest standing inside might be pointed toward the shoe cabinet by the host; and schools write "remove shoes, put away, no pushing" directly into classroom rules. The rule becomes muscle memory through repetition, eventually seeming like it was "meant to be."

This cultivation process can be broken down into four steps:

**Seeing the boundary.** Shoe cabinets, thresholds, flooring materials, tatami mats, or a row of indoor slippers first let people know where shoe changing is required.

**Imitating others.** Children watch family members remove shoes; visitors watch the host act; students watch classmates put their shoes in the cabinet.

**Accepting reminders.** "Put your shoes outside," or "Change into slippers inside" are low-cost verbal cues; schools formalize these reminders as written rules.

**Giving the rule a reason.** The floor must be kept clean, wooden floors fear wear and tear, tatami mats do not allow outdoor shoes, and computer labs require cleanliness; the reason may not always be spoken, but it allows the action to be replicated in the next space.

This set of rules does not guarantee uniformity across all families. It is more like an adjustable script: families can be stricter, guests who are close can be more casual, and old houses and new high-rises may have different entrance designs.

## "Semi-Shoe Removal" Is Closer to the Taiwanese Reality Than "Full Shoe Removal"

A life article observing Taiwanese residences points out that many homes lack a clear Japanese _genkan_, and residents might change into indoor shoes in an area that is "like a shoe changing zone"; the same article records differences between various families and old houses, listing hot and humid climate, tile floors, and cleaning methods as part of the lived experience.[^9] These articles are life observations, not nationwide censuses, but they remind us not to use a simple dichotomy of "taking shoes off / not taking shoes off."

A description closer to the Taiwanese reality might be "semi-shoe removal": shoes are changed at a certain point, but there is no formal Japanese threshold; indoor slippers are worn into the living room, but not necessarily into the bathroom; guests are asked to remove their shoes, yet family members might walk back outside while briefly retrieving something in slippers.

The "half" is not incomplete, but rather the flexibility generated within limited Taiwanese spaces. It allows one pair of shoes to correspond to multiple areas, and it allows family rules to adjust depending on different guests, weather, and floor conditions.

## Do Not Present Taiwanese Customs as Ancient East Asian Practices

Cross-cultural data must also be used carefully. The Japanese _genkan_ has a clear earthen floor (_doma_), threshold, and height difference, forming a set of shoe removal etiquette closely related to the residential structure.[^3] Data from Chinese history and modern life shows that indoor shoe removal was not always a consistent ancient tradition; as high tables and chairs became common, wearing shoes indoors sometimes increased, and there are also differences across modern regions and families.[^10][^11]

Therefore, one cannot combine the fact that Taiwan, Japan, and some Chinese families might remove their shoes to form a single continuous line from antiquity to today. The Taiwanese practice is more like a living script formed by overlapping various housing forms, colonial architectural influences, humid climate, family cleaning views, and public venue management across different eras.

## Differences in Users at the Entrance

The same shoe removal rule is not always equally easy for different people. School rules clearly state that shoes must be stored in a cabinet and no pushing; this means "shoe removal" includes bodily actions, storage location, and entry/exit order; while cultural department activity information reminds visitors to wear shoes that are easy to take off, indicating that the organizer must include the act of changing shoes in visitor preparation.[^7][^8]

These examples cannot directly prove that all places in Taiwan have adopted barrier-free shoe removal designs, but they point out a frequently overlooked issue: when shoe removal becomes an entry condition, chairs, handrails, shoe cabinet height, floor anti-slip measures, and companion traffic flow are no longer just decorative details, but part of whether the rules can be fairly enforced. This is a design inference based on two official regulations in this article, not a statistical conclusion about all venues in Taiwan.

## The Problem Left by One Pair of Shoes

Taking shoes off upon entering seems like the smallest daily action, yet it immediately reveals how a residence manages the body: who can walk right in? Who needs to change their shoes first? Where should the guest's shoes be placed? What if there is no shoe cabinet? Can people with mobility issues sit down and remove/put on shoes safely? How are communal slippers cleaned?

If shoe removal were only described as "Taiwanese people love cleanliness," the article would end too quickly. The truly interesting part is that every family must make a decision at the entrance: whether to leave outdoor dust outside, and whether to leave guest discomfort at the entrance.

Taiwan does not have a _genkan_ that is the same for everyone, but many people establish a boundary at the entrance. That line is sometimes the shoe cabinet, sometimes the tiles, sometimes the indoor slippers, and sometimes just the host looking at the shoes and saying, "Just put them here."
