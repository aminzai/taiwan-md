## First, Counting Down at Intersections

In 1998, the Taipei City Government incorporated countdown timers and human-shaped animations into pedestrian signals, allowing the originally static green figure to change pace according to the remaining crossing time.[^1] On March 18 of the following year, the intersection of Songzhi Road and Songshan Road in Taipei became the first to feature a pedestrian signal combining countdown timing with animated little green man graphics.[^2] When the countdown drops below 10 seconds, the little green man quickens its pace.[^1]

This design translates "how many seconds remain" into an action that pedestrians can understand without reading instructions. Numbers provide precise information, while pace converts time pressure into a rhythm visible to the naked eye. Pedestrians can sense the remaining crossing time through changes in the animation without first memorizing traffic rules.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The countdown numbers tell you how many seconds are left, while the pace makes you feel the weight of those seconds. The little green man's greatest success lies in bringing traffic engineering into a language the body can read instantly.

## Before Animation, Signals Were a Set of Static Poses

Before the little green man appeared, pedestrians were not simply facing a lamp without graphics. Earlier pedestrian signals already used human figures to distinguish "stop" from "go," but the figures themselves were static. The most representative version came from East Germany: traffic psychologist Karl Peglau proposed the red and green human figure design in 1961, aiming to use "color plus shape" to reinforce what was originally a signal relying mainly on color, making it easier for pedestrians to notice.[^3]

![The static red and green human figures of East Germany's 1961 Ampelmännchen](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/thumb/3/35/Ampelmann.svg/1280px-Ampelmann.svg.png)

_Photo: Static red and green Ampelmännchen. The original design was completed by Karl Peglau in 1961; both the SVG version and the original design are in the public domain.[^4]_

The message conveyed by these two figures was simple: the green figure in a stepping posture indicates it is safe to proceed, while the red figure with arms outstretched and standing still indicates to stop. They do not change speed based on remaining seconds, nor do they contain countdown numbers or character stories. The DDR Museum records that Peglau's design was initially too large and had to be reduced and simplified to fit existing lamp fixtures, later being tested on Berlin's Boulevard der Republik in 1969.[^3]

![A green static human figure pedestrian signal on the side of Berlin's Boulevard der Republik](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8d/Ampelm%C3%A4nnchen.JPG)

_Photo: A static Ampelmännchen pedestrian signal at a Berlin intersection. Photographer Arbalete released the photo into the public domain; image embedded via original hotlink.[^5]_

For Taiwan, the key in 1998 was not "first use of human-shaped pedestrian signals," but rather connecting the existing static "proceed/proceed no entry" signals with countdown timers and animation. The Ministry of the Interior's National Immigration Agency also summarized Taipei's innovation as "dynamic little green man + countdown timer."[^6] Therefore, to answer "what did the little green man look like before it appeared," the most accurate answer is: it was a fixed human figure that walks on green light and stands still on red light, rather than an animated figure that walks faster in frames.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: Only by clarifying this prehistory can we see where Taipei's innovation lies. It did not invent a human figure out of thin air, but rather extended the static signal that only said "go" or "stop" into an interface that simultaneously conveys remaining time and pace.

## Technically, It's Not Just a Special Effect

The little green man looks like a short animation, but it is actually an integration of an early LED pedestrian interface. Historical records preserved by United Daily News report that Taipei originally considered introducing countdown timers. Due to concerns that drivers seeing the countdown would rush, the design direction shifted to first handle the pedestrian signals. After discussions with multiple manufacturers, the solution not only counted down numbers but also animated the human figure.[^7]

At the time, the LED industry was still in its infancy. Making the human figure and numbers move simultaneously required over a year of research and development and programming design.[^7] The green figure was composed of seven different pose patterns, using visual persistence to create a walking effect. The countdown numbers and the red standing figure were integrated onto the same lamp panel.[^7] From a technical standpoint, the little green man is not a continuous recording, but a time interface formed by sequentially lighting up discrete frames.

This also explains why it could be quickly understood in daily life. The fixed red and green figures originally only answered "can I go now?"; the countdown numbers added "how much longer?"; and the animation further added the feeling of "time running out." Three pieces of information placed on the same lamp allowed pedestrians to make judgments at a glance.

## From Intersections into Collective Memory

The little green man did not remain confined to the context of traffic engineering. On March 18, 2016, Google created a homepage Doodle for the little green man's 17th birthday. The official introduction specifically mentioned Taipei's addition of different paces based on remaining time.[^1] A symbol that originally only appeared at intersections thus entered the web page visible to global users.

In September 2019, Chunghwa Post issued the "Taiwan Smart Transportation Construction Commemorative Stamps," featuring the little green man pedestrian signals, highway electronic toll collection, contactless smart cards, and traffic information control as four themes. The "20 seconds" on the little green man stamp symbolizes the 20th anniversary of the first signal installation.[^2] This does not mean the stamp awarded it cultural status, but it left behind a traceable public archive: in the national narrative of smart transportation, the little green man was chosen as one of the achievements of transportation construction.[^2]

From Google Doodle to stamps, the little green man gradually broke free from the state of "only visible when the light is green." It was saved, shared, and collected, allowing functional symbols at intersections to enter cultural memory. This transformation did not require mythologizing the little green man. More noteworthy is how a design passed by millions of people every day became a tellable Taiwanese experience after being re-framed.

## Local Creativity, Beyond Just One Figure

Once the little green man became a shared urban language, local governments began adding their own stories to it. In 2018, Pingtung launched a Valentine's Day version: during green light, a pair of figures walk together; during red light, the man kneels to propose. Taiwan Today reported that these themed signals were deployed at 45 intersections in Pingtung County.[^8] In May of the same year, Pingtung launched a family version, where the red light shows a husband with his pregnant wife, and the green light shows a family of three walking together. Reports stated that 37 family versions plus 39 proposal versions, totaling 76 creative animated signals.[^9]

_Extended viewing: [The proposal and family versions of Pingtung's little green man signals](https://img.taipeitimes.com/images/2018/05/10/P02-180510-pic2B.jpg)_

_Photo: Left is the proposal version, right is the family version; original caption states photo source as Central News Agency, image provided by Taipei Times via original hotlink.[^9]_

Chiayi City incorporated the local image of turkey rice into the pedestrian signal system. The city government installed light boxes at the intersections of Minzu Road and Wenhua Road, as well as Zhongshan Road and Xingzhong Street and Guanghua Road, allowing a turkey to accompany the little green man with LED dynamic imagery, paired with traffic safety slogans. The details of this approach are critical: the Chiayi City Transportation Bureau explicitly stated that pedestrian signals involve safety, guidance, and unified identification, and must retain the display of "human" to indicate crossing status. The turkey was placed in an additional light box, rather than replacing the little green man.[^10]

_Extended viewing: [Chiayi City's turkey little green man creative signals at Wenhua Road and Minzu Road intersection](https://icmp-ws.chiayi.gov.tw/001/Upload/399/relpic/9150/835728/0f1c9334-e518-48af-a2db-3cbedcfee72d.jpg)_

_Photo: Chiayi City installing turkey image light boxes next to the little green man pedestrian signals. Original source: Chiayi City Government Transportation Bureau.[^10]_

Hsinchu City also introduced Rody the jumping horse imagery at an intersection in the city center during the 2026 "Hsinchu Over the New Year" period. The Urban Marketing Office stated that the design must retain the clear indication of the original red and green lights, and whether to expand will depend on public feedback and actual effectiveness, under the premise of not affecting recognition and traffic safety.[^11] From Pingtung's love and family themes, Chiayi's turkey, to Hsinchu's festival characters, the scope of local variations has far exceeded the initial "how fast the little green man walks."

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The hardest part of local characteristics is not coming up with a cute character, but ensuring that after the character appears, pedestrians can still read "can I cross now?" at a glance.

## From Signal Lights to Urban Legends

Once the little green man entered daily life, it quickly grew stories not belonging to engineering specifications. The most famous is the claim that "it falls down once every 20,000 steps." The science communication platform Science CITY compiled Taiwan Urban Legends Encyclopedia, noting that this saying had been circulating via email around 2000, later spawning different explanations such as "engineer easter eggs" or "reminders to pedestrians."[^12]

This legend is not entirely without basis: pedestrian signals can indeed malfunction, and abnormal little green man footage was recorded in news reports from 2004 and 2005.[^12] However, the legend appeared before related malfunction reports, and there is no evidence that "falling after a fixed number of steps" was pre-programmed as an engineering mechanism.[^12] Instead, it shows that when people cannot see the programs and hardware behind the seven-frame animation, they will attribute personality, fatigue, and accidents to a repeatedly moving human figure.

Urban legends are not part of the little green man's technical history, but they are part of its social history. They turned a signal designed to improve recognition into a character that people wait for, forward, and debate. It also reminds us that once public design is close enough to daily life, its meaning is no longer determined solely by engineers.

## When Signals Say Too Much

Special animations also triggered a more rigorous design criticism: the primary task of traffic signals is to allow people to quickly and consistently identify stop and go. When the lamp face begins to carry turkeys, proposals, families, festival characters, or urban marketing, pedestrians may be asked to recognize information unrelated to crossing in addition to judging whether they can proceed. The problem is not that local elements are inherently dangerous, but whether the safety signal can maintain its priority when information increases.

This is not an abstract aesthetic debate. Chiayi City's choice of "adding light boxes" rather than directly replacing the human figure with a turkey reflects the bottom line of unified identification.[^10] Hsinchu City also took retaining the clear indication of the original red and green lights as a design premise.[^11] Both cases show that for local creativity to enter intersections, it must first answer a more fundamental question than "does it look good?": Does it help pedestrians read the signal faster?

Criticism of the fast-walking animation is even more direct. The Economic Daily News quoted Wu Yichuan, executive director of "Returning the Road to the People," pointing out that when pedestrians suddenly accelerate on crosswalks, it disrupts drivers' expectations of pedestrian speed, and pedestrians themselves are more likely to ignore surrounding environments. The report summarized the flashing signal as "a safety warning, not a starting gun."[^13] This viewpoint does not deny the creativity of the animation itself, but reminds us of something more primitive: traffic signals are not a short video that requires people to stop and decode, but an instruction that allows people to make immediate safety judgments.

## The Fast-Walking Animation Retires

On March 11, 2026, the Ministry of Transportation and Communications and the Ministry of the Interior amended Article 207 of the Road Traffic Signs, Markings, and Signal Installation Rules. The current regulations state that a steadily displayed green "walking person" signal indicates it is safe to cross the road. When the green light flashes, it indicates that the remaining green light time is short; those who have not yet entered the road should avoid crossing, while those already on the road should pass quickly or stop on the road's traffic island.[^14]

The regulations deal with the meaning of "flashing," while the fast-walking animation is a separate layer of hardware and programming. In the next day's policy explanation, the Ministry of Transportation and Communications stated that it will cancel the little green man's fast-walking animation during the final countdown, but retain the flashing prompt, to prevent pedestrians from misinterpreting the signal as "seizing the last chance to rush."[^15] At the time, the Ministry also explained that some counties and cities' signal programs need to be modified, and will coordinate with local governments, with a goal of unifying within 2026. Among the 19 cities and counties listed by Central News Agency, all have pedestrian signals with fast-walking animations.[^15]

This is not the first attempt to reduce the implication of rushing. A 2026 local report by United Daily News pointed out that Taipei City has been reducing the fast-walking animation cycle frequency from 3-4 cycles per second to 2 cycles per second since 2021. Survey data provided by Taipei City's Public Works Commission in the report showed that the rate of pedestrians running across dropped from 1.56% before the adjustment to 1.12% after the adjustment.[^16] This set of data is insufficient to prove all safety effects on its own, but it presents a direction: the speed of the signal's movement itself may indeed affect how pedestrians understand the remaining time at intersections.

This adjustment adds a less romantic but more important ending to the little green man's story. More than twenty years ago, animation made abstract countdowns visible. More than twenty years later, safety thinking requires signals to say less, giving pedestrians more time to look at the road and making it easier for drivers to predict. The meaning of the little green man will not be determined solely by how fast it runs; the design questions it leaves behind are: Can public space preserve local characteristics while not letting the most important safety signals be overshadowed by stories?
