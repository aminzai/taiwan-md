> **30-second overview:** In 1905, Taiwan's first hydroelectric plant went online, and electricity began moving from a handful of facilities into city streets and homes. Utility poles carried electricity, telephone lines, streetlights, and assorted communication cables; transformer boxes converted high-voltage distribution current into the low-voltage 110V or 220V power used inside homes. They look like street furniture, but in reality they are active system nodes in every neighborhood. When cables are buried underground, the sky becomes cleaner—but electricity does not disappear; it simply shifts to underground conduits, ground-level enclosures, and more complex maintenance work. Taipei moved transformer boxes into an exhibition; Tainan turned them into bookshelves, kitchen cabinets, and record players; Southern Taiwan Science Park painted cows and milk cartons; Taichung's Wuchih Port depicted marine life and household appliances. This article is not about how cute transformer boxes have become, but about how Taiwan is reorganizing a once-ignored object among power safety, disaster-prevention engineering, local culture, and streetscape aesthetics.

> **📝 Curator's note**
> Utility poles are not the stars of the streetscape, yet they are the most familiar street-corner coordinates for Taiwanese people. They guide the way, carry wires, withstand typhoons, and remind residents during blackouts that the power system is not abstract. This article looks at utility poles and transformer boxes on the same line, because "tidying the streetscape" has never been just about removing eyesores. Undergrounding brings new costs, and painting brings new negotiations. Who decides what story a box should tell, who is responsible for maintenance, and whether residents can still recognize its hazards and functions after beautification—those are the real questions worth asking about these street-corner images.

## How a Single Pole Becomes the City's Skeleton

In 1905, Taiwan established its first hydroelectric plant. When Taiwan Power Company (Taipower) compiled the history of the electrical industry, it placed this event within the long arc of electricity gradually entering cities and homes. Utility poles in this process were not merely wooden or concrete pillars holding up wires; they also carried public services such as telecommunications and streetlights. [^1] [^10]

This modernization did not happen only at the power plant. Electricity had to travel along lines through factories, shops, residences, and rural areas before reaching a light bulb, a fan, or a telephone. Utility poles fixed the invisible flow of energy into recognizable lines. When people say "by that utility pole," they are really using a public system to locate a neighborhood.

Taipower's monthly magazine also left another clue. Utility poles entered the paintings of Taiwanese artists—not because they were beautiful, but because they proved a place had already connected to a new order of daily life. Wires crossing the canvas signified that lighting, communications, and industry were no longer distant technologies but conditions of everyday life pulled into view. [^1]

> A utility pole is easiest to see as "just one thing," but it is more like a vertical checklist of the city. Identification plates, guy wires, transformers, streetlights, telephone lines, cable TV, and surveillance cameras may be added one by one across different eras. A streetscape looks messy sometimes not because there is too much of any single object, but because decades of public needs have never been reorganized.

## Transformer Boxes Scale Down the High-Voltage System to the Street Corner

Transformer boxes are often treated as street furniture by residents because the complex power system has been condensed into a single enclosure with a door, a lock, and warning labels. Taipower explains that pad-mounted transformers convert high-voltage distribution current into the low-voltage 110V or 220V power used in households. To consumers, they occupy a small space on a sidewalk or alley entrance; to the power grid, they are indispensable nodes between the high-voltage distribution network and the wall outlets inside homes. [^2]

When Taipower held its first transformer box-themed exhibition in 2019, it displayed equipment from the 1960s to the present and noted that the number of transformer boxes across Taiwan totals roughly 1.39 million. This figure does not mean every box should become a work of art, but it does make clear that transformer boxes are not occasional obstacles on a few street corners—they are the necessary ground-level interfaces where the large-scale distribution network meets daily life. [^2]

The appearance of transformer boxes therefore faces two competing demands. They must clearly display danger warnings, identification numbers, and maintenance information, yet they must also fit into the scale of residential areas, commercial districts, campuses, and industrial parks. Covering a box with paint does not equal completing a design. Design must still answer whether safety labels remain visible, whether maintenance doors can open, and whether the image still makes sense after equipment upgrades.

## Undergrounding Cleans Up the Sky, but Does Not Make Electricity Invisible

Cable undergrounding is often imagined as a simple swap: pull out the utility poles and the sky becomes clean. But Taipower's engineering explanation shows that undergrounding is really about reorganizing the location of cables and equipment. Overhead lines must be moved into underground conduits; some distribution equipment becomes ground-level installations; and the work must coordinate with road space, population density, load capacity, resident willingness, excavation permits, and later maintenance. [^5]

Costs shift along with the work. Taipower data shows that undergrounding projects and subsequent maintenance cost roughly 8 to 12 times that of overhead lines. This is not an argument against undergrounding, but a reminder that behind a clean skyline still lie conduits, manholes, pumping equipment, excavation, and long-term maintenance. Invisible cables do not mean unmanaged cables. [^5]

Taipower's cable-focused reporting further notes that as of December 2023, Taiwan's total undergrounded line length—including offshore islands—exceeded 170,000 kilometers, a ratio of 44.6%. When underground lines fail, repair crews may first have to locate manholes, then deal with excavation, flooding, dredging, and equipment replacement. Flood-prone areas are not necessarily suitable for undergrounding. [^6]

A National Chengchi University report compiled the risk of utility pole collapse around typhoons such as Typhoon Meranti, noting that undergrounding is one engineering option for reducing storm-related exposure, but underground facilities remain vulnerable to flooding, earthquakes, and construction constraints. [^4] So undergrounding is not about eliminating problems; it is about moving risk from the sky to the ground, trading visual clutter for construction and maintenance complexity.

> **📝 Curator's note**
> "Not visible" is easily mistaken for "nonexistent." Undergrounding is most often photographed as a clean streetscape, but manholes, pumps, and repair trucks rarely appear in the same frame. If the aesthetics of public infrastructure are measured only by what the eye sees—without considering who repairs it, where it floods, or how faults are located—then the costs are simply pushed out of the photograph.

## Transformer Boxes Begin Learning to Speak Locally

As cable undergrounding pushes wires out of the sky, transformer boxes remain more visibly on the ground. From 2015 to 2017, Taipower promoted a "living landscape" renovation program that tried to coordinate equipment colors, signage, and surrounding environments. Taipower's monthly magazine later discussed minimalist design in relation to transformer boxes, emphasizing that the goal was not simply adding more patterns but organizing the exterior, reducing visual noise, and bringing equipment and streets to an appropriate scale. [^3] [^16]

Taipei's Xinyi District case turned transformer boxes into interpretable exhibits. The 2019 transformer box-themed exhibition displayed equipment, color schemes, wooden utility poles, and wiring from different eras, temporarily moving objects that people usually pass by into positions where they could be compared, observed, and explained. [^2] In 2020, Taipower brought the themed exhibition to the Taiwan Design Expo; official data reported nearly 50,000 visitors and 10 transformer boxes on display dating from the 1960s to the present. [^7]

The significance of such exhibitions is not that transformer boxes suddenly became collectible, but that residents gained a viewing distance they normally lack. On the street corner, people first see warnings against climbing or opening the boxes. In an exhibition hall, a box can be measured, compared across eras, observed for its paint job, and discussed regarding what remains after equipment retires. High-Speed Rail's TLife magazine once reported on painted transformer boxes that, after updates, were converted into cultural-and-creative merchandise, giving power infrastructure a memorable second life. [^12]

## Tainan Paints Old-City Objects Back onto the Street Corner

The creative transformer boxes in Tainan's Zhongxi District do not treat local culture as an abstract tourism poster. The works reported by the Liberty Times were fashioned into nostalgic bookshelves, kitchen cabinets, tofu presses, and record players. [^17] They compressed reading, grocery shopping, dining, home collections, and sound memory into shapes that passersby can recognize within a few seconds.

Another batch of Tainan public-facility designs was jointly created by local designers and school students, placing cultural symbols such as those of Ye Shi-tao [^18] onto the street corner. People first see a bookshelf or kitchen cabinet, then realize it is still a power facility that cannot be climbed or opened at will. [^18] The image here does not disguise the equipment so completely that it becomes unrecognizable; instead, it gives residents an additional path to understanding it.

This approach also has an expiration date. Transformer boxes are not permanent buildings; equipment upgrades, road construction, or changes in distribution methods can all cause the images to disappear. Local culture painted onto a box is simultaneously placed inside an object that must obey power-safety and engineering cycles. This makes the work feel more like daily life. It is not a cultural relic preserved in a museum, but a streetscape object that endures daily sun, rain, collision, and maintenance.

## Southern Taiwan Science Park Brings Agricultural Imagination into the Tech Park

The painted transformer boxes at Southern Taiwan Science Park use imagery of cows, milk cartons, cheese, and strawberries. These images place the agricultural imagination of the park's surroundings alongside the technological landscape, so that transformer boxes represent not only machines and electricity but also the relationship between an industrial park and the surrounding land. [^18]

What is interesting about the Southern Taiwan Science Park case is that it does not choose a single technological symbol. Had it only painted chips, circuits, or machinery, the equipment would easily have become part of the park's brand-identity system. Cows and milk cartons redirect attention to another scale of daily life, reminding people that the park is not a closed island floating above the land. Farms, food, commuters, and residents still exist beside it.

Public-facility design here does not invent a brand-new story for a place; it places two landscapes that already coexist—technological efficiency and rural imagery—onto a single box. The efficiency of the tech park and rural imagery are not naturally harmonious, which is precisely why painting is not mere decoration but an attempt to put local relationships into public view.

## Taichung Wuchih Port Lets the Community Paint Its Own Recognition

The case of Wuchih Port in Taichung's Nantun District pushes transformer-box painting to the community scale. Local reports mention that throughout the port area, transformer boxes display images of marine life, flowers, vending machines, and ATMs. Port residents and passersby do not see a row of uniform equipment but a set of visual cues that can identify intersections and neighborhoods. [^19]

Another batch of Taichung street works turns transformer boxes into computers, coffee machines, and dish warmers. These images move objects from inside homes or shops to the outdoors, turning a box that people usually have to walk around into something visible, photographable, and discussable within seconds. [^20]

These cases should not be treated as a single successful template. Taipei focuses on display and public education; Tainan connects daily objects to historic streets; Southern Taiwan Science Park handles the juxtaposition of industry and land; Wuchih Port builds local identity through community co-creation. Their common point is not color but the way residents begin to place equipment within local narratives.

> **📝 Curator's note**
> Painted transformer boxes are most easily written off as "ugly infrastructure turned into photo spots." But that phrase misses the most important part: the box still carries electricity, still needs maintenance, still cannot be climbed, and may disappear in a few years due to equipment upgrades. Good local design does not hide functionality; it lets images, warnings, and maintenance responsibility remain visible on the street at the same time.

## After the Poles Disappear, What Local Memory Remains

The cable-undergrounding case in Kaohsiung's Huangpu New Village shows that removing utility poles is not just about improving visuals. The project simultaneously involved plantings, power-outage risks, street usage, and the cultural landscape of military-dependent's housing. Local reports placed the undergrounding within the context of settlement-preservation, reminding people that "tidying" does not mean removing every trace of daily life but rather re-deciding which traces to keep and in what form. [^11]

The cultural-memory archive collected by the National Digital Archives also placed high-voltage boxes, concrete utility poles, and electricity-fee management back into residents' daily lives. These materials are not merely engineering files of the power company; they also record how communities discussed public lighting, shared expenses, and built changes. [^8]

The local memory of utility poles does not necessarily survive as a building. It may exist only in the oral history "the pole used to stand here" or in an identification number that only residents recognize. When roads are rebuilt, lines are buried, or communities are redeveloped, what disappears is not just a single pole but an entire vocabulary used for navigation, recalling blackouts, and recognizing neighborhoods.

## Streetscape Aesthetics Is Not About Hiding Infrastructure

The debate over utility poles and transformer boxes is often simplified into order versus chaos. A more accurate question is: what does a city want residents to see, and what does it want them to forget? Undergrounding keeps wires from cutting across the sky, but it does not make the power system invisible. Transformer boxes still sit at alley entrances; manhole covers still lie in the road; repair trucks still enter neighborhoods; and during blackouts, residents still need to know where the nearest equipment is and who is responsible.

Visibility is double-edged for public infrastructure. It can bring clutter, but it also provides recognition, reporting, and accountability. The identification numbers on utility poles, the warnings on transformer boxes, and the location of street-corner equipment let residents find the system when they need it. If painting obscures safety labels, aesthetics will collide directly with safety. If painting places local stories and functions clearly together, it has a chance to become public design rather than temporary wall decoration.

Taipower's retired-transformer-box reproduction project, done in collaboration with design brands, offers another vision. Mountain-and-water painting and equipment structure were translated into cultural-and-creative merchandise, allowing decommissioned boxes to retain the memory of public service. [^21] It reminds us that the life of a piece of equipment does not necessarily end at retirement, but reuse cannot replace the original maintenance and safety requirements in reverse.

Taiwan's utility-pole landscape is becoming fewer, sinking underground, or finding new forms of presence. It may exit the visual field through undergrounding projects, or it may gain new identity through painted transformer boxes, local preservation, or public exhibitions. What is truly worth preserving is not every pole or every painting, but how people collectively decide: where the power system should appear, how local memory should be seen, and what a city must pay for a clean sky.

> **📝 Curator's note**
> Next time you pass a painted transformer box, look at the image first, then at the identification number, warnings, and maintenance door. This small shift in viewing order pulls the streetscape back from "does it look good?" to "who keeps it running?" Taiwan's modern life has always hung from these wires. When the wires are buried underground and boxes are painted with local stories, we must remember that invisibility and beautification do not equal disappearance.
