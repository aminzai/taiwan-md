> **30-Second Overview:** In 1905, Taiwan’s first hydropower plant opened, bringing electricity from a few facilities into cities and homes. Utility poles carry power, telephone, streetlights, and various communication lines onto the streets, while substations step down high-voltage distribution to the low voltage usable in homes. They may look like street furniture, but they are actually system nodes still operating in every neighborhood. As cable undergrounding cleans up the sky, power does not disappear; it simply moves to underground pipelines, ground-level boxes, and more complex maintenance tasks. Taipei has moved substations into exhibitions, Taichung has turned them into bookshelves, vegetable cabinets, and record players, the Nangang Science Park has painted cows and milk cartons on them, and Wuchi in Taichung has featured marine life and everyday appliances on their surfaces. This article is not about how cute substations have become, but about how Taiwan is rearranging an object originally meant to be ignored, balancing power safety, disaster prevention engineering, local culture, and streetscape aesthetics.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> Utility poles are not the protagonists of the landscape, yet they are the most familiar street-corner coordinates for Taiwanese. They guide directions, carry wires, withstand typhoons, and remind residents during blackouts that the power system is not abstract. This article places poles and substations on the same line because "tidying up the streetscape" has never been merely about removing eyesores. Undergrounding brings new costs, and painting brings new negotiations. Who decides what local story a box should depict, who is responsible for maintenance, and whether residents can still recognize its danger and purpose after beautification are the true questions worth asking about these street-corner images.

## How a Single Pole Became the City’s Skeleton

In 1905, Taiwan established its first hydropower plant. When Taipower organized the history of the electric industry, it placed this event within the long timeline of Taiwan’s power gradually entering cities and households. In this process, utility poles were not merely wood or concrete pillars supporting wires; they also appeared on streets alongside telecommunications and streetlights as public services. [^1] [^10]

This modernization did not occur only in power plants. Electricity had to travel along lines through factories, shops, residences, and rural areas before finally reaching a lamp, a fan, or a telephone. Poles fixed the invisible flow of energy into recognizable lines. When people say "next to that utility pole," they are actually using a public system to locate a neighborhood.

Taipower’s monthly magazine archives provide another layer of clues. Poles entered the landscapes of Taiwanese painters not because they were particularly beautiful, but because they proved a place had connected to a new order of life. Wires crossing the frame represented lighting, communication, and industry no longer as distant technologies, but as daily conditions pulled into the foreground. [^1]

Number plates, guy wires, transformers, streetlights, telephone lines, cable TV, and surveillance cameras may have been added one by one over different decades. The streetscape appears messy not necessarily because one object is too numerous, but because decades of public demand have not been reorganized.

## Substations Shrink High-Voltage Systems to the Street Corner

Substations are often treated by residents as street furniture because a complex power system is condensed into a box with a door, a lock, and warning labels. Taipower explains that the function of pad-mounted transformers is to convert high voltage in the distribution system to the low voltage of 110 or 220 volts used in homes. To the consumer, it occupies only a small spot by the arcade or alley entrance. To the power system, it is an indispensable node between the high-voltage distribution network and home outlets. [^2]

In 2019, Taipower held its first substation-themed exhibition, displaying equipment from the 1960s to the present and noting that the total number of substations across Taiwan reaches approximately 1.39 million. This number does not mean every box should become an artwork, but it clarifies that substations are not accidental obstacles at a few street corners, but necessary interfaces left by a large-scale distribution network on the ground. [^2]

The appearance of substations therefore faces two conflicting demands. It must clearly mark dangers, numbers, and maintenance information, while also coexisting with the surrounding scale in residential, commercial, campus, or industrial park areas. Covering a box with patterns does not equal completing the design. Design must also answer whether safety markings are visible, whether maintenance doors can be opened, and whether the image remains meaningful after equipment updates.

## Undergrounding Cleans the Sky but Does Not Make Power Invisible

Cable undergrounding is often imagined as an intuitive exchange: pull out the poles, and the sky becomes clean. However, Taipower’s engineering explanations show that undergrounding is about rearranging the positions of pipelines and equipment. Overhead lines are placed into underground conduits, some distribution equipment is changed to ground-level facilities, and engineering must coordinate with road space, population density, load, resident willingness, excavation permits, and subsequent maintenance. [^5]

Costs also shift. Taipower data indicates that undergrounding engineering and subsequent maintenance costs are approximately 8 to 12 times that of overhead lines. This is not a reason to oppose undergrounding, but a reminder that behind the clean skyline lie pipelines, manholes, pumping, excavation, and long-term maintenance. Invisible lines do not mean no management. [^5]

Taipower’s special topic on power cables further explains that as of December 2023, underground line mileage across Taiwan including offshore islands exceeded 170,000 km, accounting for 44.6%. When underground lines fail, maintenance personnel may first need to search for manholes, then face excavation, water accumulation, silt clearing, and equipment replacement. Flood-prone areas are not necessarily suitable for undergrounding. [^6]

The National Chengchi University Journal compiled disaster experiences from Typhoon Megi to analyze the risk of pole collapse, explaining that undergrounding is an engineering option to reduce exposure to wind disasters, but underground facilities may still be limited by flooding, earthquakes, and construction conditions. [^4] Thus, undergrounding does not eliminate problems but transfers risk from the sky to the ground, trading visual clutter for construction and maintenance complexity.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> "Not seeing" is often mistaken for "non-existence." Undergrounding is most easily photographed as a clean streetscape image, but rarely includes manholes, pumps, and maintenance vehicles in the frame. If public facility aesthetics are calculated only by line of sight, ignoring who repairs it, where it floods, and how to locate it when it fails, the cost is ultimately hidden outside the photo.

## Substations Begin to Learn to Speak Locally

As cable undergrounding causes wires to retreat from the sky, substations remain more clearly on the ground. Between 2015 and 2017, Taipower promoted a living landscape transformation plan, attempting to coordinate equipment colors, markings, and the surrounding environment. Taipower’s monthly magazine later discussed substations through "subtraction design," focusing not just on adding patterns, but on organizing appearances, reducing visual noise, and ensuring equipment and streets have appropriate scale. [^3] [^16]

The Xinyi District case in Taipei turned substations into explainable exhibits. The 2019 substation-themed exhibition displayed equipment from different eras, color schemes, wooden poles, and lines, allowing objects usually passed by to temporarily leave their original positions for comparison, viewing, and explanation. [^2] In 2020, Taipower brought the themed exhibition to the Taiwan Design Expo; official data stated the exhibition attracted nearly 50,000 visitors, displaying 10 substations from the 1960s to the present. [^7]

The significance of such exhibitions is not that substations suddenly became worth collecting, but that residents gained a viewing distance they normally lacked. At the street corner, people first see warnings prohibiting climbing or opening. In the exhibition hall, boxes can be measured, eras compared, coatings observed, and it can be discussed what remains after equipment retirement. HSR TLife once reported on cases where painted substations were updated into cultural and creative objects, giving power equipment a memorable subsequent life. [^12]

## Taichung Paints Old City Objects Back to the Street Corner

The creative substations in Taichung’s West Central District did not paint local culture into an abstract tourism poster. Four works reported by Liberty Times were made into nostalgic bookshelves, vegetable cabinets, tofu blocks, and record players. [^17] They condensed reading, buying groceries, dining, family collections, and sound memories into shapes recognizable in seconds by passersby.

Another batch of public facility designs in Taichung was collaboratively created by local designers and school students, incorporating local cultural imagery such as Ye Shi-tao into street corners. People first see bookshelves or vegetable cabinets, then realize these are still power equipment that cannot be climbed or opened arbitrarily. [^18] Images here do not disguise the equipment until it is completely invisible, but provide residents with an additional path to understand it.

This approach has its limits. Substations are not permanent structures; equipment updates, road construction, or changes in distribution methods may cause images to disappear. As local culture is painted on, it is also placed into an object that must obey power safety and engineering cycles. This actually brings the work closer to daily life. It is not an artifact protected in a museum, but a streetscape that endures daily sun, rain, collisions, and maintenance.

## Nangang Science Park Brings Agricultural Imagination into the Tech Park

The painted substations in Nangang Science Park use food imagery such as cows, milk cartons, cheese, and strawberries. These images place the agricultural imagination surrounding the park alongside the tech industry landscape, ensuring substations represent not just machines and power, but also the relationship between an industrial park and surrounding land. [^18]

The interesting aspect of the Nangang case is that it did not choose a single tech symbol. If only chips, circuits, or machinery were painted, the equipment would easily become part of the park’s identification system. Cows and milk cartons, however, direct the gaze to another scale of life, reminding people that the park is not a closed island floating above the land. There are still farmlands, food, commuting routes, and residents nearby.

Public facility design here does not create a brand-new story for the place, but places two coexisting landscapes onto the same box. The efficiency of tech parks and rustic imagery may not be naturally harmonious; precisely because of this, painting is not mere decoration, but an attempt to place local relationships into public view.

## Taichung Wuchi Lets the Community Paint Its Own Identity

The case of Dingliao Li in Wuchi, Taichung, pushed substation painting to the community scale. Local reports mentioned that substations throughout the li feature images of marine life, flowers, vending machines, and ATMs. When port residents and road users pass by, they see not a row of uniform equipment, but a set of visual clues for recognizing intersections and the community. [^19]

Another batch of Taichung street works turned substations into everyday appliances like computers, coffee machines, and dish dryers. These images moved objects from homes or shops outdoors, turning a box usually bypassed into something visible, photographable, and discussable within tens of seconds. [^20]

Taipei focuses on exhibition and public education; Taichung connects everyday appliances to historical districts; Nangang handles the juxtaposition of industry and land; Wuchi establishes local identity through community co-creation. Their commonality is not color, but the fact that residents begin to place equipment into local narratives.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> Painted substations are most easily written about as "ugly facilities turning into check-in spots." But this sentence misses the most important part: the box still has electricity, still needs maintenance, still cannot be climbed, and may disappear in a few years due to equipment updates. Good local design does not hide function, but keeps images, warnings, and maintenance responsibilities simultaneously on the streetscape.

## After Poles Disappear, What Remains of Local Memory?

The cable undergrounding case at Huangpu New Village in Kaohsiung shows that pulling out poles is not merely about visual improvement. The engineering simultaneously involves planting, blackout risks, street usage, and眷村 (military village) cultural landscapes. Local reports placed undergrounding in the context of preserving settlement appearance, reminding people that "tidying up" does not mean removing all traces of life, but redeciding which traces remain and in what manner. [^11]

Objects recorded in the National Palace Museum’s National Cultural Memory Bank, such as those from Lu Jia Community, also place high-voltage substations, concrete poles, and electricity bill management back into resident life. These materials belong not only to Taipower’s engineering archives, but also record how the community discusses public lighting, shared expenses, and infrastructure changes. [^8]

The local memory of poles may not be preserved as a building. It may exist only in the oral history of "that pole used to be here," or in a number recognized only by residents. When road renovation, line undergrounding, or community reconstruction occurs, what disappears is often not just a pole, but a vocabulary used for guiding directions, recalling blackouts, and recognizing neighbors.

## Streetscape Aesthetics Do Not Hide Infrastructure

The debate over utility poles and substations is often simplified into order versus chaos. A more accurate question is what a city wants residents to see, and what it wants them to forget. Undergrounding stops lines from cutting the sky, but does not make the power system invisible. Substations remain at alley entrances, manhole covers remain on the road, maintenance vehicles still enter neighborhoods, and during blackouts, residents still need to know where nearby equipment is and who is responsible for handling it.

Visibility has two sides for public facilities. It may bring clutter, but also provides entry points for recognition, reporting, and accountability. Numbers on poles, warnings on substations, and equipment positions at street corners allow residents to find the system when needed. If painting covers markings, aesthetics directly collide with safety. Only if painting clearly places local stories alongside function can it become public design, rather than merely temporary wall decoration.

Cases of retired substations remanufactured through cooperation between Taipower and design brands offer another imagination. Landscape paintings and equipment structures are translated into cultural and creative objects, allowing boxes that have stopped supplying power to retain memories of public service. [^21] It reminds people that equipment life does not necessarily end at retirement, but reuse cannot reverse the original maintenance and safety requirements.

Taiwan’s utility pole landscape is becoming fewer, sinking, or leaving in a different way. It may exit sight during undergrounding engineering, or gain a new identity through substation painting, local preservation, or public exhibitions. What is truly worth preserving is not every pole, nor every painting, but how people collectively decide: where the power system should appear, how local memory should be seen, and what price the city must pay for a clean sky.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> The next time you pass a painted substation, look at the image first, then the number, warning, and maintenance door. This viewing order is small, but it pulls the streetscape back from "is it good-looking" to "who makes it work." Taiwan’s modern life has always hung on these lines. When lines are buried underground and boxes are painted with local stories, we need to remember more that invisibility and beautification do not equal disappearance.
