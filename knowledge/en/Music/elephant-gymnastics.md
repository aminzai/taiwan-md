# Elephant Gym: The Taiwanese Trio You Can Hear Without Anyone Singing

![Elephant Gym performing in Portland, Oregon, USA in March 2024](/article-images/music/elephant-gym-portland-2024.webp)
_March 2024, Elephant Gym performing in Portland, Oregon, USA. Photo by TurquoiseGoose, [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Elephant_Gym_Portland_2024.jpg) ([CC BY-SA 4.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/4.0/))._

> **30-second overview:** Elephant Gym is a three-piece Taiwanese rock band with no vocalist. Brother Chang Kaiting plays guitar, sister Chang Kaiting (KT) plays bass, and junior member Tu Chia-ch'in is on drums; the band formed in Kaohsiung in February 2012[^1]. They push the bass to the forefront as the lead melody, sidestepping the language barrier that Mandarin vocals face in the international market. Over 13 years they've taken the Fuji Rock main stage, Tyler the Creator's Camp Flog Gnaw, SXSW three times, the UK's ArcTangent, and signed with American indie label Topshelf. In June 2024 they won the 35th Golden Melody Awards' Jury Prize, and in November at Taipei's flagship venue they announced they were "entering a very long hiatus"[^2]. The documentary _A Dream More Real Than Dreams_ simultaneously reveals that on the eve of their 2023 world tour, they were standing on the brink of breaking up.

---

Hey, did you know? There's a band from Taiwan who, when they played a full set on the main stage of Japan's Fuji Rock, the three- or four-thousand people in the audience didn't hear a single word of lyrics from start to finish.

That wasn't a performance mishap. Their band just doesn't have a lead singer.

## A Rock Band Without a Frontman

The first time someone hears Elephant Gym, they usually do one thing within the first 15 seconds: look for the vocalist's position in their brain.

Taiwanese listeners of pop music are trained to "catch the vocals first, then judge the style." The moment Elephant Gym starts playing, the bass begins with melody. That bass line doesn't follow the traditional low-frequency support route but instead takes bright, high-positioned melodic lines, almost standing where the vocals would be. The drums break down the rhythm underneath, and the guitar provides counterpoint harmonies. By the time you finish listening to the full song "Underwater," you'll realize the "vocalist" has actually been there the whole time—it just switched to an instrument doing the talking.[^3]

KT's playing style is quite rare among Taiwanese bands. Traditional rock bass follows root notes, octave jumps, and fifth slides, handing the rhythm over to the drum kit. KT's left hand often moves around frets 9 to 14, and her right hand uses finger style to pluck out clean, bright tones. That range is closer to jazz bassists like Jaco Pastorius and Victor Wooten—melodic bass. Her training background is actually classical music and flute, and she only later switched to bass. This unconventional path means her understanding of "melody" came before "rhythm."

Jiuteng explained this design herself: "All three of us have a lot we want to say, so we speak directly through our own instruments rather than through a vocalist—this actually allows us to better express the thoughts of all three of us."[^4]

This collision of listening habits was actually picked up faster by international music critics than by those in Taiwan. A Hong Kong _South China Morning Post_ article from August 2014 spread widely: "Math rock is typically a technical, complex and somewhat intense genre, but Taiwanese trio Elephant Gym add a touch of cuteness to the equation."[^5] American _Pitchfork_ used the word "sublime" in their review of _Dreams_: "Like the music of L'Rain or Ryley Walker, it's as close to jazz as rock: virtuosity not as a means of showing off, but approaching the sublime."[^6]

"Math rock" translated into Chinese is "mathematical rock," tracing its origins to the late 1980s American bands Slint and Don Caballero. Its characteristics include asymmetric time signatures (7/8, 11/8), irregular pauses, and counterpoint techniques.[^7] It sounds like the music of an engineering major, yet Elephant Gym's sound is unexpectedly gentle—KT's bass is bright, Jiucun's drums are dry, and Jiuxiang's guitar often retreats to the background as a supporting color. The overall feel has a jazz fusion sense of breathing. When you watch them live, you'll notice something special: during complex rhythm switches, the three members look at each other. That eye contact is how they align the 7/8 and 11/8 sections—recordings can be aligned with a click track, but live performances can only rely on muscle memory and eye contact. That's how 13 years of chemistry is forged.

Spotify later directly selected them as the cover artist for their global Math Rock playlist.[^8] For a Taiwanese band to become the visual face of what Spotify uses to explain "math rock" to the world—if this had happened in the early 2010s, it would have read like science fiction.

## Brothers and Sisters and the Junior

inbox's memory often writes this band as "three siblings." Actually, no.

Zhang Kai-xiang (Tell) and Zhang Kai-ting (KT) are the brother and sister. The drummer, Tu Jia-qin, is Kai-xiang's junior from the music club at Kaohsiung Normal University Affiliated Senior High School[^10]. Their meeting point was Kaohsiung. When Kai-xiang was president of the music club, he set a rule: "You must have original songs to take the stage." Junior Tu Jia-qin grew up in that club office[^11]. Sister Kai-ting was in the neighboring Xiong-nu music club.

What brought these three together was the small ecosystem of music clubs at Kaohsiung's high school level. Kaohsiung Normal University Affiliated Senior High School and Xiong-nu were just a few streets apart in the Wenshan District. From rehearsing to performing, they came together to form a band, and had connections since their student days. Later, Kaohsiung produced bands like Elephant Gym, Fire!, Qianti, Crispy, Chair, and Barai, all linked to this music club network.

Mother was a music teacher, specializing in vocals and piano[^12]. The siblings were pushed into classical music training from an early age: Kai-ting started learning flute, violin, piano, and folk guitar in kindergarten[^13]. Kai-xiang also played piano as a child, later switching to guitar; Tu Jia-qin began learning percussion at age four and studied classical percussion for ten years[^14]. All three of them actually started from classical music, not rock. This background determined how their sound would develop later: they have obsessive-compulsive levels of pickiness about rhythm, counterpoint, and harmonic progressions.

All three later moved to Taipei for university. Kai-xiang studied Radio and Television at National Chengchi University (took five years), Kai-ting studied Sociology at National Taiwan University, and Tu Jia-qin studied at National Chengchi University[^15]. The timing of their move north coincided with the band's formation (February 2012[^1]). The early rehearsal scenes of Elephant Gym were almost like three students flying from Kaohsiung to Taipei for school, finding a tin-roof shed near National Chengchi University and rehearsing until midnight. Regarding how the band name came about, Kai-xiang's own revelation in 2026 is more amusing. Initially, Kai-ting suggested "Cherry Wine Party," which the guys found too awkward and rejected; Tu Jia-qin, inspired by the cartoon _Doraemon_, suggested "Gymnastics"; Kai-ting then threw out "Baby Gymnastics," which was also rejected because "guys absolutely cannot accept being called baby"; later, somehow they associated it with the _Doraemon_ episode where the protagonist draws an elephant for his genitals, and named it "Elephant Gym"[^16].

Kai-xiang's self-assessment: "The truth is just a very crooked story."[^16]

Of course, the official explanation given to the outside world is not this version. Wikipedia records it as: "Elephant" symbolizing bass-driven melodies, and "gym" referring to the special rhythm in the song[^17]. Both versions are kept, for a more complete picture.

## First Hiatus

Many people thought that the 2024 announcement was Elephant Gym's first hiatus. In fact, it wasn't.

In June 2014, they released their first album, 《Angle》. The production was done in Japan, where they found toe's guitarist, Miho Noboru, to mix and master the album[^18]. This collaboration later became a link between Elephant Gym and toe's aesthetic, and starting in August 2014, they began touring across China, Hong Kong, Taiwan, Japan, and the Asia-Pacific region. Entrusting the mixing of their debut album to toe, a textbook-level math rock band in Japan, was itself a declaration: we are not here to be a band in the Chinese-language market.

Then in November 2014, they announced they would go on hiatus. The reason was simple: the guys had to do military service.

The final show was called "See You Then" in February 2015. In 2016, after being discharged from the military, Kaiting returned to Kaohsiung to continue making music[^19]. In August of the same year, they were invited to perform at Summer Sonic in Chiba, Japan, and the Japanese release of 《Angle》 was issued simultaneously[^20]. Just after the hiatus ended, they were immediately thrust into one of Japan's largest music festivals, a speed that is rare in the history of Taiwan's indie scene going international.

After returning to Kaohsiung in 2016, Kaiting said, "What I most want to do might be a bridge."[^21] This band's dedication to the Japanese scene began with their debut album, and subsequent collaborations with toe, Tsugiya Mizuno, Flake Sounds, and cinema staff all followed this line. As a freshman in college, Kaiting founded the "Shallow Sound Music and Arts Camp," which by 2026 had reached its tenth edition, nurturing new-generation Taiwanese indie bands like Sidewalk, Haze, and DSPS. The systematic integration work he began in college was already underway.

2014 was also the time they collaborated with Lin Yuyu. They were special guests on "Shape of Mouth," not the often-mistaken "Lie."[^22] They performed as a trio on stages in Hong Kong's Hung Hom and Taipei's Taipei Arena. Lin Yuyu's choice to have an instrumental trio as special guests was also an interesting marker in the 2014 Chinese-language pop scene.

## Fuji Rock's Red Stage, 30 Minutes

On Sunday, July 31, 2022, the final day of Fuji Rock, at the Niigata Yuzawa Ski Resort in the mountains of Niigata.

Elephant Gym took the Red Marquee stage, the third-largest stage at Fuji Rock, which can accommodate five to six thousand people[^23]. They had originally been invited in 2020, but COVID delayed them by two years, and they finally got to perform in 2022. Another band from Kaohsiung was also on the lineup that year: Fire!. Both southern Taiwanese bands made it onto Japan's largest rock festival that same year—a sight rarely seen in the history of Taiwanese indie music.

Fuji Rock holds a special place in the hearts of Taiwanese indie music fans. Since 1999, it has been held at the Yuzawa Ski Resort in Niigata, attracting 100,000 people who spend three days on the mountain every late July. The number of Taiwanese bands that have managed to perform a full set on the main stage is fewer than can be counted on one hand. From this perspective, those 30 minutes on the afternoon of July 31, 2022, mark a coordinate point in the history of Taiwanese indie music.

It was a rainy afternoon. Kai-xing spoke on stage:

> "It is a great honor to be here at Fuji Rock today. I really enjoy the feeling of experiencing life, music, and unity with everyone at a music festival." "I hope that when you encounter negative things—whether it's war or the pandemic—you don't forget the things you believe in."[^24]

That summer was just five months after Russia's invasion of Ukraine, and COVID had not yet fully retreated. For those words to come from the mouth of a Taiwanese three-piece band without a lead vocalist, the entire framing suddenly became very concrete.

## The Taiwanese Band at Camp Flog Gnaw 13:20

Rewinding three years. November 9, 2019, Los Angeles, Dodger Stadium.

That was the 8th edition of Camp Flog Gnaw, hosted by Tyler, the Creator, with headliners including Solange, H.E.R., and A$AP Rocky that year. Elephant Gym was scheduled on the Flog Stage at 13:20 on the first day, one of the opening acts of the day[^26].

Camp Flog Gnaw is one of the most important enthusiast music festivals in the American hip-hop/R&B scene. Tyler, the Creator has been curating since the Odd Future era; his taste in bands spans genres and regions. Booking a Taiwanese instrumental trio without a vocalist in such a lineup is itself an aesthetic statement.

The performance was later covered in detail by Los Angeles music critics and college radio stations. A reporter from Harvard University's radio station WHRB wrote: "shredded, grooved, and blew the performance out of the water," adding "like a trio of puppeteers." The Ones to Watch review directly stated "the jam session to end all jam sessions"[^27].

On stage, KT said two lines that later became band memes:

> "the most important part of an instrumental rock band? The alcohol! ... We don't know why we're here!"[^28]

"We don't know why we're here." A Taiwanese band without a vocalist, scheduled by Tyler, the Creator to open at the LA Dodger Stadium. That year's lineup also included 65daysofstatic, Polyphia, and Covet. Three weeks later, they flew to Chicago to record Audiotree Live[^29].

2019 was the most intensive year of Elephant Gym's international tour. March: SXSW; August: UK ArcTangent; November: LA Camp Flog Gnaw; in between, a North American tour and the official Topshelf Records tour. Topshelf signed them to their roster in December 2017, reissued the three old albums _Angle_, _Balance_, and _Work_ on colored vinyl in 2018, and released the new album _Underwater_ (TSR201) in November 2018. The two years following their signing to Topshelf marked the explosive period of this band's international exposure.

## Three SXSW Appearances and a Speech

SXSW (South by Southwest) is the annual indie music pilgrimage in Austin, Texas, every March. Elephant Gym attended three times.

The first time in 2019 was their physical debut. On March 16, they performed at the Pearl Street Coop showcase, and the day before, March 15, they participated in the Math Rock Times Fest. In 2022, during the pandemic, it was the online Taiwan Beats Showcase, sharing the stage with Mong Tong and Apologized Youth. In 2023, they had two shows: March 16 at Cheer Up Charlie's for the Topshelf Records official showcase, and the Taiwan Beats six-hour showcase at Elysium[^30].

At the 2023 Elysium show, Kaiting delivered a speech using a translation script, leaving behind a line later quoted by _Austin Chronicle_:

> "Taiwan is the homeland for dreamers like you and me. We not only dream, but also make dreams come true."[^31]

The other acts sharing the stage that day were 9m88 and Lucy. Taiwan Beats is a Ministry of Culture-sponsored initiative to promote Taiwanese music internationally, but within less than a year after that show, the band would announce a long-term hiatus. No one in Texas that afternoon in March 2023 knew that yet.

That August, they flew to the UK to perform at ArcTangent. Held at Fernhill Farm, Bristol, it is considered one of the most important festivals in the global post-rock/math rock scene. The lineup included Meshuggah, Coheed and Cambria, Battles, 65daysofstatic, Polyphia, Cult of Luna, Three Trapped Tigers, and Cult of Luna[^32]. Taiwanese bands that managed to squeeze into that lineup that year were only them. For math rock fans, ArcTangent is a holy site; performing there signifies recognition by the genre's internal community.

## The Seven Minutes That Changed Everything

If you don't listen to indie music, you might have first heard of Elephant Gym on the night of December 31, 2023, around 9 p.m., through a television broadcast.

It was the 2024 Kaohsiung Dream Mall New Year's Eve concert. When it was Elephant Gym's turn to take the stage, around 9 p.m., the crowd below began to stir. KT suddenly brought his mouth close to the microphone and shouted:

> "Someone's holding a knife, FXXK" "Be careful with safety before leaving the stage"[^33]

The live broadcast was cut for about seven minutes[^34]. At 21:12, the venue returned to calm. Overnight, the four characters "Elephant Gym" shifted from the vocabulary of indie music fans to headlines across Taiwan.

The next day, January 1, KT wrote a post on IG Stories explaining the situation at the time:

> "First I heard the crowd pushing and shoving, then through my earpiece I heard someone say 'someone's holding a knife,' followed by the command to 'get off the stage.' I felt extremely unhappy and very distressed."[^35]

The band's official statement was measured: "Thank you all for your concern. The actual situation at the venue will be addressed by the organizing unit. We hope everyone takes note of their personal safety when participating in large-scale events, and wish everyone a smooth and safe 2024."[^36]

Later, the Kaohsiung City Government's Xinzheng Substation explained: 35 people were injured from pushing and shoving, with no one hurt by a knife; the "knife" referred to by witnesses was actually a pen[^37]. A man in a white hat later came forward to give his statement. The city government's supportive response was as follows: "The incident on New Year's Eve is unrelated to any performing group... We urge everyone not to blame the performing groups."[^38]

On social media, people split into two camps. One side felt the band overreacted and affected the New Year's atmosphere; the other believed that KT's judgment at the time, with incomplete information, was already a responsible choice. Both sides voiced their opinions, and so did the police's reconstruction. As for how those seven minutes should be remembered, there is still no consensus version.

For Elephant Gym, those seven minutes became a mirror of their identity. Before that, they were known in Taiwan's indie scene as "the band that performed on the Fuji Rock red stage," "the Taiwanese band signed to Topshelf," and "the band that performed with Jun Tawada on bass." Afterward, to most people in Taiwan, they became "the band that shouted about someone holding a knife during New Year's." International music critics understand them as instrumental virtuosos, but Taiwanese society first came to know them through a news event. This gap is not unique to Elephant Gym—it reflects the dual nature of many Taiwanese indie bands, both locally and internationally.

## 9m88, Lin Shengxiang, and Kaiting: Three Curves of Collaboration

Starting with _Water_ (2018), Elephant Gym began incorporating vocals into their arrangements, doing so through collaboration—each song bringing in a different singer.

The 2022 album _Dreams_ represents the peak of this approach. The track "Shadow" features jazz-pop vocalist 9m88, with the song title drawing from Jung's "shadow theory"; "Dreaming to You" brings in Hakka folk singer Lin Shengxiang, whose lyrics were largely written for his deceased father around the time of Master Tu Ch’i-ch’in’s passing[^39]. The same album also invited the Kaohsiung Municipal Wind Band to perform "Wings" and the Jiutian Folk Arts Troupe to perform "Feast of the Gods"[^40]. The Jiutian segment places temple fair drum formations within a math rock framework—an uncommon cross-cultural experiment in Taiwan indie.

Kaiting rates _Dreams_ as "finally reaching a state where technique and imagination are relatively balanced." The first three albums leaned more toward technicality; this one begins pulling emotion and narrative back in. NME gave it considerable coverage, positioning it as the turning point where Elephant Gym expanded outward from the math rock genre.

Kaiting explained the logic of collaboration to NME:

> "The most interesting thing to do is to find other musicians to break down your world. We should learn the culture of others first, then we'll talk about how to work and create a new world together."[^41]

Breaking the world apart, then reassembling it.

On December 14, 2023, they released their fourth album _World_, a commemorative work marking their 10th anniversary as a band[^42]. The collaboration list on this album is one leap beyond the next: "Name" features Tokyo Jiutai bassist Kaiting on dual bass—a first for Elephant Gym in the dual-bass format; "Jhalleyaa" collaborates with Indian Uttar Pradesh singer Shashaa Tirupati; "Feather" brings in both Badu and Japanese creator TENDRE; "Happy Prince" features Lin Yi-le[^43]. With singers lined up from Taiwan, Japan, and India, the album title _World_ truly lives up to its name, genuinely bringing people in.

Kaiting’s collaborative thread can be traced back to the Zepp Premium concert on February 23, 2023. Zepp New Taipei featured SKY-HI and Kaiting performing together[^44]. The dual-bass track "Name" was recorded after that show. Placing KT and Tokyo Jiutai’s bassist in the same song to converse—this production decision itself is Elephant Gym telling everyone: their bass is on equal footing with Asia’s strongest bassists, with no intention of being mere accompaniment.

## The Argument in the Documentary

The "World THE WORLD" tour wrapped up in 2024, totaling nearly 60 shows across Asia, the Americas, and Europe[^46].

At the flagship show in November, KT spoke those words on stage:

> "Starting next year, Elephant Gym will enter a long hiatus."[^2]

Thirteen years. From February 2012 in Kaohsiung to November 2024 in Taipei.

Almost simultaneously, director Alulu Kuo's documentary _Elephant Gym: More Real than Dreams_ was confirmed for release. At 95 minutes, it follows the band from its 2012 formation through to just before the 2024 tour, with KT serving as producer[^47]. It premiered in January 2026 in Tokyo and Kyoto, Japan, and on February 6th at the in89 Premium Cinema in Taipei[^48].

The least Taiwan-indie aspect of this film is that it doesn't package the band as heroes. It focuses the camera on a major dispute among band members in 2023, right before their world tour, when they were on the verge of breaking up. Tu Chia-ch'in's forced departure from another band, the accumulated tensions between siblings as both family and work partners, are all included in the edit[^49].

Topshelf Records directly wrote in the documentary's promotional copy: "In 2023, Elephant Gym was preparing to embark on a world tour—yet the band stood on the brink of breaking up."[^50] A band about to set off on a world tour, simultaneously standing at the edge of disbandment. KT once said during the tour, later cited by United Daily News: "Actually, when I go home often, I just lie in bed and cry."[^51]

Contrasted with the intensive period of classical music training pushed by their mother in childhood, their teenage years in the brass societies at National Taiwan Normal University Affiliated High School and National Taiwan University, and the dense international touring schedule after signing with Topshelf, this breakdown on the road is another side of the band they've never publicly revealed. Taiwan indie band documentaries typically follow the "we worked hard" inspirational narrative, but _More Real than Dreams_ chose to include the moment of "we can't hold on anymore" in its edit—this decision itself demonstrates Elephant Gym's maturity as a band.

The documentary soundtrack was released simultaneously by Topshelf on February 3, 2026, featuring dooodooo, Wu Pei-ling, and members of the Kaohsiung City Government Brass Band[^52].

## A Long Hiatus

June 29, 2024, the 35th Golden Melody Awards Judges' Award.

Judges' chair Chen Zihong's speech:

> "They perform around the world, letting the world see Taiwan." "Step by step toward the world, unbound by frameworks, fusing musical elements, upholding creativity." "The Judges' Award is not given every year; it requires deliberation by the judges and a two-thirds majority vote."[^53]

Kaiting's speech when accepting the award later became the most quoted line of that year's Golden Melody Awards:

> "No matter how weird the music you play, once you go abroad, you'll discover there are many like-minded people willing to come watch you perform — and the same is true in Taiwan. So, whatever music you want to make, just make it!"[^53]

The band won the Golden Melody Award for Best Band three times: 5th Edition _Angle_ — Best Style/Genre Album (2014), 10th Edition _Underwater_ — Best Band (2019), 15th Edition _World_ — Best Band (2024); KT herself won Best Instrumentalist at the 13th Edition (2022)[^54]. When KT accepted Best Instrumentalist, she brought fellow nominee Fang Q up to the stage, promising to split the prize money with Fang Q, who had been her teacher, and mentioned how Fang Q had once been ridiculed for her bass skills when she first started, only to now "redeem herself."[^55] From being ridiculed as a female bassist to becoming Fender Taiwan's first female bass ambassador and winning a Golden Melody Award for Best Instrumentalist, this identity-political arc is worth remembering in the history of Taiwan's band scene.

On March 18, 2020, KT became Fender Taiwan's first female bass ambassador and was featured in Japan's _Bass Magazine_.[^56] A girl who learned flute, violin, piano, and folk guitar from kindergarten grew up to become one of the most important bass icons in Taiwan's indie scene.

From February 2012 to November 2024, the band toured 23 countries across 60 shows, attended SXSW three times, performed on the Red Stage at Fuji Rock, Camp Flog Gnaw, ArcTangent, Audiotree Live, received Pitchfork's glowing reviews, and released a complete vinyl reissue with Topshelf. Thirteen years, three students from Kaohsiung, on stage they could lock into complex rhythms just by looking at each other — that's thirteen years of muscle memory.

On the day they announced their long-term hiatus, they did not frame it as a breakup. Kaiting spoke of "a long hiatus," not "disbandment." The same band took a hiatus in 2014, and that time it ended because the guys went off to serve their military duty and came back. This time, it's the fatigue and strain accumulated over 13 years of touring — between siblings, between band members. The conditions for returning are different. Whether they'll reunite in 10 years, no one knows.

The documentary is titled _More Real Than Dreams_. The path they walked was even longer than what they imagined lying by the apartment window during their university days. A three-piece band no one was listening to, only seen by the whole island when they announced their disbandment. But the bass lines, the asymmetric time signatures, and the performance footage left behind at Camp Flog Gnaw and Fuji Rock will continue to speak for themselves in their absence.
