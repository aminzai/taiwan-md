> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwanese rap is often treated as a "Chinese cover of American hip-hop," but the real story is the exact opposite. Chinese is a tonal language, described as "innately unsuitable for rap"; Taiwanese Hoklo has seven tones plus tone sandhi, once considered unfit for the mainstream stage; the main force of Taiwanese rap consists of urban middle-class men with backgrounds in National Taiwan University’s electrical engineering, chemistry, and sociology departments, completely mismatching the American hip-hop narrative of "weapons of the ghetto poor." These three "disadvantages" have been turned into features one by one—mother tongues became the accent of resistance, high academic credentials redefined what "real" means, and the diversity of ideologies, ethnicities, and languages has created a voice distinct from the one across the strait. From the 1989 album _Madness Songs_ to MC HotDog winning the Golden Melody Award for Best Male Artist in 2024, this article tells how Taiwan turned three innate disadvantages into a sovereign voiceprint that only this island can sing.

In May 2004, the 15th Golden Melody Awards (GMA) gave the Best Lyrics award to _Life's a Struggle_. The person who took the stage to accept the award was not the creator: Song Yueh-ting had passed away two years earlier from multiple myeloma at the age of 23, and his mother and brother accepted it on his behalf[^1]. The same year, the other nominees for Best Lyrics included Jay Chou’s _Terrace Farm_, Vincent Fang’s _Broken East Wind_, and Faye Wong’s _No Regrets_[^2]. An underground rapper who never lived to hear himself win had defeated the hottest names in the Mandarin music scene at that time.

This is a very Taiwanese opening: the first rap work recognized by the establishment had its creator already gone. And the people who truly brought rap into Taiwanese living rooms were not anyone on the Golden Melody Awards stage. To understand Taiwanese hip-hop, you must first discard a convenient prejudice: "Taiwanese rap is just copying the US." This narrative is convenient, but it reverses the causality. What makes Taiwanese rap most unique is precisely the points where it is **not** like the US: Chinese tones, the Taiwanese dialect accent, and a group of creators who are not the poor. This island turned three innate disadvantages into a voice that only it itself can sing.

## Chinese Was Never Meant to Rap

Let’s start with a technical issue. The core of rap is rhythm and rhyme. English is a stress-timed language; where the stress falls and how syllables are broken naturally carries a drumbeat. Chinese is not. Chinese is a tonal language; the rise and fall of each character determines **meaning**: the same "sh" sound, across four tones, represents four different characters. When you quickly cram characters into a beat, pursuing the English-style flow, the tones are easily flattened, resulting in "hardly knowing what is being sung." From this perspective, Chinese is indeed "innately unsuitable for rap."

How did Taiwanese rappers respond to this dilemma? The answer lies with Song Yueh-ting. DJ KU from the NTU Hip-Hop Culture Research Society described him as the "pioneer of double-rhyme techniques," with phrasing and vocal styles approximating Tupac[^3]. Double rhyme involves rhyming two sets of end-rhymes simultaneously within a single line, allowing Chinese rhythm to rely on **rhyme density** rather than tones to sustain the beat. This is the first solution to bypass the disadvantage: since tones cannot naturally produce drumbeats like English stress, the rhythmic gap is filled with denser rhyming.

![A memorial portrait of rapper Song Yueh-ting, a young face with a quiet expression.](/article-images/music/sung-yueh-ting-portrait.webp)

_Song Yueh-ting (1978–2002), aged 23. His posthumous work *Life's a Struggle* pushed Chinese rap forward with double rhymes, winning the 15th Golden Melody Award for Best Lyrics in 2004. Memorial portrait, fair use editorial commentary._

Twenty years later, this path was pushed to a nearly obsessive extreme. Xiong Zai (Bear Zai): Born Xiong Xin-kan, a bachelor’s degree in Electrical Engineering and a master’s in Telecommunications from National Taiwan University, and former president of the NTU Hip-Hop Culture Research Society: He simply treated rhyming as a science that can be modeled. His own explanation is: "Through prosody research and establishing mathematical models, I understood that prosody is a multi-layered structure... placing lyrics in positions that sound pleasing to the ear."[^4] He is known in the circle as the "President of Infinite Rhymes," relying on this method of deconstructing multi-layered rhyming into mathematical structures. His track _88BARS_, featuring dense rhyming across 88 bars (lines) in a single song, won him the 33rd (2022) Golden Melody Award for Best Lyrics[^5].

> 📝 **Curator’s Note**
> Popular online science explanations often say "Taiwanese rappers worked hard to overcome the problem that Chinese isn't suitable for rap." But the closer truth is that they did not "overcome" it; they changed the rules. English rap generates rhythm through stress; Taiwanese rappers generate rhythm through rhyme density—it is less like running slower on the same path and more like taking a fork into a different path. Xiong Zai’s mathematical rhyming and Song Yueh-ting’s double rhymes are essentially doing the same thing: rewriting a "Chinese can't do this" problem into a "how Chinese can do this" problem. The moment a disadvantage becomes a feature often happens in this rewrite.

The question of "whether Chinese is suitable for rap" has never had consensus within Taiwan. Xiong Zai’s _88BARS_, containing a large amount of English segments, was questioned by some listeners as "isn't it still relying on English." Meanwhile, the existence of Dark Lo’s Taiwanese Hoklo works and the entire genre of Taiwanese rap rap is used by another faction as counter-evidence, arguing that Mandarin-language structures can absolutely rap[^6]. The debate itself is part of Taiwanese hip-hop: a group of people serious enough to argue about "whether language can" proves that this matters here.

## The Seven Tones of Taiwanese Hoklo: Bug or Feature?

If Mandarin’s four tones are a rap challenge, the situation for Taiwanese Hoklo is even more subtle. Taiwanese Hoklo has seven tones, plus "tone sandhi" (連讀變調): a character’s tone automatically changes when read alone versus when read following another character. In the standard framework of Mandarin pop songs, this was once considered an "informal accent, unfit for the mainstream stage."

But fishLIN from the group KhoAukKhun turned this completely around. His explanation is direct: "Even just speaking Hoklo has melody."[^7] Hoklo is Taiwanese Hoklo. Manchuker from the same group used a more vivid metaphor: Taiwanese Hoklo’s seven tones are "circular," while Mandarin’s four tones are "square": meaning the tonal curves of Taiwanese Hoklo are smoother and have more起伏 (ups and downs), actually giving flow extra phonetic variation space[^7].

In the research of US scholar Meredith Schweig, this practice of treating mother-tongue accents as creative assets is categorized as "resistance vernaculars": post-authoritarian Taiwanese rappers use their mother tongue to turn rap into a "creative political intervention"[^9]. In other words, the "accent" of Taiwanese Hoklo is not a bug to be corrected; it is the feature.

This thread of mother tongues is actually older than hip-hop itself. In November 1989, Blacklist Studio released _Madness Songs_ (抓狂歌), stitching together the Taiwanese "za-liam-á" (a traditional chanting style close to rapping), rap, and reggae, becoming the precursor to the "New Taiwanese Language Movement"[^10]. Taiwan already had its own chanting roots: the "liām-kua" (唸歌) accompanied by the moon lute is a chanting tradition spanning hundreds of years. So when rap arrived in Taiwan, this already-existing local narrative bloodline became the soil for it to take root[^9].

The one who pushed Taiwanese Hoklo the furthest and most politically is Dwagie (Tseng Kuan-jung, from Tainan). His 2002 album _Tongue Blossoming Lotus_ (舌燦蓮花) was one of the last albums released before Mo Yan Records ceased operations, often called the first full-rap album in the Mandarin-speaking circle[^11]. In his Taiwanese Hoklo representative work _[Taiwan SONG](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=mn_ptfQDOJA)_, he directly treats the Taiwanese accent as Taiwan’s sonic mark.

On this same mother-tongue path, Hakka also has its practitioners. Yappy (Tseng Yi-bin, from Zhuolan, Miaoli) wrote _Trap Miaoli_ in Hakka at age 16, connecting the cold beats of British Drill with the Hakka accent; he stated heavily: "Hakka is the existence closest to my deepest, most core self."[^12]

Listening to these accents together reveals what makes Taiwanese hip-hop most different from a single-language market: on this island, rap has never been limited to one language. Mandarin, Taiwanese Hoklo, Hakka, and the sixteen indigenous languages are all present.

The representative of the indigenous thread is Abao (Aljenljeng Tjaluvie, from Paiming Village, Jinfeng, Taitung). Although her album _kinakaian Mother’s Tongue_ is positioned as indigenous electronic pop rather than pure rap, her winning the 31st (2020) Golden Melody Award for Album of the Year is itself a signal: Taiwan’s mainstream music industry is willing to give an award to a mother-tongue album that many do not understand. Abao’s acceptance speech is worth recording word for word[^13]:

> **✦** "Thanks to all the listeners who pressed the PLAY button to share, thank you for trying to listen to a language you do not understand."

## A Microphone for a Group of NTU Students

The second disadvantage is hidden deeper and is most counter-intuitive. The prototype narrative of American hip-hop is the weapon of the ghetto poor: growing from the streets of South Bronx, speaking of the anger, survival, and resistance of the underclass. If following this template, Taiwanese rap "should" come from the social bottom. But the reality is exactly the opposite.

The main force of Taiwanese rap consists of middle-class, highly educated, urban Han men. Xiong Zai is from NTU Electrical Engineering. Guo Dan (Yang Kuo-chun) attended Tainan No. 1 High School and NTU Chemistry[^14]. Leo Wang (Wang Zhi-you) studied at NTU Sociology before dropping out. Even the anthropologist Lin Hao-li, who explained this phenomenon most thoroughly, is himself an Associate Professor of Anthropology at National Tsing Hua University and the second president of the NTU Hip-Hop Culture Research Society[^15]. When asked why Taiwanese hip-hop is so different from the US, his answer was nearly self-deprecating: "Because we are just a group of teenagers living relatively well-off."[^16]

This sentence punctures a fact many are unwilling to admit. When your creators are mostly not "the poor," then the core value of hip-hop—"keep it real" (maintain authenticity)—must be redefined. You cannot pretend you come from the ghetto; that is fake. But you also have authenticity to speak of. Leo Wang gave a beautiful answer: Creators being true to their thoughts and feelings, and realistically reflecting their values, is keeping it real[^17]. A thesis from National Taiwan Normal University on "Battles of Orthodoxy" also points out that Taiwanese rappers reconstruct the concept of the "streets" within local social spaces, rather than copying the American street imagination of violence and gangs[^18]. Thus, real has a new meaning: it measures the degree of honesty, unrelated to where you come from.

> 📝 **Curator’s Note**
> Here lies an unspoken choice. Taiwanese rappers could easily have copied the US, packaging themselves as more "street" and more "dangerous"—this is the safest shortcut, just copy and paste. But most did not. A group of NTU students chose to admit they are NTU students, and then asked "what is real" under this premise. Lin Hao-li’s sentence "we are just a group of teenagers living relatively well-off" is important because of its honesty—and honesty is precisely the real they redefined. The place where Taiwanese hip-hop is least like the US is precisely where it grew its own legitimacy.

This also gave Taiwanese hip-hop a temperament rarely seen elsewhere. Lin Hao-li has a sentence that could serve as a footnote for the entire article: "In other music fields, it is hard to see such complex ideological entanglement; hip-hop music can see the changes and mixtures of the entire country and society."[^19] A group of highly educated people playing with this brings in not just technique, but a large amount of reading, critical thinking, and self-awareness. The academic nature is both a feature of Taiwanese hip-hop and, as we will see later, an unexpected entry point for some.

## Temple Fairs, Dorm Networks, and Riverside Lyrics

For any subculture to grow, material conditions are needed: a place where enthusiasts can find each other. In the underground era of Taiwanese hip-hop, this "place" had several layers.

The earliest layer was online. In 1998, Lin Hao-li, then a high school student, and classmates set up a message board called "Master U," Taiwan’s first hip-hop online community[^20]. Two years later, in 2000, the NTU Hip-Hop Culture Research Society was established: the first semester was an "illegal club" (not officially approved)[^21]. This was an era of sharing pirated CDs via dorm networks, hunting for cassettes in Guanghua Mall, and self-teaching by devouring American original albums one by one. Physical bases were livehouses and music festivals: Riverside Lyrics (He’an Liuyan), Kenting’s Spring Breeze Music Festival, places where underground people had stages to stand on.

However, a common impression must be broken first: the assumption that Taiwan’s "general audience" grew up listening to MC HotDog and Dwagie. The ones who truly brought rap into temple fairs, night markets, betel nut stalls, and taxi sound systems were actually Nine One One.

Nine One One was established in Taichung in September 2009. The three members: Chun Feng (Hung Yu-hung), Yang Cong (Chen Hao-yu), and Jian Zhi (Liao Chien-chih) started from temple fair stages. Their 2012 track _[Devoted Man_ (癡情的男子漢)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=UdnSKw0wI8I) surged to over 80 million views on YouTube, becoming thoroughly popular in the grassroots market[^22]. In 2015, they founded their own label, Mixed Blood Entertainment. Media estimates suggest that eleven years of commercial performances generated approximately 500 million in value[^23]. This is a world completely different from the literary youth of Yanshe (Gen Yue): one side is poetic, allusion-heavy rap; the other is the maximum traffic of temple grassroots. The true map of Taiwanese hip-hop has never had only one look.

## Four Ways of Growing Up

After the underground scene matured, Taiwanese hip-hop grew several labels with completely different temperaments. Each represents an answer to "how to continue doing rap."

The most literary is Yanshe (KAO!INC.). Founded in 2005 in Tamsui by Di La-pang (Chang Yi-sheng), it follows a poetic, jazz, boom bap route[^24]. Yanshe’s most representative artist is Dark Lo (Du Zhen-xi, from Tainan, Department of Visual Communication at Yunlin University of Science and Technology), who stayed with Yanshe for approximately fourteen years. His 2010 _Moonlight_ collaborated with the Japanese jazz band JABBERLOOP, connecting the live feel of Japanese jazz into hip-hop; his 2020 _Home Cooking Music_ won the 32nd (2021) Golden Melody Awards for Best Mandarin Male Artist and Best Mandarin Album[^25].

On the day Dark Lo accepted the award, he dedicated the award to a name most viewers in front of TVs might not recognize: "Today I want to dedicate this award to my family and relatives, to the brothers of Zhu Bang and Yanshe, to Taiwanese hip-hop, and to Brother Bao in heaven."[^26]

Brother Bao is MV director Huang Hsin-chia, known in the circle as "Da Bao Dao." In the early days of Taiwanese hip-hop, he shot MVs for names not yet seen by the mainstream like Dwagie, Dark Lo, and Can Pi; the visuals for MC HotDog and Tseng Chen-yue’s duet _I Love Taiwan Girls_ were also from his hand; he passed away from colon cancer in 2011 at age 37[^55]. Ten years later, when Dark Lo stood on the GMA stage, the first person he thought of was this person behind the lens who held the scene together.

The most commercially mainstream is Ben Se Music (Original Color Music), founded in 2004 in Taipei by Tseng Chen-yue and Huang Jing-bo, featuring MC HotDog and顽童 (JWJ) as its招牌 (signboards), following a brotherhood-style commercial route[^27]. JWJ (MJ116: MJ for Muzha, 116 for Wenshan District postal code): Shou Zi E.SO (Chen Yu-jung), Xiao Chun Kenzy (Chou Wen-chieh), and Da Yuan Muta (Lin Mu-yuan) three members: Their 2017 _Do Big Things_ won the 29th (2018) Golden Melody Award for Best Vocal Group, representing the new generation of idol-style rap[^28].

The one with the most social practice color is Ren Ren You Gong Lian (Everyone Has Power Practice), founded in 2003 in Tainan by Dwagie. Due to its strong local consciousness and collectivism, it is described as "Taiwan’s Wu-Tang"[^29]. The most grassroots is the Mixed Blood Entertainment mentioned earlier: Nine One One’s temple empire. Yanshe and Ben Se in the north, Ren Ren You Gong Lian in Tainan, Mixed Blood in Taichung: This label map itself is a geographic slice of Taiwanese hip-hop: poetic, commercial, social practice, grassroots, four ways of growing up each occupying a corner.

> 📝 **Curator’s Note**
> Looking at these four labels together reveals an easily overlooked fact—Taiwanese hip-hop’s "diversity" has been in its bones from day one, an出厂 (factory-out) setting. Literary youth and temple fairs, Taipei and Tainan, social practice and commercial idols, things that might be cut into different "subgenres" and live in separate hills never interacting in the US, grew up simultaneously, side-by-side, and knowing each other in Taiwan. When Dark Lo accepted the GMA, he thought of Da Bao Dao who shot MVs for the entire scene; when Xiong Zai went to Dwagie’s talent show as a mentor—this circle is so small that all routes know each other. Diversity is not a slogan here; it is the size here.

This circle also once extended its tentacles across the strait. In 2015, Tseng Chen-yue, MC HotDog, and JWJ formed "Brothers Original Color" in Beijing, under Rock Records, but disbanded in 2017[^30]. This interlude brings us exactly to the most sensitive part of Taiwanese hip-hop history, and the segment that best illustrates the pillar of "sovereignty."

## From Pirated CDs to Golden Melody King

Back to that line from underground to the establishment. Taiwanese hip-hop spent twenty years, from pirated CDs all the way to the Golden Melody King. This is a long campaign about "legitimacy."

The Golden Melody Awards never had an independent hip-hop category; all rap works had to clash with the entire Mandarin music scene in general categories (hip-hop’s independent category was in the Jin Yin Creative Awards established in 2010)[^31]. So every win is a test of "has the mainstream acknowledged you?" Laying out this timeline of recognition, we see a clear trajectory:

```tw-timeline
2004 | Song Yueh-ting *Life's a Struggle* | 15th GMA Best Lyrics, Posthumous, Accepted by Mother and Brother
2007 | MC HotDog *Wake Up* | 18th GMA Best Mandarin Album, Underground Godfather Enters Mainstream
2013 | Miss Ko | 24th GMA Best New Artist, First Female Rap Artist to Win This Award
2018 | JWJ MJ116 *Do Big Things* | 29th GMA Best Vocal Group, New Generation Idol Rap
2019 | Leo Wang + ØZI | 30th, Leo Wang First Rap King, ØZI Best New Artist, Same-Year Double Hit
2022 | Xiong Zai *88BARS* | 33rd GMA Best Lyrics, Mathematical Rhyming Peaks
2024 | MC HotDog *Dirty Artist* | 35th GMA Best Mandarin Male Artist, Underground Godfather Finally Wins King
Source: Historical GMA, CNA
```

There are two closed loops on this line. The first is in June 2019: Leo Wang won the 30th Golden Melody Award for Best Mandarin Male Artist with _Moaning Without Illness, Lyrical With Affection_, becoming the **first Rap King** in GMA history[^32]. In the same ceremony, ØZI (Chen Yi-fan, born 1997 in Los Angeles, returned to Taiwan at age two) won Best New Artist, two rappers each taking one award in one ceremony[^33]. Comparing to twenty years ago, when MC HotDog and Dwagie were still relying on American original albums imported from record stores, devouring them one by one to self-teach[^8]: This distance is the path Taiwanese hip-hop has walked.

The second closed loop is in 2024. MC HotDog (MC HotDog, born Yao Chung-jen, from Fu Jen Catholic University’s Department of Film and Television), with _Dirty Artist_, won the 35th Golden Melody Award for Best Mandarin Male Artist: his first King award, while also winning Best Lyrics[^34]. From debuting in 1998, to winning Best Mandarin Album with _Wake Up_ in 2007, to finally becoming King in 2024, the Godfather used twenty-six years to complete the last puzzle piece of establishment recognition.

But being acknowledged is not entirely a good thing. A comment from an underground perspective wrote clearly: The Golden Melody Awards "gave hip-hop a stage, but also invisibly changed its outline," and "this is not evolution, but a cost"[^35]. When a music growing from underground is co-opted by the mainstream, it exchanges for a larger stage and more resources, but may also grind away its roughest, most untamed parts. This tension has no standard answer: it hangs there, a problem each person from pirated CDs to the GMA stage must face themselves.

## Who Is "Taiwan Already Had Hip-Hop" Shouted At?

In 2017, a talent show called _The Rap of China_ exploded on iQiyi, "Do you have freestyle?" became a viral phrase across the web. The most subtle part is the list itself: The four mentors that season, Pan Wei-bo (Wang Sicong? No, Wang Sicong is different. Wait, the text says 潘瑋柏 William Peng, 熱狗 MC HotDog, 張震嶽 Tseng Chen-yue), all from Taiwan, except Wu Yi-fan (Kris Wu), all were Taiwanese faces[^36]. Taiwanese rappers were already top-ranking in the Mandarin hip-hop circle, but a show from the other side of the strait reversed to become the stage defining "Mandarin hip-hop."

The reaction within the Taiwan circle to this event split into two factions, and both had reasons. Di La-pang of Yanshe saw squeezing: "In the entire Mandarin hip-hop circle... originally Taiwanese rap artists... were top-ranking, but after _The Rap of China_, China cultivated a large number of rap stars, naturally squeezing the space for Taiwanese rap artists."[^37] Dwagie saw opportunity: Taiwan’s recent hip-hop boom is definitely related to _The Rap of China_, but he believes this is also a good thing; people will become interested and search for Taiwanese rap artists[^38]. The same phenomenon, one reads as a threat, one as exposure.

> 📝 **Curator’s Note**
> The phrase "Taiwan already had hip-hop" must be placed in its context to be understood. On the stage of the 29th GMA in 2018, Liu Fu-chu led a group of rappers in a performance of "Taiwan Already Had Hip-Hop"—on the surface it is pride, in the core it is anxiety. When the discourse power of "Mandarin hip-hop" is being redefined by a larger market and a hotter show, Taiwan needs to loudly say once "we already had it, and ours is different." Where is its difference? In all the things mentioned earlier: the multi-accent of Taiwanese Hoklo, Hakka, and indigenous languages, the mixture of ideologies and ethnicities, a group of highly educated people redefining real. Lin Hao-li’s words can be quoted again: "Taiwan’s ideological diversity, Taiwan’s ethnic diversity, Taiwan’s linguistic diversity, make hip-hop, a music that emphasizes multi-directional connections, present a more colorful and complex face in Taiwan." This diversity is the true capital under that pride—it makes Taiwanese hip-hop distinct from the single discourse across the strait, becoming a voiceprint that cannot be co-opted by any single market.

Dwagie pushed this "difference" to the furthest place. In January 2017, he performed _Taiwan SONG_ at the Brooklyn Nets’ "Taiwan Culture Night" on the NBA stage, becoming the first Taiwanese rapper to appear on an NBA stage[^39]. He was also the first Taiwanese rapper to invite the Dalai Lama into an MV (_Ren_ / Person), long discussing human rights, politics, and universal values in his songs[^40]. In 2016, a Chinese blacklist circulated online, listing him; Dwagie’s response was干脆 (crisp): "I often talk about human rights, politics, and universal values, which naturally conflicts with the CCP... I want to say my words, earning less money is fine."[^41]

Must be honest: The authenticity and scope of that blacklist are unverifiable due to conflicting official statements; the relationship between the Taiwan hip-hop circle and the other side, from developing there to being named on a blacklist, has a wide spectrum and diverse stances. Rather than picking sides, here we want to make people see one thing: When a music’s multi-accent itself is political, it is destined to have friction with a market pursuing a single discourse. This friction is the most concrete evidence of the "sovereign voiceprint" concept.

## Not Just a Man’s Microphone

The names mentioned above are almost all male. This reflects a real structure of Taiwanese hip-hop: it has long been a man’s microphone. The way female rappers break through in front of this microphone exactly照 (shines/reflects) the two pillars mentioned earlier from another angle.

Look at the entry point first. Male rappers rely on "street certification"—how hardcore you are, whether you can mix in, this threshold naturally keeps women out. But the entry point for Taiwanese female rappers is actually campus clubs. RapShark (Zhang Bo-han) comes from Cheng Kung University’s Black Sound (Black Music Club), Chen Xian-jing’s earliest works also appeared on Cheng Kung University’s Black Sound channel, and the NTU Hip-Hop Culture Research Society nurtured Yang Shu-ya[^42]. A BBC special video explains this line clearly: In Taiwan, university hip-hop clubs "occupy key positions in the industry"—it is the most important entry point for newcomers[^43].

But this club entry point itself carries contradiction: Female rappers interviewed described that entering club classes often means "the room is full of boys, in that moment you know you are the only girl," boys build默契 (tacit understanding) through "straight male jokes," and girls pretend not to hear to not break the atmosphere[^43]. The academic nature is a general feature of Taiwanese hip-hop; for women, it is simultaneously an entry point and the first wall.

Standing alone in this wall for nearly ten years is Miss Ko (Ko Zhong-shan, born 1985 in Queens, New York). Returning to Taiwan in 2011, in 2012 Yanshe released _Knock Out Ge Pi_, produced by Dark Lo; the following year she won the 24th (2013) Golden Melody Award for Best New Artist, becoming Taiwan’s first female hip-hop artist to win this award. The judges’ comment left a ruler: "Taiwan’s first hip-hop female artist, authentic and到位 (in place) hip-hop skills, performance stronger than male hip-hop artists, unmatched in the Mandarin music scene."[^44] This sentence is both a coronation and a ruler: the four words "never seen before" show how lonely she was. After her 2016 _[Queen of Queens_ (皇后區的皇后)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=z62cSYXwF4s), she remained one of the few female voices in that circle.

The real explosion was in early 2025. The fuse was a song released in November 2024 that went viral on Douyin, criticized as misogynistic, _Ai Ni Zhen De Mei Ban Fa_ (I Really Have No Way to Love You) (Yi Yi). On January 22, 2025, RapShark (Zhang Bo-han, born 2000 in Tainan, from Cheng Kung University’s Black Sound) released _[Zai Chao La Ji_ (Another Noise, Trash)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=wyjDlPigQ50) diss, becoming the first female rapper to strike head-on[^45].

Next, jumping out to support her was someone who originally had nothing to do with this circle war: [?te (Lin Chi-yi)]. She does not actually rap; she studied at the National Defense Medical College for nine years and won the 32nd Golden Melody Award for Best New Artist as a lo-fi R&B singer-songwriter. Precisely because of this, her criticism on Threads that those songs were "full of traditional male masculine culture" was particularly刺眼 (eye-catching) in the hip-hop circle’s unspoken rule of "not stepping on each other," and was immediately swarmed by出征 (attackers)[^46]. After being swarmed, she did not retreat, speaking more thoroughly on Threads: "I knew I was doing the right thing, what I am doing is also representing the core values of hip-hop."[^47]

The battlefield exploded on February 12, 2025, with a song. Yang Shu-ya (born 1999, NTU Political Science, from Hip-Hop Club, former Hualien elementary school teacher) released _[Rule Nan Freestyle_ (Rule Men Freestyle)](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=MHHHWAyq8qM), with the line "Real is not your misogynistic fig leaf" flipping the table directly[^48]. This song with no label resources surged to the StreetVoice Real-Time Champion in less than 24 hours, YouTube views approaching 100,000, even music critic Ma Shi-fang and writer Huang Li-qun reposted it[^49]. A girl from a campus club background, with no company, pierced through the music circle and literary circle with one song: This itself is the most powerful proof of Taiwanese hip-hop’s "academic entry point."

This debate is worth laying out both factions, because it is not black and white. One faction claims creative freedom, believing hip-hop inherently carries roughness and should not be over-examined; such voices exist inside and outside the circle; half a year later, _Rule Nan Freestyle_ was nominated for the Jin Yin Award, even political commentator Zhao Shao-kang joined the war, criticizing it as "extremely vulgar"[^50].

The other faction believes using discrimination as an excuse for creation is untenable. Yang Shu-ya asked directly: "If you want to express anger in music, why must you step on women’s feet to be angry?"[^46] Musician Lao Mo’s summary brought this war back to the core of keep it real: The attitude of facing head-on, "is the most real, and the most hip-hop"[^51]. The misogyny war is on the surface a骂战 (scolding war), in the core a debate about "what is real": circling back again to that word repeatedly redefined by Taiwanese rappers.

The most moving turn is that these girls did not stop consuming each other on the diss battlefield, but jointly built their own stage. On June 14, 2026, the Cultural Foundation will host, led by ?te, the _[She Vibes Hip-Hop Music Day_ (嬉哈音樂日)](https://www.indievox.com/activity/detail/26_iv040468b) at The Wall Live House, eight all-female groups in relay: ?te, Yang Shu-ya, RapShark, 7LING, Majin, etc.[^52] The event will also combine menstrual equity, with free sanitary products provided by the Little Red Riding Hood Association. ?te’s original intention is simple: "This time finally have the opportunity to hold an activity completely belonging to girls, whether performers or staff, hope it is all girls participating." Majin said, hoping through this activity, "to bring the same hope to little girls who want to make music"[^53]. From battlefield to stage, this group turned "sisterhood" from a slogan into a real afternoon that will happen.

> Notice a detail: sisterhood here is "deliberately" constructed, not naturally grown. Female rappers interviewed said plainly—"when everyone keeps talking about brothers, why are sisters never mentioned," so she "consciously put 'sisters' into songs"; RapShark said she was riding on ?te’s courage to dare do this[^43]. This forms a sharp contrast with the existing, unexplained "brother" structure of the male hip-hop circle: Men’s brotherhood is the default value; women’s sisterhood must be built from zero. So She Vibes is less a performance and more a structural fill-in—in front of a microphone originally built only for men, forcibly building another one. Must be honest, this road is still long: Female rap artists in Taiwan are still a minority, those achieving commercial success are even more rare[^54]. But the ruler has moved from "never seen before" to "eight groups in relay."

## Mixed Voiceprint

Back to the initial question: What makes Taiwanese rap special?

Laying out the entire history, we find its most moving place is never how much it resembles the US. Chinese is a tonal language, described as "should not be able to rap"—they used double rhymes, mathematical rhyming, to rewrite the rhythm problem into rhyme density. Taiwanese Hoklo’s seven tones and tone sandhi were once considered accents unfit for the stage: They treated it as a signature "even just speaking has melody," adding Hakka and sixteen indigenous languages, growing into a multi-accent mother-tongue resistance. Creators are mostly highly educated urban middle-class, mismatching "weapons of the ghetto poor": They干脆 (crisply) redefined "real" as honesty, not origin.

These three things, each looked at alone, are "disadvantages." But together, they became something else: A mixed voiceprint that only this island can sing. It mixes language (Mandarin, Taiwanese, Hakka, Indigenous multi-accent), mixes class (NTU students, temple fair kids, idol styles coexist), mixes gender (a microphone originally built only for men is being forcibly built into another one), mixes ideology (from developing across the strait to inviting the Dalai Lama into MVs, the spectrum is wide enough to hold all stances). This "mixing" is precisely where it is distinct from the other side’s single discourse: It cannot be co-opted by any single market or single narrative, because it itself is composed of un-co-opted diversity.

In 2004, Song Yueh-ting’s mother took the stage to accept the award, the song’s name was _Life's a Struggle_: Life is a struggle. Twenty years later, MC HotDog finally became King, Leo Wang had already written the first page of Rap King, Yang Shu-ya flipped a war with a song without a label, ?te carrying a white coat, leading girls to build their own stage. From pirated CDs to Golden Melody King, the road walked was long; but what this island truly did not change is that it kept answering that oldest question with its own language, its own education, its own diverse identities: What is real.

Chinese’s four tones, Taiwanese Hoklo’s seven tones, sixteen indigenous languages: Taiwan turned the language disadvantage of "should not be able to rap" into a voice that only this island can sing. That is not a voiceprint resembling anyone; that is a mixed sovereign voiceprint that no one can copy.
