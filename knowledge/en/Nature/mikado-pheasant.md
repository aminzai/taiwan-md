# Mikado Pheasant: The King on the Back of the NT$1,000 Bill — The Easier to Encounter, the More You Must Step Back

> **30-second overview:** In 1906, British explorer Walter Goodfellow identified the bird later named the Mikado Pheasant from two long black-and-white tail feathers on a Tsou Indigenous headdress. It inhabits cloud forests at elevations of 1,800 to 3,800 meters, yet entered every household via the NT$1,000 bill. The counterintuitive fact: the _2024 Taiwan Bird Red List_ assesses it as "Least Concern," yet it remains a Rare and Valuable Protected Species under the law; a stable population does not mean people may approach closer. [^1]

## Two Tail Feathers, Later Printed on a Banknote

In 1906, Walter Goodfellow (古費洛) saw two long, glossy black tail feathers with white bars on the headdress of a Tsou (鄒族) Indigenous person. They were sent to Britain for identification, and the Japanese transliteration of "emperor" — _mikado_ — was preserved in the scientific name. The Chinese-speaking world thus calls it the "Mikado Pheasant" (帝雉). In more rigorous taxonomic usage, it is also called the **Black Long-tailed Pheasant** (_Syrmaticus mikado_). [^2]

This name easily misleads one into thinking it is a bird kept in a display case. In fact, the Mikado Pheasant belongs to the order Galliformes, family Phasianidae, genus _Syrmaticus_, and is a species endemic to Taiwan. The male measures 72–87 cm, its glossy black plumage showing an iridescent blue sheen, with a ring of red bare skin around the eye. The female measures 46–52 cm, her brown plumage letting her blend into the forest understory. [^2]

Placed together, the two appearances look like the high mountain's night and its leaf litter split into a pair of birds. They also entered the daily life of Taiwan's people together: the two birds on the reverse of the NT$1,000 note are Mikado Pheasants. The banknote magnifies it; the forest hides it. This contrast is the starting point for understanding the Mikado Pheasant. [^2]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
>
> A banknote makes people think they already know the Mikado Pheasant, yet it happens to obscure the most important fact: it is not a symbol of Taiwan's high mountains, but an animal that can survive only with intact forest, leaf litter, and a sense of distance. **Recognizing it does not equal possessing it.**

## 2,300 Meters Elevation Is Not a Safety Line

The Ministry of Agriculture's species data describes the Mikado Pheasant's habitat quite specifically: elevations of 1,800–3,800 meters, high mountain areas with frequent fog, understory of mixed coniferous-broadleaf and coniferous forests. It eats flowers, fruit, tender shoots, seeds, as well as earthworms and small insects. Rather than flying into open sky, it more often moves quietly along forest edges, leaf litter, and forest road surfaces. [^2]

Yushan National Park, compiling survey records, notes that the Mikado Pheasant is most frequently recorded in the park at elevations of 2,300–2,550 meters, favoring sites with slopes of 30–60 degrees and abundant ground vegetation and leaf litter cover. It utilizes secondary forest and plantation areas, and in winter shows movement to lower elevations. These data remind us that what the Mikado Pheasant needs is not a label reading "primary forest," but a forest structure that still provides cover, food, and safe corridors. [^3]

Forest roads are a contradictory part of this structure. They give researchers, rangers, and general hikers a chance to see the Mikado Pheasant, but also bring people, vehicles, and cameras onto the road surfaces where it commonly forages. When humans treat "easy to see" as "should approach," a road that originally connected the mountains can become a boundary where pressure concentrates.

Breeding season falls roughly from April to August. The female builds a nest padded with dead branches and fallen leaves under rocks or in other concealed spots. When moving as a pair, the male often stays near the female and remains vigilant. For hikers, such an encounter may be a lucky photograph; for the Mikado Pheasant, it may be occurring precisely during the courtship, foraging, or chick-rearing periods when quiet is most needed. [^2]

## A Protected Area Is Not a Seamless Green Expanse

Based on years of surveys, Yushan National Park once estimated at least 11,000 Mikado Pheasants within the park. Tataka, Junda Forest Road, Batongguan Historic Trail, and Walami Trail are locations where it is relatively easily encountered. This number is reassuring, but it does not mean the island-wide population faces no risk, nor can it be simplified into a promotional label reading "lots of Mikado Pheasants." [^3]

A 2002 study on Mikado Pheasant habitat and conservation status, using data available at the time, estimated that about 39% of the Mikado Pheasant's distribution range in Taiwan lay within national parks or protected areas, and projected possibly 6,477 km² of suitable habitat. The authors simultaneously noted explicitly that the model underestimated the actual distribution range. These figures are therefore more like a historical cross-section for understanding the protected-area network, not a total population figure directly applicable today. [^4]

This study also recorded that at two Yushan study sites the Mikado Pheasants' home ranges were very small, and no individuals were observed crossing the roughly 400-meter gap between the two sites. That cannot be exaggerated into "Mikado Pheasants definitely cannot cross 400 meters," yet it suffices to remind us: high mountain forests may appear connected on a map, but for a bird that moves mainly in the understory, the actual traversable paths may be narrower than humans imagine. [^4]

Researchers also saw different population signals in different habitats: the primary forest study site was relatively stable, while the secondary forest site showed a decline between 1989 and 1999. The paper lists poorer reproductive success, increased nest predators, cooler and wetter weather during incubation, and poaching pressure as possible contexts. This is research from a specific era and location, insufficient to declare an island-wide trend, yet it preserves conservation's most important common sense: a protected area is not a piece of green that stays safe forever once a stamp is applied. [^4]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
>
> We often imagine nature reserves as safes with boundaries. The Mikado Pheasant's data instead seem to say something else: conservation is more like maintaining a net — when the mesh is too large, the forest on the map remains, but the paths animals can walk have already dwindled.

## "Least Concern" and Protected Species — Why Can Both Be True?

The Mikado Pheasant's conservation status appears contradictory at first glance. The Biodiversity Research Institute, Ministry of Agriculture explained in 2024 that in the _2024 Taiwan Bird Red List_ it is assessed as **Least Concern**, meaning the population is currently stable and faces no immediate extinction risk. The same official response also reiterated that in Taiwan it is still designated by the Ministry of Agriculture as a **Rare and Valuable Protected Wildlife Species**. [^1]

The two judgments do not overturn each other. The Red List asks "given current evidence, how high is the extinction risk"; the legal protected status restricts disturbance, abuse, hunting, illegal trade, and habitat destruction. The former is an assessment; the latter is a defense line. [^1] [^2]

The Mikado Pheasant was once threatened by hunting pressure and habitat destruction; its magnificent tail feathers even made males targets of poachers. After the _Wildlife Conservation Act_ passed in 1989, conservation measures and the protected-area system gradually changed the environment it faced. Today's "Least Concern" should be read more as these defense lines still functioning, not that they can be dismantled. [^2]

This is also why "Mikado Pheasant numbers have rebounded" cannot directly lead to "we no longer need to care." Conservation work is most easily misunderstood as the final moment of rescuing an endangered species. The more difficult stage is actually when the situation has just stabilized, to continue guarding those inconspicuous conditions that will not immediately make headlines.

## A Genome Map Cannot Speak for the Forest

In 2018, a research team published a whole-genome study of the Mikado Pheasant, reporting a genome of approximately 1.04 Gb with 15,972 annotated protein-coding genes. In genes related to energy metabolism, oxygen transport, hemoglobin binding, immune response, and DNA repair, they found signals of expansion or positive selection. These findings provide important clues to understanding how the Mikado Pheasant adapts to high-elevation environments. [^5]

Using mitochondrial data and a molecular clock, the study also estimated that the Mikado Pheasant's ancestors likely entered Taiwan from the north around 3.47 million years ago. This time scale pulls a bird foraging with its head down beside a mountain trail back into the long duration of Taiwan's island formation, isolation, and evolution. It is not "a pheasant good at climbing mountains," but a species filtered out by the environment over the long term on this mountain island. [^5]

However, the value of genetic research should not be mythologized. The Biodiversity Research Institute, Ministry of Agriculture specifically noted that whole-genome sequencing means humans understand this species' genetic information better; **it does not represent restoration success**. Science can help us see clues of adaptation, but cannot block poaching for the Mikado Pheasant, clear away human-fed food, or make severed understory corridors automatically grow back. [^1]

_Science Monthly_'s summary of this study also mentions that genomic data can assist subsequent understanding of population size, genetic diversity, and possible deleterious mutations. In other words, the genome map is more like a more precise diagnostic tool. It improves our ability to judge, but will not make conservation decisions for us. [^6]

## A Slice of Toast, Turning a Wild Bird into a Performer

The Mikado Pheasant does not hide in the fog forever. Precisely because Dasyueshan, Tataka, and other places offer relatively high chances of encountering it, it also bears another pressure: people want it to appear before their lenses, stay a little longer, come a little closer. The Ministry of Agriculture's "five don'ts" for birding are direct: **do not startle, do not lure, do not chase, do not damage, do not capture**. [^2]

In 2024, the Environment Information Center reposted a field observation from Dasyueshan, recording a male Mikado Pheasant picking up toast, rice, and leftovers, with surrounding tourists stopping to photograph. This is a single case, not generalizable to the whole population; but it captures the problem clearly: when wild birds become predictable because of human food, the forest's most precious chance encounters slowly turn into performances staged for people to wait for. [^7]

Feeding is not an enlarged kindness; it changes the rules of interaction. Human food may not suit birds' nutritional needs. More importantly, humans use food to exchange for proximity, letting animals associate people with obtainable rewards. For a wild bird protected by law, the best close distance is not it walking to your feet, but it still being able to choose not to approach. [^7]

If you encounter a Mikado Pheasant on a mountain trail, the smallest action is the hardest: stop, let it decide where to go. Do not block the path, do not trade food for photos, do not follow it as it moves. If you discover suspected bird nets, hunting gear, or other illegal activities in a protected area, Yushan National Park advises reporting directly to park management personnel, rather than treating suspicious objects as another photograph. [^3]

## Let It Stay in the Forest, Not Only on the Banknote

The Mikado Pheasant traveled from two tail feathers, a taxonomic catalog, to a banknote everyone has touched; it also traveled from a history threatened by hunting and habitat destruction to today's "Least Concern" assessment. This path was not walked by the bird alone — it is the space left together by the forest, protected areas, researchers, and people willing to step back.

Next time you encounter it at Dasyueshan or Tataka, you might remember a question that does not sound like a travel rule: if I truly love this bird today, do I want to take away a closer photograph, or its right to keep retreating into the forest understory?

What the Mikado Pheasant most deserves to be remembered for may not be how king-like it looks. It is more like a reminder: a wild animal being visible to us is often not because it has finally lost its fear of humans, but because there is still a place that allows it not to learn to please people.
