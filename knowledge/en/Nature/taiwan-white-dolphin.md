> **30-Second Overview:** In 2002, Taiwan began systematic surveys of the western coast white dolphins; in 2020, the Agency for Marine Conservation announced an important habitat spanning 763 square kilometers; and in 2026, controversy over whether reclamation projects at Taichung Port require an environmental impact assessment (EIA) continues. These three dates together show that even after being seen and mapped into legal territory, the white dolphins have not secured a disturbance-free ocean.
> The true story of the Taiwanese white dolphin is not "humans finally saved the dolphins," but rather how fisheries, ports, energy, and coastal engineering can change their use of this shallow sea once its existence is known.
> Their limited range, slow reproductive rate, and overlap with nearshore fishing and port construction mean that conservation is not about one dolphin, but an entire set of coastal usage regulations.

In 2002, research teams began systematic surveys of the white dolphins along Taiwan's western coast. On September 1, 2020, the important habitat for the Chinese white dolphin was officially announced. In April 2026, there was controversy regarding the EIA for a 60-hectare reclamation project at Taichung Port, as reported by public television (Gongshi). These are not three isolated years, but a conservation timeline moving from "seeing them" to "how to share."[^4] [^6] [^7]

The Agency for Marine Conservation designated the important habitat of the Chinese white dolphin as 763 square kilometers, spanning waters in Miaoli, Taichung, Changhua, and Yunlin. On a map, it looks like a boundary, but there is no fence in the sea: fishing nets, cargo ships, reclamation, pollutants, and noise all cross the lines drawn by humans.[^1] [^6]

This area is large, but it does not mean that white dolphins can avoid risks arbitrarily within it. The IUCN points out that their core distribution remains a narrow band hugging the western coast. NOAA also describes them as living only in the narrow estuarine waters of Taiwan's west coast.[^2] [^3]

📝 **Curator's Note:** Area is a necessary policy number, but not ecological function itself. What truly needs protection are water depth, estuaries, food, and quiet activity conditions.

## Why They Live So Close to Shore

Taiwan's white dolphins are not large cetaceans that roam the open ocean. The IUCN Cetacean Specialist Group notes that they mainly inhabit narrow waters within about 3 kilometers off Taiwan's west coast, with higher densities near estuaries and in waters less than 5 to 8 meters deep. They rarely appear in deeper water below 20 meters, unless it is an artificially deepened shipping channel.[^2]

The Agency for Marine Conservation describes the Pacific Ocean Spinner Dolphin (_Sousa chinensis_) as a species of nearshore and estuarine environments, typically inhabiting waters less than 25 meters deep, occupying a relatively high position in the marine food chain. These conditions help them find food more easily, but also bring them into direct contact with the most densely used coastal areas by humans.[^1]

Mature individuals often appear white or pinkish, with a hump at the base of the dorsal fin; juveniles are grayer. They are not "white" from birth; their coloration changes with age and body spots. The pink figure that is remembered is the result of growth.[^1] [^3]

📝 **Curator's Note:** For white dolphins, shallow water is not a temporary parking lot; it is their home. Yet humans treat this same shallow sea as the most convenient place for construction, fishing, and transport.

![Pacific Ocean Spinner Dolphin active on the surface](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/be/Sousa_chinensis_%282%29_by_Zureks.jpg)

_Photo: Zureks / Wikimedia Commons, CC0 1.0_

This image does not show a field record of the western coast population in Taiwan but is a morphological reference of the same species. The article does not treat this as geographical evidence for the Taiwanese white dolphin. Maintaining this boundary regarding visual data is important because "it looks like" cannot replace photographic identification, fieldwork, and location records.

This nearshore lifestyle also explains why conservation of the white dolphins cannot rely solely on marine protected areas far from the coast. They need estuaries, tidal flats, nearshore fish populations, and activity ranges that are not excessively fragmented. Protecting a single point might still lose the entire coastline they need for movement, foraging, and reproduction.[^1] [^2]

## Constrained by Shallow Water Conditions All Their Lives

The species page on NOAA Fisheries states that Taiwanese white dolphins measure about 6.6 to 9.2 feet long and weigh about 330 to 440 pounds. They primarily eat fish, and may also prey on cephalopods and crustaceans. These figures are not sensational data; they remind us: they depend on the entire ecological condition of estuaries and shallow waters that can sustain fish populations.[^3]

The same page notes that life history data for this subspecies is still limited—they mature late and reproduce slowly, with long relationships between females and calves. When a population is already small, the death of any adult or calf is not just one less individual, but could affect the next reproductive cycle.[^3]

This means "habitat degradation" is not just about an ugly coastal landscape. Reclamation, freshwater diversion, pollution, or construction noise may first change the fish populations and water currents, forcing white dolphins to spend more time searching for food. For a slow-reproducing, narrow-ranging population, these changes are difficult to solve by moving to another area.[^1] [^3]

📝 **Curator's Note:** A species' vulnerability comes not only from low numbers but also from their inability to reproduce fast enough to offset losses caused by habitat and fishing gear.

## From "White Fish" to a Scientifically Named Subspecies

Western coastal fishermen have long known about white dolphins at sea. _Taiwan Guanghua_ records that fishermen in Changhua call them "white fish," while those around Kinmen and Xiamen often call them "Mazu fish." These names are not scientific classifications, but they preserve how local residents identify, remember, and speak of them.[^4]

In 2002, systematic surveys began in Taiwan. A special paper from the IUCN notes that Taiwanese white dolphins were only scientifically recorded in 2002 and formally described as a subspecies of Pacific Ocean Spinner Dolphin (_Sousa chinensis taiwanensis_) in 2015. The scientific naming is not about when they appeared, but when the research system finally had the capacity to write them into scientific records.[^2]

Early reports in _Taiwan Guanghua_ describe survey teams searching for white dolphins along the coasts of Miaoli, Taichung, and Changhua, identifying individuals through photographs. Researchers must document the time, location, behavior, and dorsal fin notches of each sighting to estimate the small population size. A single flip at sea cannot directly equal "one more."[^4]

📝 **Curator's Note:** "White fish" and the scientific name are not two mutually exclusive bodies of knowledge. One comes from years at sea; the other comes from photos, models, and statistics. What is truly missing is integrating both sets of knowledge into decision-making.

![Side view image of Pacific Ocean Spinner Dolphin](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/a/a3/Sousa_chinensis_%283%29_by_Zureks.jpg)

_Photo: Zureks / Wikimedia Commons, CC0 1.0_

In research, dorsal fins, back humps, spots, and body color can help identify individuals or judge age stages, but a web image without time and location can only serve as morphological reference material. True population estimates still require long-term, repeatable photographic identification data.[^2] [^4]

## Numbers Are Not Just One Answer

The population numbers of the white dolphins are often written as a single shocking figure, but different years, methods, and research pages cannot simply be added together. The IUCN page records that mark-recapture analysis from photo identification estimated the highest annual population in 2010 to be about 74 individuals, with estimates between 67 and 71 for 2011 to 2013. The same page also describes their endangered status as having no more than 75 individuals and fewer than 50 mature individuals.[^2]

The NOAA Fisheries species page states that there are fewer than 100 remaining, noting that Taiwan's white dolphin was listed as endangered by the U.S. under the Endangered Species Act in 2018. This number is not necessarily contradictory to the IUCN estimates because of differences in page update times, estimation methods, and cited research. The real danger is piecing them together into a falsely precise latest population curve.[^3]

The 2026 public television report used "a few dozen" as the opening for its special segment, citing interviewees who mentioned a maximum group of 9 at Taichung Port in 2025 and a maximum group of 14 in 2026. These are observations from news reporting and interviewees, not equivalent to a complete population census. They allow people to see the scene but cannot replace population estimates.[^7]

📝 **Curator's Note:** A credible conservation article does not select the saddest number; it clarifies the year, method, and evidence level for every number.

![Image of Pacific Ocean Spinner Dolphins aggregating on the surface](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/bf/Sousa_chinensis_%284%29_by_Zureks.jpg)

_Photo: Zureks / Wikimedia Commons, CC0 1.0_

Aggregating images are easily read as population numbers, but one frame only represents visible individuals at a specific point in time. White dolphin estimates must account for repeated sightings of the same individual, missed individuals, seasonal variations, and survey scope; the intuitiveness of an image cannot replace estimation methodology.[^2]

If these numbers are placed back into the same question, they still point in one direction: this is a small, narrow-ranging population dependent on nearshore environments with no obvious downward trend. The IUCN special paper notes that both population viability analyses indicate a high probability of decline after considering bycatch mortality and habitat loss/degradation.[^2]

## The People on the Other Side of the Net

Marine dolphin conservation is easily written as "fishermen harming dolphins," but research compiled by Airiti provides a more complex picture. A 2011 study by Ko Meng-chen, using interviews with 127 fishermen from May 2010 to April 2011, resource use overlap indicators, and longline catch data, studied the potential food source competition between western coast fisheries and white dolphins.[^5]

The research summary indicated that 54% of interviewed fishing operations overlapped with the main habitat of white dolphins. Within this overlapping area, 94% of the primary fishing methods were gillnetting. This does not mean "all fishermen are doing the same thing in the same place," but it shows that white dolphins and nearshore fisheries indeed share a narrow resource space.[^5]

The same study found that 42% of interviewed fishermen believed fishery resources had declined over the past decade, and 49% cited industrial pollution and illegal trawling within three nautical miles as reasons for the decline. The study also noted that fishermen's views on whether dolphin appearances reduce catches were inconsistent. This data shows that fishermen are also suffering from resource depletion, but their losses usually do not appear in conservation posters.[^5]

📝 **Curator's Note:** Fisheries and white dolphins are not two unrelated enemies. They share the same food web that has been compressed by many uses.

The IUCN lists fishery interactions as a direct and urgent threat, noting evidence of severe harm caused by human activities to nearly 60% of individuals, and over 31% of individuals were photographed carrying fishing gear or potentially injured by it. These percentages come from studies cited in the IUCN page and cannot be directly extrapolated to mean that every type of fishing method or every port is equally responsible.[^2]

Therefore, conservation policy cannot just issue "do not catch" orders. It also needs to know which type of net, which season, which stretch of water, and which reward system is most likely to reduce risk, and it must ensure that fishermen do not have to bear the entire cost of public conservation with their own income. The coordination before and after the 2020 habitat announcement started from here.[^6]

## What Has Changed With a Legal Habitat?

The important habitat for the Chinese white dolphin, announced on September 1, 2020, spans from Longfeng Port in Miaoli to Waisanding Lighthouse in Chiayi, with a total area of 76,300 hectares. The Center for Environmental Information reported that this announcement followed classification as a protected species in 2008, research surveys, and coordination with multiple stakeholders in 2014 before it was officially implemented.[^6]

The announcement is not turning the entire sea into a total fishing ban zone. Citing the Marine Protection Agency (MPA), the Center for Environmental Information stated that existing fishing activities not targeting white dolphins will not automatically violate regulations just because an important habitat has been designated. However, development and utilization within the area cannot damage original ecological functions; environmental impact assessments must be conducted and approved by competent authorities when necessary.[^6]

This distinction is crucial. A legal habitat is not a seal prohibiting human entry, but a system that places ecological function into development review, monitoring, education, and local participation. It acknowledges that fishermen already work in this sea and requires that new projects cannot pretend the sea has no other residents.[^1] [^6]

📝 **Curator's Note:** A truly useful protected area does not just tell people what they cannot do; it must clearly state who is responsible for monitoring, who bears the transition costs, and how to remedy violations.

## The 60 Hectares of Taichung Port

The public television report _Our Island_ in 2026 covered the dike construction project at Taichung Port, which plans to reclaim 60 hectares for sediment disposal, as well as land needs related to wind power, green hydrogen, and liquefied natural gas. This project is located within the range of the white dolphin's important habitat, leading to "legal habitat" and "port project" being featured in the same news story.[^7]

The report presented two different views: transportation and port authorities argue that the reclamation area is within existing breakwaters and does not require an EIA by law. Civil society groups and lawyers argue that the project scope belongs to open waters and should be assessed under environmental laws. The article cannot pre-judge the court or competent authorities, but it can clearly point out that the core of the dispute is whether the boundary of existing projects is sufficient to represent ecological boundaries.[^7]

The public television report also recorded observations from private observers who claimed the largest group seen at Taichung Port in 2025 was 9 individuals and the largest in 2026 was 14, suggesting it might be an area for nursing, foraging, and resting. This is an observation and judgment by interviewees, not official population statistics. Its value lies in pointing out that pre-project assessment should not only ask "have white dolphins appeared," but also "what are they doing here."[^7]

📝 **Curator's Note:** The hardest part of development disputes is not deciding whether to side with the dolphin or the port, but acknowledging that a port can also be a workplace for some families and a nursery area for some dolphins.

Greenpeace Taiwan featured the white dolphins as "Mazu fish," compiling information on their color, distribution, research methods, and habitat threats. This type of conservation advocacy brought a species previously only seen on the west coast into public discussion across Taiwan, but the language of advocacy must still be read separately from official announcements and academic estimates.[^8]

## A Real "Home"

The history of white dolphin conservation has a turning point that is not very much like a victory story: in 2002, researchers began systematically looking for them. In 2020, the government mapped their habitat into legal territory. In 2026, the question of whether construction requires an EIA is still being debated on site. Being seen, named, and protected does not equal being left safely.[^2] [^6] [^7]

For the white dolphins, a real home is not a polygon that only exists in official documents. It must allow fish to stay, provide fishermen with predictable rules, ensure that construction knows underwater noise and reclamation are not afterthoughts before building, and attach a year, method, and uncertainty to every population number.[^1] [^2] [^5]

In this view, the white dolphin is not just a decorative mascot of Taiwan's coast. It connects things we rarely see together: a fishing net, a port, an estuary, a development review process, and a nearshore population estimated at only dozens to under a hundred.[^2] [^3] [^6]

The next time someone says "Taiwan's white dolphins finally have a home," they should push the question one step further: does the rule of that home also allow the people who work by the sea to live? If the answer is no, then 763 square kilometers is not the end; it is Taiwan finally beginning to learn how to coexist with a neighbor who cannot speak for themselves.[^1] [^6] [^7]

The habitat announcement should therefore be understood as a beginning, not a promotional poster. It requires continuous government monitoring and demands that construction, fisheries, and conservation data can read each other. If every unit only preserves its own numbers, the white dolphins will continue to live in the gaps of the data.[^1] [^6]

For Taiwan's west coast, protecting the white dolphin is also about relearning how to use the coast. This learning has no single answer that can be achieved at once, but at least it starts by not confusing numbers, not excluding fishermen, and acknowledging that someone lives in the sea before construction begins.
