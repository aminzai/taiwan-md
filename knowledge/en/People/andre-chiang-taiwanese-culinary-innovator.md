# André Chiang: He Never Waited for a Definition to Settle Before Turning Away

> **30-Second Overview:** André Chiang is Taiwan’s first chef to win Michelin stars on the international stage, but he is perhaps even more remembered for voluntarily leaving at the peak of his career three times. The year after Singapore’s Restaurant André climbed to the 14th spot on the world rankings, he turned off the lights on Valentine’s Day; in the tenth year of Taipei’s RAW, which was “harder to book than Taiwan Railways tickets during long holidays,” he announced he was closing it to teach. This piece explores why he always turns away before finishing the journey: asking from “Who am I?” to “What is the taste of Taiwan?”—a question he himself has not yet decided to answer.

## Ten Days Later, He Bought a Ticket to a Language He Didn’t Speak

In 1997, a twenty-something André Chiang was already the head chef of a French restaurant at the Western Hotel in Taipei, the youngest French restaurant chef in Taiwan’s history[^1]. That year, he proactively invited the renowned twin-star chefs Jacques and Laurent Pourcel from the three-star Michelin restaurant _Le Jardin des Sens_ (Garden of Senses) in southern France to Taiwan for a guest chef demonstration. The two worked together in the kitchen for ten days[^1].

He entered the kitchen largely through the story of a bento box. Born in Shipei, Beitou District, Taipei in 1976, he grew up in Shilin[^2]. His mother was an outstanding cook who had worked in a Chinese restaurant in Japan for ten years. Other people’s bento boxes were steamed in the morning; his were not. “Thirty minutes before mealtime, she would stir-fry them at home and ride her motorcycle to the school gate,” _The Newsweek_ recorded in a 2007 interview[^1]. A mother who demanded “freshly stir-fried, just right, no compromises” in even the most everyday meals had already set his standards for taste very high.

During those ten days, language was almost useless. André Chiang did not speak a word of French, and the Pourcel brothers did not speak a word of English; they had to rely on a translator to converse[^1]. But what truly convinced the brothers was something beyond translation: how he chopped, how he fried, how he plated and finished the dishes. After ten days, they asked André Chiang, “Are you going to France?” He said yes[^1].

So he bought a ticket. It is worth pausing here: a twenty-something person, with no language skills, no prestigious degree, and no guarantee for the future, decided to fly to the other side of the Earth based on a ten-day encounter. He did not figure it all out before leaving; he left first, then went on to understand what it was like there. Later people added many inspirational footnotes to this story, but stripped to its core, it is actually a very simple, yet difficult, action: taking that first step when you cannot yet see clearly what lies ahead. This is the prototype of his decision-making for the rest of his life. Every subsequent turn at the peak of his career is an amplification of those ten days.

Ten days is not long, but it is just enough for a person to make up their mind. What awaited him next was fifteen long years in a foreign land. And he still did not know how to order a cup of coffee.

## Over the Indian Ocean, Asking Again “Who Am I”

In the kitchen of Le Jardin des Sens in Montpellier, southern France, language was the first wall. He did not speak a word of French, and all instructions, scoldings, and chats in the kitchen had to be guessed with his eyes and body. André Chiang was not treated with special respect simply because he was “the person invited by the Pourcel brothers.” He started from the very bottom, peeling potatoes for two whole years. “At that time, I didn’t speak a word of French and couldn’t make any friends,” he later recalled. “The only thing I could converse with was probably the potatoes.”[^3] Two years, just to do the most insignificant thing flawlessly. This patience to return to the bottom and retrain himself would repeatedly appear in every turn he made later. In the days that followed, he climbed up, taking nine years to become the executive chef (chef de cuisine) of the entire restaurant, managing the kitchen of this three-star establishment[^4].

He fell in love with that world. “I fell in love with French culture, history, and emotion,” he later recalled to _The South China Morning Post_. “The emotion a chef feels when creating a dish was something I had never experienced at that age. A person cooking for another person is such an intimate relationship. It was at that time that I decided to continue down this path.”[^6] He achieved technical precision to the finest degree. But something strange happened near the peak: the more mature his skills became, the more he felt he was disappearing within someone else’s system. He was making the most authentic French cuisine, but whose cuisine was it, really?

The true turn happened over the Indian Ocean. He left the European continent and went to a resort on a small island in the Seychelles to work as a head chef. According to reports from Taiwanese media, _Time_ magazine described his dishes twice around 2007 as “the greatest cuisine in the Indian Ocean”[^1]. But more critical than the external title was that on that island, he first seriously asked himself: removing France, removing the Michelin framework, “what is my own cuisine.”

The answer was a thinking framework later called “Octaphilosophy”: eight elements, ranging from the most basic “salt” to the most abstract “memory” and “terroir”[^4]. This philosophy sought to answer a more fundamental question than “does it taste good”: why does a dish exist. Interestingly, the order of these eight words varies across different publications[^5]. Even his own philosophy has no fixed order, corresponding precisely to his creative belief: no fixed menu, rethinking every day based on the ingredients of the day.

One dish, however, was set in stone. In 1997, he created “Memory,” a warm foie gras jelly served with a black truffle coulis[^4]. This dish has the year directly written into its name and has remained almost unchanged for over twenty years.

> **✦** A person who spends a lifetime “changing the question” hides the only fixed answer in a dish called “Memory.”

Memory was his first dish that was completely his own, with no template to copy. The reason this “never changed” fact is moving is that everything around it—restaurants, cities, titles, identities—later all changed. He had finally found an answer that belonged to him. But this answer was soon to be tested on a higher stake.

## On the Night at the Peak, He Turned Off the Lights First

In 2008, André Chiang opened Jaan par André at the Swissôtel The Stamford in Singapore. Eighteen months after opening, it climbed to the 39th spot on the World’s 50 Best Restaurants list[^4]. Two years later, on October 10, 2010, he hung his own name on the sign. Restaurant André opened in a three-story historic old house at 41 Bukit Pasut Road in Singapore, with only 30 seats[^7].

What followed was a beautiful upward curve. On the Asia’s 50 Best Restaurants list, Restaurant André went from the 5th spot in the inaugural 2013 list, slid only once to the 6th in 2014, and then climbed all the way to the 2nd in 2017. On the World’s 50 Best, it jumped from the 32nd spot to the 14th in just one year, advancing 18 spots at once, marking the highest position for a Singaporean restaurant on this global list that year[^8].

This was not a straight-line story of climbing to the top. The drop to the 6th spot in 2014 reminded everyone that this path was not taken for granted; but for the next three years, he climbed step by step, pushing a small restaurant with only 30 seats to a place where gourmets around the world were willing to buy tickets to fly in specifically to eat. At this height, most people think about how to hold on to it. He was thinking about something else.

In the very year his rankings reached their career peak, he chose to get off the bus.

On October 10, 2017, exactly seven years after the opening of Restaurant André, after closing, André Chiang gathered more than twenty employees from around the world, took out a written white paper, and read it out sentence by sentence in English. His wife was in tears nearby. The employees knew about this only one hour earlier than the outside world[^10]. According to reports at the time, this thought had been planted half a year ago: he looked around the restaurant and found that “in every corner, I could not say which thing needed adjustment, nothing at all.” Precisely because he could not pick out anything to change, he decided to stop[^10].

In the statement, he wrote: “I am a perfectionist. In my past 30 years of professional life, I have been constantly pursuing that non-existent ‘perfection’... Only now do I realize that this moment is the most perfect.” The ending sentence, later quoted repeatedly, was: “I have always pursued perfection. This moment is perfection.”[^9] In the same statement, he did something even rarer: he proactively asked Michelin to exclude him from the evaluation. “I will return the Michelin two stars, and simultaneously remind the 2018 new edition of the Singapore Michelin Guide to exclude Restaurant ANDRE from consideration.”[^9] On February 14, 2018, Valentine’s Day night, the 30 seats were filled for the last time, and he personally turned off the lights. “Now, I am going home,” he said[^9].

There is a detail many people get wrong. The public often says André Chiang “abandoned the Michelin three stars,” which sounds like a more tragic sacrifice. But his first Michelin in Singapore was two stars, not three; he returned two stars, and the true action was exiting the entire evaluation system, not rejecting all recognition[^9]. On March 27 of the same year, he received a Lifetime Achievement Award in Macau. The official name was the “Diners Club Lifetime Achievement Award” hosted by Asia’s 50 Best Restaurants, completely unrelated to Michelin, so do not remember it as a Michelin award[^10].

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
>
> The misconception of “returning the three stars” itself demonstrates how much people want to read André Chiang’s turn as a more dramatic sacrifice—as if the more he gives up, the more moving the story becomes. But what he truly did was quieter and more resolute: not a dignified exit after being defeated, but a person standing at the peak refusing to let that peak define who he is. His freedom is simple: not to be trapped by any title.

The two years around the time he turned off the lights were filmed by Singaporean director Josiah Ng into the documentary _André & His Olive Tree_, which aired on Netflix in 2020[^11].

“This moment is perfection” held this step in place. But perfection was never his true endpoint—in the same period of exiting, he had actually already been asking the next question in his heart.

## RAW Asks a Different Question

First, break a common chronological misunderstanding: Taipei’s RAW was not a fallback for him to return to Taiwan after turning off the lights, but the other half of his dual-city advancement. As early as 2014, when Restaurant André was still shining in Singapore, he had already opened RAW in Taipei, operating both places simultaneously.

On December 9, 2014, RAW opened in the Linqun 3rd Road area (Dazhizhou commercial district) of Zhongshan District, Taipei. The chef returning in glory exchanged for a difficult-to-get position. News at the time wrote, “RAW reservations are harder to get than Taiwan Railways tickets during long holidays.” Within less than two weeks of opening, reservations for subsequent dates were snatched up on the same day[^13]. How difficult was it to book? Australian food blogger Lorraine Elliott wrote after visiting in person in 2015: getting a seat at RAW was “a matter of planning and luck”: the 56 seats had to be snatched online in advance, and the 20 bar seats where you could watch the chef cook up close had to be booked by phone another two months in advance[^14]. This dual-track difficulty itself is a microcosm of fine dining: the closer you are to the chef, the higher the threshold.

But what RAW truly asked was a different question. In Singapore, André Chiang asked “Who am I”; back in Taipei, the question changed to “What is the taste of Taiwan.” In early 2022, he opened the “Institute of Taiwanese Taste” on Dihua Street in Taipei[^15]. The object he drew inspiration from was Sichuan cuisine—“Sichuan cuisine has a taste spectrum, with 24 representative flavors,” he said in an interview, so Sichuan cuisine can cover so much ground[^16]. What about Taiwan? He wanted to organize a similar spectrum for the flavors of Taiwan. And what exactly is the taste of Taiwan, he described: “I cannot decide what the taste of Taiwan is with one sentence or one flavor; it is a formula of constant addition, subtraction, multiplication, and division.”[^15]

In fact, long before the Taste Spectrum Institute, RAW had tried another answer. At the end of 2016, at RAW’s two-year anniversary press conference, he announced he would spend a year compiling a “Taiwanese Food Dictionary,” organizing the ingredients of the entire island into a system based on the twenty-four solar terms. The cause was his worry that Taiwan’s ingredients were losing their sense of season: chickens injected with growth hormones shortened from 125 days to 40 days before slaughter; carrots that could originally grow 18 varieties now only had two left on the market[^17]. This dictionary was never officially published, and the scale of the plan was delayed again and again; but that ambition to clarify the flavors of Taiwan, from solar terms to taste spectra, never broke.

Notably, he never said he held the standard answer for the taste of Taiwan. Quite the opposite, he left it as an open question: “The taste of Taiwan is not just the taste of grandmas or moms; what is the taste of Taiwan that belongs to me? What will the taste of Taiwan for the next generation be?”[^15] There is no stamp in this sentence, only a series of question marks.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
>
> A person who started with French training, international rankings, and Michelin certification talking about “the taste of Taiwan” inevitably raises the question: who defines those stalls and home kitchens that do not have an international resume but truly cook the taste of Taiwan every day? André Chiang’s own answer is honest: he presents the “Taiwanese Taste Spectrum” as his personal question, not an official certification. And this question still hangs in the air today: the “Taiwanese Food Dictionary” project, centered on the twenty-four solar terms, has never been officially published, and the Taste Spectrum Institute has no publicly accessible complete output. The definition of the taste of Taiwan was never meant to be decided by one person; this is the blank space he left himself.

RAW later received Michelin stars in Taipei: one star in 2018, and two stars continuously for five years starting from 2019[^12]. And in the tenth year of RAW, when it was nearly legendary how difficult it was to book, he still turned off the lights first, handing the answer to an academy he had not yet finished writing.

What does this posture look like when applied to a kitchen that demands people to be on time to the minute?

## The 45-Degree Chair, Punctured by a Strawberry

During the heyday of Restaurant André in Singapore, André Chiang’s kitchen was known for near-neurotic precision: every chair was placed at an exact 45-degree angle to the table, every table was precisely two finger-widths away from the wall, and he and the front-of-house manager went through the reservation list every day, agonizing over who ought to sit where[^5]. This obsession with detail was later brought into Taipei’s RAW. In such a kitchen, whether a newcomer could survive often depended not on their past credentials, but on their ability to keep up with the chef’s uncompromising standard line in a very short time. This precision produced extremely high-level cuisine, but also left an environment with almost no room for error—this is the other cost of “refusing to be defined.”

To be honest about the industry background: the Taiwanese dining industry has long had structural problems of low wages and overwork; any plan to “cultivate the next generation of chefs” cannot bypass this realistic context. Precision and cruelty, in the entire high-pressure dining industry, often separate by only a thin line.

The sharpest irony came from a plate of strawberries. In 2020, food critic Michael Fei questioned in an independent review that RAW’s spring menu that year “did not form a coherent set of ideas,” and pointed out directly: why was there an unripe strawberry on the spring menu?[^18] Know that strawberries are actually a winter fruit in Taiwan. If a restaurant claiming “seasonal eating” serves out-of-season strawberries in spring, then the philosophy itself ran into itself.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
>
> Even his most proud “seasonal eating” was caught out by a strawberry out of season. A person who lives the “undecided” as a life posture will occasionally be counter-attacked by his own perfectionism—because when you demand precision in everything, any detail that is not aligned will be magnified into a question against the entire philosophy. This does not unintentionally negate him: his high standards are real, his occasional slips are also real; the coexistence of the two makes a living chef, not a statue without cracks.

The restaurant that was hard to book for ten years and had a strawberry picked on it, the Michelin official channel once filmed a video for it, recording his quality control.

And this person, famous for “zero errors,” had the softest time cooking in his life precisely for “allowing errors.” This requires looking at where he is now.

## Now, He Is Still Turning

![André Chiang visiting the Public Digital Innovation Space (PDIS) in 2021.](/article-images/people/andre-chiang-pdis-2021.webp)

_In 2021, André Chiang visited the Public Digital Innovation Space (PDIS). Photo: PDIS / Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 3.0._

In September 2020, André Chiang collaborated with the Hongdao Foundation for the Aged on a pop-up restaurant named “Ah! It’s Okay!”—the front-of-house servers were all elderly people with dementia, and serving the wrong dishes or forgetting orders were the norm[^19]. This menu was not his most technically complex, but it might have been his most emotionally dense. The reason is personal: “My grandmother was also a person with dementia. In her last few years, I had already returned to Taiwan and had much contact with her...” he said. “Grandma would forget some things, forget she had eaten, forget who I was... She was just a lovely old child in the family, so we relaxed and got along with her.”[^20] The child who was raised by his grandmother since childhood, in this restaurant that deliberately allowed mistakes, put aside his most skilled precision and learned to let go; in that event, his mother also helped make potato salad in the back kitchen[^19].

Now he has put the center of his life back in Taiwan. In recent years, he has moved to Yilan with his Thai wife Pam, slowing down the pace of life[^22]. He is listed as the Culinary Creative Director of archi at the Kaohsiung Plaza Hotel in the local area, one of the few projects he can manage without crossing the sea[^30]. His wife is a former fashion magazine editor-in-chief[^23]; in a 2018 interview, he joked that the house had two refrigerators, “one for my wife to use, one for my mom to use,” and outside there was a garden of herbs planted by Pam, which he jokingly called the “Tom Yum Garden”[^21]. A person at the world’s peak returns home, competing with his mom and wife for a kitchen; a few steps out the door, there is a restaurant he is listed as managing.

He is not idle. Starting in 2024, he took on the role of Culinary Curator for Belmond’s “The Asian Express,” using the logic of “one dish paired with one spice” (cloves, cinnamon, galangal), allowing passengers to travel the history of terroir on their taste buds[^24]. Around the same time, he founded the high-end hot pot brand Bon Broth, currently planning to expand to overseas cities[^24]. That same year, he also took on the role of culinary consultant for the four restaurants of “S·S·A·W” under the Wyndham Group, overseeing the comprehensive renovation of spaces and menus[^25]. As for earlier bases, Chengdu’s The Bridge opened at the end of 2017, and Macau’s Sichuan Moon had already closed in 2023[^26]. The map is always moving; some grow, some close, this is originally his norm.

He called the closing of RAW in 2024 “retirement.” But this word in his mouth has never meant leaving the stage. “Providing better education and continuously creating new opportunities and solutions for our dining industry and the next generation has become extremely important to me, more important than operating a successful restaurant,” he said when announcing his retirement[^27]. From chef to teacher, he took this step earlier than anyone else, thinking about how to hand over the method of winning while he could still win. The original site of RAW is to become an international culinary academy, but this has not yet landed: as of now, the academy’s website still only displays “COMING SOON,” with no official opening yet[^28]. Announced for nearly two years, the image is still stuck there.

In April 2026, the month he turned fifty, 1887 by André opened at Raffles Hotel in Singapore, with the concept of “Taste of Time,” a meal of nearly sixty dishes spanning 139 years of the hotel’s history[^29]. Daily cooking was handed to two young people from RAW, and he retreated to the background. Handing over cooking to young people is, for a chef known for control, another form of letting go. “My role has evolved from creating restaurants to establishing a platform for education and guidance,” he said. “I hope it can stand for the next 30 years, 50 years, or even 100 years.”[^30] He described what he is doing now as “more Timeless things,” because he no longer needs to work to sustain a business, “I can just purely do what I like”[^24].

![The dining space of archi.](/article-images/people/andre-chiang-archi-interior-2024.webp)

_The dining space of archi created by André Chiang for the Kaohsiung Plaza Hotel in Yilan. Provided by archi / Kaohsiung Plaza Hotel, cited here under fair use for editorial commentary._

In Singapore, there is a new restaurant spanning 139 years; in Taipei, there is an academy that has not yet opened; in Yilan, there is a kitchen shared by three people. Whether the academy will truly open is no one can guarantee; it might just be a delay in progress, not some deep meaning. But he is once again standing in this moment without an answer. At twenty-something, he bought a ticket without knowing French; this time, he knows where he is going, just hasn’t arrived yet. A chef’s last dish is always tomorrow.
