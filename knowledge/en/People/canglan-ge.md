> **30-Second Overview:**
> In 2017, Chi-ying Wu (吳其穎), a graduate of the Department of Medicine at National Taiwan University (NTU), began operating "Azure Pigeon's Medical World" (蒼藍鴿的醫學天地). He initially created videos based on hard knowledge, later bringing medical dramas, current events, and vaccines into his content. He also utilized Podcasts and text to handle different forms of communication. The focus of this story is not merely a doctor becoming an online celebrity, but how professional medicine must leave the clinic: it must translate jargon into language that audiences can understand, and it must show the necessity of researching data, acknowledging limitations, and maintaining professional boundaries.

## White Coats and Lenses in Shilin Breakfast Shops

In October 2020, an interview took place at a breakfast shop in Shilin. At that time, Chi-ying Wu was a resident physician in pediatrics at Taipei Medical University Hospital (臺大醫院), and his YouTube channel had 320,000 subscribers, with his Podcast gaining attention in health programs. When the interviewer asked how he managed both hospital work and online presence simultaneously, his answer was light: "It's just that everyone has different things to do in their leisure time."[^1]

![Taiwan COVID-19 vaccination site, medical staff preparing vaccines; this image illustrates the context of public health communication in this article](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/2b/Taiwan_COVID-19_vaccination_20210716.jpg)

_Image: Presidential Office, 〈Taiwan COVID-19 vaccination 20210716〉; [Original file page on Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Taiwan_COVID-19_vaccination_20210716.jpg); Licensed by: [CC BY 2.0](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by/2.0)._

This statement sounds like an overly simplistic description of creation, but the reality was quite different. The work of a hospital resident—recording, writing scripts, and editing—is not something that can be automatically completed by simply carving out a small piece of after-work time. In that interview, Azure Pigeon discussed the division of labor across platforms: YouTube for medical drama analysis, Podcasts for news topics, and text via Fang Gezi (方格子) for pediatrics and family medicine.[^1]

This division of labor later became the first key to understanding him. He did not mold all content into a single "doctor persona," but rather allowed each platform to carry out a specific pace: videos captured attention, audio followed the news cycle, and text allowed a medical issue to unfold slowly.

📝 Curator's Note: Azure Pigeon’s true multi-tasking was not about running many accounts simultaneously, but about knowing that the same profession must adopt different ways of speaking across different media.

## Beyond the Lens: Seeing Patients at Appletree North Branch Clinic

Azure Pigeon’s work is not confined to the camera lens. The official website for Dr. Appletree (蘋果樹), which lists Chi-ying Wu as an attending physician at the North Branch Clinic, also notes that he graduated from NTU and is a medical best-seller writer.[^11] This information provided another concrete anchor for his online identity: the medical issues audiences heard in videos were still connected to an actual clinic setting and patient scenarios.

His job title has changed over time. A profile page at the Azurean Health Aesthetics Clinic (蒼嵐健康美學診所) listed him as the "Director of Appletree North Clinic," while Dr. Appletree's current official website uses the title "Attending Physician at the North Branch Clinic."[^12] Therefore, this article uses the title from the current Dr. Appletree official website and does not treat earlier titles as timeless designations. This difference seems like just a few words on a business card, but it is important for biographical reporting: a doctor's appointments, clinical practice, and management roles can all be adjusted over time, and the source must specify which point in time it describes.

The clinic work also prevented "popular science" from being merely an abstract content type. A video might explain a common problem to a large audience, but a consultation must address the age, medical history, lifestyle habits, and genuine concerns of individual patients. The former requires clear, repeatable language; the latter requires continuous questioning despite incomplete information. Azure Pigeon doing both does not mean one video can replace a consultation. On the contrary, it better illustrates why public health communication must separate general knowledge from individual treatment.

## 2017: Hard Knowledge Meets YouTube

Azure Pigeon began operating "Azure Pigeon's Medical World" in 2017. During his university years, he was involved in stage performances and club activities, and when knowledge-based YouTubers started gaining attention, he saw medicine as a potential channel topic. Later reports trace his name back to the gaming nickname "Jianqi Canglan" (劍起滄瀾) from college, which friends called "Canglan Ge," before settling on Azure Pigeon.[^4]

The start was not smooth. Azure Pigeon recalled that the channel initially focused on hard knowledge, then shifted to interesting medical topics, and finally used medical dramas to analyze the medical practices within them. He said, "I always encountered bottlenecks in running YouTube; from starting with hard knowledge, transitioning to interesting medical topics, and now analyzing medical practices or knowledge through medical dramas, I gradually felt that acceptance was higher."[^1]

This transition is easily written as "knowing how to attract traffic," but that misses the real work. Medical content must first be vetted before it can be packaged as a source of humor. The drama provides the entry point; the medical knowledge must still clearly indicate the exit. Another creator interview in 2023 stated his philosophy more directly: "Explaining medicine in a way audiences can understand."[^2]

He also treated performance as part of his job. The interview mentioned that he enjoyed receiving applause through performances in his student days. After becoming a doctor, this desire to perform did not disappear; it was simply used to help the audience listen through difficult knowledge.[^2]

## Patient Questions Become Topics on Screen

The starting point for medical popularization is often not a beautiful concept, but a recurring question from a consultation room. When Taipei City Library introduced Azure Pigeon's lectures and books in 2021, they recorded his initial motivation: hospital internships showed him the short consultation times and the information gap between doctors and patients, where misunderstandings could accumulate into medical disputes.[^3]

That official event organized his books into four aspects and twenty-eight pieces of health knowledge, covering basic medical trivia, bodily warning signs, common diseases, and how to seek care under the National Health Insurance (NHI) system. These classifications did not paint him as an omniscient figure; rather, they showed that he was doing something very practical: translating the most frequent sticking points for patients into language that could be taken home.[^3]

This also explains why he needed to move between professionalism and daily life. Speaking only in jargon might cause viewers to leave within the first minute. Telling stories alone risks having medical risks obscured by laughter. Azure Pigeon's content must accommodate two things simultaneously: making people willing to listen, and ensuring they don't mistakenly believe they can self-diagnose after listening.

## The Pandemic Turns the Lens into a Public Space

After 2020, Taiwanese medical video content gained a larger public context. An English academic study analyzed 943 YouTube videos from 17 medical centers in Taiwan between December 2019 and August 2021, pointing out that healthcare institutions used YouTube to convey COVID-19 information due to its accessibility and ease of use.[^10]

Azure Pigeon is not an official hospital channel, but he operated within the same communication environment. During the pandemic, he uploaded videos analyzing the AZ vaccine and another video discussing vaccination issues during the epidemic.[^7][^8] The existence of these videos itself shows that vaccine information had left the single-sheet health education handout from the hospital and entered a space where people judge credibility using mobile phones, comments, and shares.

The complication of that space is that views can arrive before understanding. If medical messages are reduced to only two slogans—"rest assured" or "be careful"—the audience receives emotion, not a diagnostic tool. What Azure Pigeon can do is deconstruct an issue that seems to have only one stance back into risk, evidence, and applicable conditions. This also requires his tone to be more cautious than in general entertainment videos.

📝 Curator's Note: The pandemic forced doctors into the lens, but it did not turn the lens into a consultation room. Videos can explain common problems, but they cannot complete a diagnosis for every person in front of the screen.

## When Profession Meets Greater Volume

Around 2021, Azure Pigeon commented on influencer Elisa Sasa (愛莉莎莎) regarding "gallbladder stone removal methods," and the discussion quickly extended from medical information to social media buzz. Related reports framed that controversy within a larger issue: when a doctor and a high-reach influencer discuss health simultaneously, are viewers comparing evidence or comparing who speaks better?[^4]

"Professionalism" is not an automatic pass in the online world. A doctor's training can make a statement worth checking, but it cannot erase all uncertainty. The relatability of an influencer can make information flow more easily, but it cannot replace clinical evidence. Azure Pigeon’s work thus often gets stuck between two expectations: people want him to speak simply, yet they expect him to be responsible for complex issues.

This tension is also evident in his description of his own limitations. In early interviews, he admitted that he could not master every detail like every specialist doctor, so he had to do thorough homework beforehand, at least ensuring the general concept conveyed was sound.[^1] This admission is important because medical popularization is most dangerous when the speaker mistakes familiarity for omniscience.

## Upholding Fairness with a Single Line

Azure Pigeon's content later extended to books, Podcasts, lectures, and health product collaborations. The Apple Podcasts show description listed him as a graduate of NTU, founder of a medical YouTube channel and Facebook fan page, and detailed the program’s activity from 2020 to 2026, covering current medical events, health knowledge, and doctor/nurse interviews.[^6]

The more platforms there are, the harder it is to hide conflicts behind a line like "recommending this to everyone." A 2023 biographical report recorded his principle: "I do not want the channel introducing medical topics to lose its fairness."[^4] This statement is not a denial of all commercial collaborations, but rather separating health-related endorsements from general products. Viewers still need to clearly see the boundaries between content, advertising, and personal experience.

He has also published books on lifestyle medicine and extended book content through lectures at official library events.[^3] The common thread among books, videos, and Podcasts is placing complex knowledge next to everyday problems. The difference lies in that a book allows readers to stop and re-read; a video can be shared during moments of high emotion; and a Podcast provides a longer period of companionship for medical news.

## Book Pages, Audio, and Different Paces of Health Education

Azure Pigeon does not treat all audiences as the same type of person. Some only want to know in short videos whether a symptom is worrying, some listen to full medical news segments on Podcasts, and others prefer to keep an explanation of a childhood illness on paper. These differences are not just content packaging; they relate to whether the medical message can be brought back into daily life.

In a 2020 Fang Gezi interview, he described the three platforms like tables of different sizes: YouTube used drama analysis to bring closeness, Podcasts maintained immediacy with news topics, and Fang Gezi retained long-form text for pediatrics and family medicine.[^1] By 2026, the Apple Podcasts page still positioned the show as medical current affairs analysis, health knowledge popularization, and doctor/nurse interviews, listing 373 episodes. These dynamic data change, yet they allow viewers to see how a creator continuously changes the entry point for the same issue over time.[^6]

Books offer another kind of patience. When Taipei City Library hosted events for his books in 2021, the book introduction divided the content into four aspects—basic medical knowledge, bodily warning signs, common diseases, and smart care under the NHI system—totaling twenty-eight knowledge units.[^3] A video can be re-shared on a phone, but a book requires the reader to sit down. Both are health education, but the shape of the audience's attention is different.

This cross-platform work also brings an easily overlooked reality: popular science is not about copying one manuscript into ten places. Podcasts must handle the rhythm and pauses of sound; videos must use visuals to help viewers grasp key points; and text must retain conditions, exceptions, and terminology. In early interviews, Azure Pigeon said that when he first thought "just record it," he later realized that mouth sounds, echoes, and uneven volume all required post-production.[^1] This small detail speaks more about the creator's daily routine than just "he works hard": there is an entire set of invisible production work beyond the professional content.

## Not All Simplification Is Safe

Medical knowledge must be simplified for the public to understand it. But every simplification leaves something outside the door. Changing "some situations may require medical attention" to "go to the emergency room if you have this symptom" is easier for viewers to remember, but it might distort judgment. Clipping a treatment that is effective under specific conditions into "this method works" turns the video from education into misinformation.

Azure Pigeon stated in an interview that he could not master every detail like all specialist doctors, so he had to do thorough homework before recording to ensure the general concept conveyed was accurate.[^1] This statement does not eliminate the difficulty of medical popularization; rather, it clarifies the difficulty: the speaker must know where to stop, and the audience must also know that a video cannot replace a doctor's judgment on an individual case.

A study from Taiwan Medical Center in 2023 provided another measure. Researchers analyzed 943 videos released by 17 medical centers between December 2019 and August 2021, finding a correlation between video length/likes and view count.[^10] This does not mean "longer is more accurate," nor does it mean view counts prove content quality. It merely reminds us that public health messages are influenced by both accessibility and platform mechanisms.

For Azure Pigeon, this contradiction plays out in front of the lens every day. The content must first get people to click; only then can it discuss evidence. Once humor keeps people engaged, one must avoid the humor making people think there is no risk associated with the illness. This is a creative technique and a boundary of medical ethics.

## The Issue Left by the Elisa Sasa Incident

The reason the gallbladder stone removal controversy is worth including in Azure Pigeon's story is not because it offers a simple answer to any medical dispute, but because it magnifies the question of "who has the right to speak." Azure Pigeon has medical training; Elisa Sasa has massive social reach; and the audience moves between two different senses of trust.[^4]

After the controversy entered social media, evidence was often re-formatted into short phrases. Complete study designs, risk conditions, and target populations might be compressed into an image or a title. A person's professional status can also be used as an authority stamp that requires no further explanation. At this point, the problem facing medical popularizers is not just "what to say," but also "how to make people see why I am saying it."

The approach Azure Pigeon later adopted did not solve this problem with higher volume. He used medical dramas, news, and life issues as entry points, then guided the audience back to a more stable concept. This does not guarantee acceptance by everyone, nor does it eliminate conflict in social media, but it is closer to the original goal of health education than turning every dispute into opposing camps.

## How a Doctor Stays in the Audience's Life

Azure Pigeon’s content also has an often-overlooked dimension: it does not only serve those who already trust medicine. For those familiar with journals and hospital processes, a video might just be a reorganization of basic concepts. For those encountering a child's fever for the first time, understanding NHI rules for the first time, or feeling anxious about vaccine controversies for the first time, this content may be their starting point to keep searching.

This also means that "being relatable" cannot just be a tone. The real work of being relatable is placing medical knowledge next to moments when people make decisions: when a child is uncomfortable in the middle of the night, parents need to know which signs warrant seeking help. When new terminology appears in the news, readers need to know if it relates to their risks. When they see a persuasive health statement, audiences need a way to discern whether it substitutes personal experience for general rules.

When Taipei City Library introduced his books in 2021, they specifically listed "smart care" as a content aspect.[^3] These four words are closer to daily life than "health knowledge," because seeking care is not an exam; patients do not need to be half-doctors to ask questions. If health education only makes people remember terminology but does not teach them how to ask better questions or when to seek professional assistance, the information has not truly arrived.

Therefore, Azure Pigeon's value lies not just in which diseases he discusses, but in making "understanding a bit before asking the doctor" less embarrassing. It is a small but practical public service: moving medicine from an elevated pedestal of authority to a desk people can approach, while reminding everyone that proximity does not equal replacement for clinical practice.

## What the Audience Takes Away

Azure Pigeon's shift from hard knowledge to dramas, current events, and lifestyle medicine did not move away from professional medicine; rather, it more clearly exposed the difficulty of professional communication. Every time a disease is explained in plain language, it might bring people closer, but it might also make them forget conditions and exceptions. Every time humor reduces distance, one must reconfirm that the jokes have not covered up the risks.

So his story is not as simple as "doctors can be online celebrities." It is more like a continuously adjusting public translation: handing knowledge from the clinic to the lens, bringing questions from the lens back for verification, and returning what can be explained clearly to the audience. Research on Taiwanese medical video shows that YouTube can indeed be an accessible channel for delivering health information. Azure Pigeon's case reminds us that when the channel gets closer, the responsibility does not get lighter.[^10]

He once said that the time it takes to make one YouTube episode is equivalent to three or four Podcasts—this is a difference in platforms and a reality of time.[^1] But what truly remains is the recurring action: applying knowledge from books to real people, and bringing back the questions real people ask to the books and evidence.[^4]

After the screen goes dark, viewers may still remember a simple judgment: the doctor's words are worth listening to, but they must be listened to with reasons. Therefore, credibility should not only be built on subscriber numbers, video speed, or a powerful slogan, but on the ability to account for evidence, scope, and uncertainty. This requirement is more demanding of the creator but fairer to the audience.

This might be the part that Azure Pigeon cannot be replaced by traffic. The white coat makes people willing to stop; performance makes them willing to keep listening; what truly keeps the content going is his willingness to deconstruct professionalism, allowing people to see the work involved in verification, selection, and self-limitation. For the audience, this provides a third path that is more useful than "believe or not believe": first confirming who is speaking, what the basis is, where this statement applies, and then deciding whether to continue asking. This way of viewing does not instantly make one an expert, but it reduces the chance of mistaking fame for evidence, and it retains some time for verification when facing medical information next time, without rushing to hand complex health issues over to the loudest answer, while also retaining space and support for everyone's real possibility of seeking professional help.
