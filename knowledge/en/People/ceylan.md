> **30-second overview:** Ceylan is a Mandarin-speaking YouTuber. After Shanghai's lockdown in 2022, he brought his life experiences back to the camera; in 2023, he lived in Taiwan for about six months and obtained a residence permit, but due to being recognized, photo requests, and the stress of being photographed without consent, he announced he was considering moving to Japan. This article does not make a blanket judgment about Taiwan, but instead follows a person who became famous by being seen, and how he re-chose his living location after being seen too much.

In 2022, Ceylan left China after Shanghai's lockdown. In 2023, he lived in Taiwan for about six months and obtained a residence permit. In August of the same year, he announced he was preparing to leave Taiwan and was considering moving to Japan. [^1][^2][^3] This timeline may seem like a record of relocation, but what truly moves people is the contrast within it: a person who built his channel by being "seen," ultimately lost the daily freedom of going out simply because he was too easy to be seen.

## He Was Not Seen Starting from Taiwan

Ceylan is a mixed-race Mandarin-speaking YouTuber and live streamer from Belgium and China. During the Shanghai lockdown in 2022, he recorded his life experiences in videos, describing his experience from the announcement of the lockdown to the distribution of supplies. Newtalk reported that he once said the last supply he received only had 8 eggs. What the audience saw was not only "a foreigner watching China," but also how a person who had lived in Shanghai for a long time suddenly lost the ability to move and arrange his life in a familiar city. [^4]

After leaving Shanghai, Ceylan turned his camera to Taiwan. On April 24, 2023, he posted "After Living for 6 Months, Here Are My Real Thoughts on Taiwan," with video chapters discussing media, transportation, and cityscape in order, while preserving positive observations such as democracy, friendliness, and cost of living. [^5][^6][^15] This structure is important: Ceylan did not only list the shortcomings of Taiwan, but used "expectation gap" as the narrative framework, organizing his living experience into a set of comparative commentary. The report pointed out that he also mentioned Taiwan's freedom of speech, democracy, enthusiastic people, and relatively affordable living. [^5][^6] His tone is often blunt, even intentionally making people uncomfortable, because he is not filming Taiwan as a tourism promotional video, but putting the pedestrian street, road, and news feed that he passes by every day on the same review table. This creative approach also explains why he is easily triggered two completely opposite reactions: supporters think he has said out loud the problems that people are used to, while opponents worry that the short-term living experience of a foreigner is edited into an overly determined conclusion. Ceylan's characteristics are not only in "daring to criticize," but also in packaging his personal feelings into a public text that can be widely forwarded, cited, and refuted.

The importance of this content is that it pushes Ceylan's channel from "escaping from Shanghai" personal witness to a comparative commentary on the place of residence. The core of the Shanghai video is how to obtain food, move, and leave after being locked down. The Taiwan video turns the camera to more daily and harder-to-generalize issues in a democratic society: how the media reports, how transportation distributes risks, and how the city appearance accumulates into residents' habits. The video also arranges sections such as "expectations before coming to Taiwan," "the bigger structure behind the problem," and "some small things," showing that he wants to talk about not only the street view, but also how institutions, habits, and quality of life are interconnected. [^10][^11][^15] Mirror Weekly summarized the three criticisms as media, transportation, and architecture / cityscape issues, but this is still the observation framework of the creator and media, not a complete diagnosis of Taiwanese society.

## Why "Real Thoughts" Caused Controversy

Ceylan's Taiwan video caused discussion, not only because of what he criticized, but also because the identity of the critic became news. Taiwanese media reported his Mandarin pronunciation, mixed-race background, residence permit, and subscription count together. Among them, the report in August 2023 recorded that his channel had about 1.17 million subscriptions at that time, but that was the number at the time and could not be used directly as the current scale. [^1][^2][^7]

He said in the video that Taiwanese people don't have to always seek recognition from the outside, should speak less lies, and be open to advice. Supporters see this as neutral, while opponents may think that a person who has only lived for half a year should not condense a complex society into a few shortcomings. Both sides actually encounter the same problem: YouTube needs a clear position, but social reality rarely has only one answer. The media ecology, transportation, and cityscape can be real problems, but they cannot be concluded only by the observation of a single creator.

More noteworthy is that Ceylan's videos have both "life record" and "argument" functions. The streets, news footage, and personal narration in the camera make the audience feel that they are watching the scene. But when he infers the overall society from a few examples, the audience still need to question whether the sample is sufficient, whether the comparison object is fair, and whether the video omits counterexamples for the sake of rhythm. This is not a requirement only for Ceylan, but a media literacy issue that all commentary creators must face.

## What Finally Left Was Not Taiwan, But the Gaze

In August 2023, Ceylan said he was "not very happy" in Taiwan. TVBS, Mirror Weekly, and CTI News all pointed out that what he cared about most was the freedom to go out: being recognized, being asked to take photos is not entirely unacceptable, but being photographed without consent and having the photos posted online made him gradually reduce his outings. He also mentioned the long-term humid and hot weather in Taipei, and regarded Japan as the next possible living place. [^1][^2][^3][^7]

This departure announcement did not judge Taiwan as "bad." On the contrary, Ceylan still said he had friends in Taiwan and would come back. His judgment is more like "this place is not suitable for me to live in at the moment." For an ordinary office worker, being recognized when going out may be an occasional trouble. For a person whose face and voice are placed on the Internet to work, it may become a cost that needs to be calculated every day. Fame brought him to more people, and also pushed him away from anonymous life.

This timeline is not to compress Ceylan's life into a few years, but to mark how his role in the public discussion has changed: first as a witness to record the restricted life, then as a creator to comment on the place of residence, then as a target of criticism of the media and being reported by the media, and finally having to face the disputes caused by his own commentary. The dates and contents of the events in the timeline shall be subject to the respective page sources. It provides the reading order, not a conclusion for all events.

Ceylan's Taiwan experience cannot be used to make a general comment on Taiwan, nor can his departure be simplified into a piece of gossip about "internet celebrities not liking Taiwan." If you want to understand this experience, you can treat it as a creative trajectory that keeps changing positions: In 2022, the Shanghai lockdown made him a witness to record the restrictions and daily disorder. In 2023, his living experience in Taiwan made him a commentator who compares cities, media, and institutions. Later media criticism, the Dai incident, and the pet communicator incident made him a public figure who is being viewed, cited, and refuted by others. This timeline shows that his content has always been moving between "observing others" and "being observed by others." [^4][^12][^13][^14]

### Pet Communicator: From Debunking to Professional Boundaries

On March 26, 2026, the variety show "Pet Communicator Grand Challenge" was broadcast on Haha Taiwan, and on April 18, Ceylan posted "Pet Communication Killed Me!!! Why Do People Believe This Stuff!!!," raising questions from the perspectives of scientific basis, quantum theory, lost pets, and deceased pets. He then posted a video of taking the course himself, extending the commentary to the path of "watching the program first, then questioning the method." [^12][^16][^17] The incident thus expanded from a web show to a discussion between healing needs and scientific skepticism. PeoPo interviewed pet communicators, pet owners who had used the service, and professionals with a background in animal behavior training: some people see the service as psychological comfort when they lose their pets or face anxiety, while others worry that incorrect specific advice may delay the treatment of animal behavior and health problems. [^12]

The focus of this incident is not to declare "who is definitely correct" for Ceylan, but to separate three different claims. The first is the emotional comfort obtained by the pet owner, which belongs to personal experience; the second is whether the communicator can provide verifiable animal behavior judgments, involving methods and evidence; the third is whether medical or training advice can be put forward, which involves professional qualifications and actual risks. PeoPo's interviews also showed a middle position that should not be ignored: some pet owners said the service did not necessarily find the missing pet, but provided psychological comfort in helplessness; animal behavior trainers believe that if incorrect specific advice is used as the basis for handling, it may cover up the real causes of stress, environment, or disease. [^12] If the three are mixed together, pet communication is likely to be quickly defined between "faith" and "fraud," and the examination of the content and responsibility boundary of the service is lost.

Ceylan's subsequent personal pet communication course added a turn to the method. This does not mean that personal experience is equivalent to completing scientific verification, but it makes his criticism move from watching the program and making intuitive responses to contacting the service process and checking the claims. For the audience, a more prudent reading is to treat the video as an open question and record, rather than to take Ceylan's conclusion, the communicator's statement, or the comments in the comment area as the final answer. [^16][^17]

### Spiritual Course: From Self-Growth to Power Relations

In 2025, Ceylan used a long video "Your Mind Doesn't Need a Course" to organize his investigation of LGAT / three-stage spiritual courses. Next Digital reported that the video was about 2 hours and 20 minutes long, and Ceylan said he spent about two months reading data, contacting researchers and participants, focusing on how the course uses intensive activities, group pressure, and re-interprets personal difficulties to make participants believe they are experiencing major changes. Here, it should be distinguished at two levels: Ceylan put forward the creator's investigation and criticism. The participants, researchers, and helpers in the report each provided different experiences and judgments, which should not be simplified into a single conclusion. [^18]

The reason why such courses are worth discussing is not only because some people believe or don't believe in spiritual growth, but also because the courses may involve specific relationships such as fees, confidentiality, freedom of exit, group pressure, and lecturer authority. The page-by-page review of clinical psychologists reminded that if participants are asked to cut off external information, cannot question the course, or attribute all pain to their own lack of effort, they need to re-examine the informed consent and autonomy. These are the risk frameworks proposed by professional reviewers, not a judicial determination of each spiritual course or individual institution. [^19]

### Communication with Organs and Body: Spiritual Language Meets Medical Risks

In 2026, Ceylan further shot "I Took an Organ Communication Course! The Result Found This Thing Worse Than I Thought..." The official video page lists the definition of organ communication, the intestinal dialogue with pancreatic cancer patients, tumor surgery statements, the origin of theta healing, and the response of Taiwanese spiritual teachers. Social Lab sorted out the discussion volume after the video was released. This incident continues his approach of "entering the scene first, then breaking down the claims," but also pushes the verification responsibility to a higher-risk field: when the course involves cancer, tumors, or surgery, the content cannot be measured only by personal healing experience. [^20][^21]

"Communication with organs and body" can be regarded as a symbolic self-awareness language, such as making people pay attention to stress, pain, or living habits. But if it further claims to read the will of organs, explain the cause of tumors, or imply that the course can replace diagnosis and treatment, it must require higher-level evidence. This article does not make a medical judgment on Ceylan or the course teacher, but establishes a clear boundary: psychological comfort can be understood, while cancer and surgical decisions should be made by qualified medical personnel in accordance with evidence-based medicine. This also makes the organ communication incident different from the pet communication incident - the former directly touches major diseases and medical choices, and cannot be proved by "some people think it is helpful." [^20][^21]

From spiritual courses to organ communication, the problem left by Ceylan's content is not "all mind-body-spirit services should be laughed at," but who puts forward the claim, who bears the consequences of the error, and whether the participants have sufficient information and freedom of exit. This kind of question is more meaningful than simply taking sides, and it can also avoid mixing the emotional needs of individual cases with medical facts.

### Taiwan Media Criticism: Who Decides the Public Narrative

In September 2023, Ceylan posted a video criticizing Taiwan's media for taking things out of context and using titles to attract traffic, and advocated that journalists in a democratic society should not only respond to errors with "freedom of speech," but should be subject to public scrutiny and accountability. The Initium reported that Ceylan named Sanli News Network, which admitted that the relevant journalists did serve, and said it would reflect on the editorial process and invite scholars and social people to launch a news self-discipline committee. The report also recorded the pressure of media practitioners on page views and platform recommendation mechanisms, indicating that the problem is not only about a single title, but also involves the cycle of mutual shaping among media, platforms, and audiences. [^13]

This makes Ceylan not only commenting on Taiwan, but also competing for "who has the right to name the incident." When the media cut a long video into a few quotes, the creator may think his original intention has been rewritten. When the internet celebrity uses a longer and stronger video to counter-attack, the media criticism may be re-packaged into another flow incident. Ceylan's official Taiwan commentary video also lists sections such as "post-truth environment" and "bad media consumption," showing that the media problem in his narrative is not an incidental complaint, but one of the core arguments of the video. [^13][^15] Therefore, the real thing to check is not "who is always right, the internet celebrity or the media," but whether each retelling is fully explained in context, whether the criticized party has the opportunity to respond, and whether there is a traceable correction mechanism after the error occurs.

### The Dai Incident: Criticism May Also Generate New Misunderstandings

The media criticism later extended to the public confrontation between Ceylan and Dai. Newtalk reported that Ceylan called Dai because he thought Dai was affected by the media's out-of-context reporting. After a 40-minute phone call, Ceylan apologized, and Dai said he accepted it. This follow-up supplements what cannot be seen from the live broadcast alone: Ceylan's motivation, Dai's feelings, and the correction after direct communication. [^14]

The Dai incident also formed a reflexive reminder: when the creator opposes the media's use of fragments to create conclusions, his own live broadcast fragments may also be extracted from the context by the audience and media. Public criticism does not automatically guarantee that the judgment is correct, but requires the creator to bear higher verification and correction responsibilities. And the audience cannot take "what I see in the fragments" as the complete incident.

### Conclusion: From Being Seen to Learning to Take Responsibility

Therefore, what Ceylan left behind is not a set of Taiwan ratings that can be copied, but three continuing problems: when the creator puts forward criticism, where are the evidence and boundaries; when the media retells the internet celebrity, whether the context is fully explained; when the audience shares and watches, whether they simplify a person's life, profession, and mistakes into consumable drama. Ceylan became famous by being seen, but left because he was seen too much; in the pet communication, Taiwan media, and Dai incidents, he also became part of the public gaze mechanism he criticized. This is not a period about whether Taiwan is good or bad, but a conclusion about how the digital age observes, verifies, and takes responsibility.
