> **30-second overview:** In 2021, Cheap used war scenes from Age of Empires to narrate world history; the channel already had 810,000 subscribers. Two years later, he stepped onto the front lines of public issues, calling for the "Return the Road to the People" grand march. The most counterintuitive part of this journey is: he didn't become an expert before speaking out, but rather became a compelling storyteller first, and only then suddenly gained the reach that required him to be accountable for the consequences of his words. [^1] [^2] [^3]

In 2021, Zheng Caiwei's channel had 810,000 subscribers. ETtoday reported that he used game war scenes to narrate world history. A later interview published by the New Taipei City Government Youth Bureau labeled his channel at 1.33 million subscribers. Numbers change, but that image remains the same: a person takes armies, castles, and resources on screen and uses them to narrate real-world history. [^1] [^2]

Cheap is not a history professor, nor does he package himself as a researcher. In a 2021 debate with "History Brother from Kaohsiung," he directly stated: "I'm just a YouTuber; don't call me teacher, don't call me a historian," and described his work as "providing interesting and fun historical knowledge under correct premises." [^3]

📝 **Curator's note:** What's most worth writing about Cheap isn't the binary question of whether he "knows history" or not, but rather the harder question he raises: when a storyteller is heard by the public before scholars, who decides how much room is left between "interesting" and "correct"?

## First Find a Sustainable Method, Then a Professional Title

Cheap's starting point lacks the neatness of a typical celebrity story. In an interview recorded by the New Taipei City Government Youth Bureau, he worked in web design in Taichung for about six years, starting at around NT$30,000, with overtime reaching up to ten hours a day, yet his salary didn't increase with experience. After a breakup, he began livestreaming Age of Empires to chat with people, even titling his stream directly as "Heartbreak Age of Empires." The livestream unexpectedly brought in about NT$22,000 per month. These numbers aren't a complete income history, but they are an exit he could see when transitioning from employment to content creation. [^2]

His educational path isn't a straight line to history either. Zheng Caiwei attended Songshan Vocational High School's Department of National Trade and Business, discovering that the history and geography he once liked weren't in the curriculum. Later, he re-entered Shih Chien University's Department of Japanese Language and Literature, minoring in history. Describing this experience as "too fast to be called 'overcoming the trade department'" is inaccurate. A more precise way to put it is that he preserved his non-major interests, allowing them to become reusable content material over the years. [^2]

The significance of this experience lies not in providing a replicable success formula for everyone, but in showing that Cheap first found a repeatable method: talking about things he's familiar with to strangers, observing where the audience stays, and then connecting the next topic. Livestreaming, game footage, and historical data thus became not three separate interests, but gradually combined into a workflow. [^2] [^12]

## How a Game Screen Becomes the Gateway to History Class

Cheap's early recognition came from turning the war scenes of Age of Empires into narrative backgrounds. A 2021 report said he narrated world history this way, with the channel already having 810,000 subscribers. That same year, video clips were also uploaded to bilibili, turning him from a content creator into someone who had to deal with copyright and cross-platform reuse. [^1]

Games here aren't just decoration. They provided a lower entrance to history, allowing audiences to approach unfamiliar eras through troop types, castles, and battles. An interview by the New Taipei City Government Youth Bureau described his turning point bluntly: he once described himself as an underperforming, low-salary office worker, later entering YouTube work because of his videos. [^2]

Game footage also has its limitations. It condenses complex matters into memorable battles, characters, and outcomes, making history possibly look like a strategic map that can be quickly judged. In the 2021 "History Internet Celebrity" debate, his opponent argued: "Just because a YT video is popular doesn't mean you're a good storyteller, let alone a historian." This isn't a judgment on Cheap's academic credentials, but a reminder of the role of internet knowledge. [^3]

📝 **Curator's note:** Game footage makes history easy to enter, but doesn't guarantee there are no forks in the road afterward. What Cheap's method truly changes is who can begin to take an interest in history. It doesn't automatically solve the question of "how to verify."

## From Narrating the Past to Intervening in the Present

Cheap's public role didn't appear overnight. In 2022, he publicly questioned the claim "motorcycles should yield to large trucks" due to the content of a traffic safety promotion script. Taipei City Traffic Bureau responded, saying this wasn't a traffic regulation, and explained that the focus of the collaboration was "don't drive alongside large vehicles, stay away from large vehicles." [^8]

This incident was small, yet it well illustrates his working style: he doesn't just organize others' information into videos, but also puts collaborative texts back into public scrutiny. Of course, public scrutiny doesn't mean his every judgment is correct. It just makes the originally private communication between creators and the government into a public controversy that the audience can see. [^8]

In 2023, this intervention expanded into traffic advocacy. Newtalk reported that Cheap announced on August 20th a "Return the Road to the People Grand March," placing the issue of approximately 3,000 annual traffic deaths in Taiwan against the backdrop of "per capita $30,000." The same year, a feature interview by The News Lens summarized his turning point: from narrating history and gaining a million fans, to organizing a pedestrian rights march on the streets. [^7]

In the march advocacy, Cheap said: "Let the pedestrian hell stay with us in this generation." This sentence's power doesn't lie in providing a traffic policy, but in replacing cold accident statistics with generational promises: the next generation doesn't have to inherit the same fear.

## Reach on the Crosswalk Line, and the Reaction Force of Reach

The traffic advocacy brought Cheap into direct contact with the scene. TVBS reported that he shared a photo of about 50 elementary school students in Fengshan, Kaohsiung, using ropes to separate walking space. He criticized that even basic sidewalks were missing, yet they still talked about major construction projects. The report also preserved another side's opinion: parents doing pickup/drop-off illegally parked might also be part of the road danger. [^9]

The ropes aren't just props in the photo. They pull the traffic issue back to the actual scene where parents, drivers, schools, road design, and local enforcement are intertwined. The problem isn't just "the government hasn't done enough," but also includes the choices of parents, drivers, schools, road design, and local enforcement. Cheap's post can make the photo seen by more people, but the photo doesn't automatically tell the audience how responsibility is distributed. That still requires institutions, data, and responses from different roles.

In December 2023, the "Imperial Clause" artwork controversy pushed this reaction force to its peak. A junior high school student in Taoyuan won a special award in a national art competition with a comic about pedestrians and vehicles. Cheap believed the work vilified pedestrians, publicly demanding the school and organizing unit face the award issue. United Daily News reported lawyer Lu Qiu-yuan's rebuttal, believing one shouldn't use the million-follower status to pressure a junior high school student. [^4]

The News Lens' report added the social context of the event: the school once deleted the congratulatory text, and a public form supporting the students received 8,435 letters. On December 15th, Cheap publicly apologized, pulling the controversy back to the long-unresolved pedestrian rights issue. [^5]

He said in his apology text: "Yet even with my scars, on the road of traffic reform, 'Though it be ten thousand people, I will proceed.'" This sentence can be understood as persistence, or as a warning: if public advocacy only remains as "I'm not afraid of being cursed," it might forget that those pulled into it don't necessarily have the same microphone, fans, and endurance.

📝 **Curator's note:** Cheap's controversy isn't an incidental side note. The controversy itself is the story. When a person turns "speaking for pedestrians" into a personal brand, every move simultaneously advances the issue and changes the focus of the issue—from road systems to "whether this person said it right, overstepped."

## Two Controversies: Content Sources and Friend Ethics

In November 2023, Cheap faced two different types of questioning at the same time. The first is about content sources: a PTT user accused his "Mystery of Evil Matsu" video of being similar to a 2020 article, pointing out that the original article had deliberately fabricated historical details. The Liberty Times reported the accusation, Cheap's response, and the original author's refusal to accept the explanation, but this report presents a public controversy, not a court ruling or completed academic identification, so the article cannot write "suspected plagiarism" as confirmed plagiarism. [^10]

Cheap's response also exposed the most difficult responsibility boundary for knowledge-type channels. He admitted that he once directly referenced Wikipedia, later gradually adding library books and professional data. He also said sometimes he omits sources, and apologizes when he omits them. The problem isn't just "whether there's a reference," but whether the audience can see where each layer of data comes from, and whether the creator treats unverified stories as true history. [^10] [^11]

The second type of questioning comes from friend ethics. After Joeman publicly apologized for a marijuana case, Cheap once wrote on Instagram Stories "I support Joeman! This isn't a big deal," which led to netizens questioning his past advocacy of law-abiding behavior, now being lenient towards his friend's illegal acts. Sanli News Network reported that Cheap later stated he didn't support drug use. He said he didn't want his friend to be criticized by everyone after making a mistake. He also admitted "I shouldn't have said Joeman's marijuana possession is a minor issue." [^11]

The controversy here isn't simply "whether friends can support friends." It's about whether public figures can clearly separate private support from public judgment. One understandable part of Cheap's defense is that wrongdoers shouldn't be permanently excluded. Critics' doubts are equally valid: when a creator who has long talked about law-abiding and public responsibility posts support for a friend, the audience has reason to question whether he's using the same ruler or a different ruler that only applies to close friends. [^11]

Looking at both controversies together, we get a more concrete conclusion than "Cheap is often in trouble." The content source controversy requires him to account for how knowledge is organized, the friend ethics controversy requires him to account for how values are applied. The former concerns traceability, the latter concerns consistency. These two requirements are exactly the cost that knowledge-type internet celebrities must bear after moving from "being watched" to "being relied upon." [^10] [^11]

📝 **Curator's note:** What's truly worth preserving isn't to judge Cheap's innocence or guilt, but to break the controversy into checkable issues—whether sources are listed, whether errors are corrected, whether positions are consistent, and whether those pulled into it have a voice.

## Internet Celebrities Are Not Mini-Governments, Nor Are They Irresponsible Passersby

Cheap's case stacks three roles in Taiwan's internet public domain. The first is the storyteller: he uses game footage to turn history into shareable narratives. The second is the commentator: he judges traffic policies, public information, and social events. The third is the mobilizer: he can lead audiences to marches, signatures, or controversy scenes. [^1] [^6] [^7]

Storytellers, commentators, and mobilizers face different responsibilities. Storytellers need to be accountable for historical materials and wording. Commentators need to separate opinions from facts. Mobilizers must anticipate how their words are retransmitted, and what the weaker parties in the event will bear. The 2023 "Imperial Clause" controversy is important precisely because it made the intention "I'm only targeting the system, not the child" encounter the actual effect: schools, students, and families were all pulled into public opinion. [^4] [^5]

This also explains why Cheap shouldn't be simplified as a "history YouTuber." That title only describes his starting point, not how his influence extends beyond the video. A more accurate description is: he's an internet creator who started with knowledge content, later bringing the storytelling reach into traffic and public issues. And public issues will in turn demand higher intensity scrutiny from him.

## How One Person Was Pushed to the Center of the Public Domain

Cheap's rise to fame is quite different from the traditional celebrity path. He didn't first have a TV show, publisher, or political party position, then bring his opinions to the audience. He first accumulated a group of people willing to listen to his stories on the platform, and later discovered that these people would also take his traffic judgments as public signals. [^1] [^2] [^7]

This platform power has an easily overlooked object: the share button on the phone. When historical videos are shared, the audience mostly takes away a story. When traffic controversies are shared, they might take away accusations against schools, children, or certain groups. The content format looks the same, but the actual risks are not the same. This is exactly the media ethics that knowledge-type internet celebrities must relearn after entering social issues.

The 2021 copyright incident also provides another perspective. Cheap discovered that screenshots of his videos were used to make others' videos, and the report quoted him saying: "This behavior is worse than stealing a video." For creators, this sentence points to the fact that when content is taken apart, the original context and labor are also taken away. But in public controversies, the audience might also only excerpt one sentence from him, letting the original context be reassembled.

Therefore, Cheap's story is not just "from a loser to a million-subscriber YouTuber." The New Taipei City Government Youth Bureau used this inspirational framework in the title, while The News Lens put him at the intersection of internet celebrity economy and pedestrian rights advocacy. The truly worth-preserving part is the gap between frameworks: a person who succeeded through entertaining narratives must eventually face issues that are not suitable for entertainment, accepting the public's questioning of accuracy, proportion, and consequences.

📝 **Curator's note:** The public nature of internet celebrities isn't because they claim to represent anyone, but because someone brings their words into their own judgment. This influence can make issues visible, but it can also compress complex problems into the easiest-to-share enemy-friend sentences.

## Don't Mistake Reach for an Answer

Cheap's case also reminds readers that internet influence and professional qualifications are two different resources. In the 2021 debate, his opponent questioned his rigor with historical training. Cheap admitted he was a YouTuber, emphasizing that he provided interesting historical knowledge. Both sides are actually answering different questions: the former asks "who has the qualification to produce historical knowledge," the latter asks "how to make more people willing to approach history."

Both questions can be true at the same time. Audiences can use videos as an entrance, then go read monographs, archives, and research. Creators can also admit that narratives are simplified for clarity, and tell the audience about these choices. The truly dangerous thing isn't non-academic background, but mistaking traffic for correctness, and mistaking the number of opponents as evidence.

Traffic issues are especially so. The 2023 march reported by Newtalk put "approximately 3,000 people die on the road every year" into the advocacy context. The photo of elementary school students using ropes to walk the sidewalk reported by TVBS turned abstract traffic safety into a line that students had to draw for themselves. These materials can illustrate the urgency of the problem, but they can't decide alone which road project, enforcement measure, or regulatory amendment is most effective.

The lesson left by the "Imperial Clause" incident is exactly to separate the heat of the issue from the way it's handled. Cheap's concern for pedestrian rights is real, and the critics' worry about junior high school students being pulled into it is also real. The two can't offset each other with "who cares more about pedestrians."

## How Influence Is Measured

If we only measure Cheap by subscription count, we get a quick but shallow answer. ETtoday's 2021 report recorded 810,000 subscriptions. The New Taipei City Government Youth Bureau's follow-up interview recorded 1.33 million. These numbers can explain the scale, but not what concepts the audience actually took away. [^1] [^2]

Another way to measure is to see whether a creator can connect personal interests to public issues. Cheap went from historical videos to traffic safety, from posts to marches, creating a continuous relationship between "content" and "action." [^6] [^7] But continuous relationships aren't causal proof: marches don't happen just because of one person's post, and traffic reform doesn't complete just because of one video.

A third way to measure is to see whether clearer questions remain after the controversy. The controversy with Taipei City Traffic Bureau left behind how promotional content is inspected. The "Imperial Clause" left behind how advocates face weak stakeholders. The elementary school students' rope sidewalk left behind who should be responsible for basic walking space. [^4] [^8] [^9]

These three measurement methods together are closer to Cheap's position. He's not a policy maker, nor an academic reviewer, nor the sole spokesperson for all pedestrian experiences. He's a person who can push topics into public view, and once topics enter the view, they must be handed over to more people, more data, and longer institutional work.

This also makes "internet celebrity" no longer just a job title. It's a capability to allocate attention, and attention will affect which accident is seen, which word is repeated, and which person is pushed to the center of public opinion. If understanding Cheap only stops at "he's good at going viral," the real important media turning point will be missed.

## Conclusion: The Entrance Is Still There, and the Road Is Still There

Cheap's importance doesn't lie in whether he can replace historians or traffic experts. His existence proves that Taiwan's public knowledge isn't only produced in schools, newspapers, and government websites, but also re-packaged and redistributed in a video with game footage and narration.

But the entrance isn't the end. Audiences can start researching history because of a battle, and can start paying attention to zebra crossings because of a pedestrian rights video. Afterwards, they still need to return to regulations, statistics, scenes, and the experiences of different people. Cheap brings people to the door, and the complexity behind the door can no longer be replaced by one person's reach.

In 2023, he said he hoped "to let the pedestrian hell stay with us in this generation." This sentence shouldn't belong to just one internet celebrity in the end. It should be broken down into questions that everyone can ask: who designs the road, who enforces the rules, who bears the risk, and whether we're willing to see who else is in the frame before the next share.
