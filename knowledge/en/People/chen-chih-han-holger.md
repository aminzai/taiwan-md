> **30-Second Overview:** The Gym Boss did not start in political commentary; he started in a gym. Chen Zhi-han placed weight training, combat sports, e-commerce, and live streaming on the same stage, allowing viewers to see a boss who binds the body, enterprise, and personal trust together. He later brought this format of "I am on the scene, you follow my eyes" into anti-red media mobilization, live streaming in China, and cross-strait issues. Whether this trajectory should be called a pivot, an extension, or simply the continuous deformation of a personal brand, the article leaves the question to the data and readers, refraining from drawing conclusions about the figure in advance.

Chen Zhi-han was exposed to fitness and sanda (Chinese kickboxing) in his early years. At 17, he began studying combat sports under Bi Jing-long, the chairman of the Chinese Sanda Association. In a 2017 interview, he recalled that a muscular appearance without speed, rhythm, and technique was still insufficient for actual combat; this view of the body as something that can be trained, tested, and verified later became the brand language of Genghis Khan Fitness Club. [^1]

In the same interview, he discussed the entrepreneurial process: early on, due to insufficient funds, he first opened a pub, recruited apprentices, and then found investors; the Lin-kou flagship branch required an investment of approximately NT$100 million, with actual capital needed for the venue, equipment, and safety facilities. [^1] Thus, "The Gym Boss" held two identities from the start: a coach teaching people to train their bodies, and a boss handling leases, equipment, employees, and cash flow. Later live streams were not like simple talk shows precisely because they were connected from the beginning to a real, operating enterprise.

## The Gym as the First Studio

In the 2017 interview, The Gym Boss talked about more than just muscles. He discussed Taiwan's sports training, scientific division of labor, and athletes gaining experience abroad, while also criticizing Taiwan for often treating athletes as scapegoats for failures. [^1] He understood supporting athletes as a long-term investment: sending athletes abroad and passing their experience to the next generation. These contents shaped his public image before a clear political stance emerged: a person impatient with the system yet willing to discuss how to improve it.

His live streams turned this impatience into a format. Long duration, minimal editing, and direct responses to comments made viewers feel they were not watching a post-event speech, but standing by as a boss handled today's problems. This sense of immediacy shortened the distance of traditional media and weakened the buffer provided by traditional editors: a sentence can quickly reach a large audience and be clipped, shared, and detached from its original context within minutes.

English media refers to him as the "Gym Boss," describing him as a fitness influencer who moved from a fitness enterprise to commenting on politics and cross-strait issues. [^2] This title captures his brand posture: he does not speak with the authority of a scholar or official, but with the posture of a boss who has "lived through it, fought in it, and made money." Supporters see this as authenticity; critics ask whether personal experience can replace data, procedures, and professional review.

## From Anti-Red Media to Political Microphone

In June 2019, The Gym Boss co-organized the "Reject Red Media, Protect Taiwan Democracy" march with then-New Power Party legislator Huang Kuo-chang, gathering people on Ketagalan Boulevard. The demands included opposing infiltration by the Chinese Communist Party and pro-China media, protecting Taiwan's democratic freedoms, and supporting the Hong Kong anti-extradition movement. In 2025, reports reviewing this mobilization also recorded The Gym Boss's later statement in live streams that he would apologize and express gratitude if he met the owners of China Times and China Television. [^3]

The timeline here can be confirmed, but the meaning of the timeline should not be decided by the article on behalf of the reader. The early march demands, the later live stream statements, and the 2025 trip to China are indeed placed in the same set of public discussions; but naming this as a "pivot," "route change," or other political judgment must preserve the subject's own account and the statements of different observers.

The difference between influencer politics and party politics emerges here. The Gym Boss does not need to first propose a complete political platform, go through party nominations, or vote item by item in the legislature; he can open a live stream, issue a call, and gather dispersed emotions onto the streets. The cost is that this political trust is placed in personal tone and personal commitment. When the individual's public statements change, followers also treat "what you said before" as a political ledger.

## The China Trip: Self-Funded Travel or a Watched Exchange?

In June 2025, The Gym Boss went to Shanghai for a six-day, five-night China live streaming trip. He called himself a "Peace Ambassador," stating he wanted Taiwan viewers to see China with their own eyes; the Taiwan Affairs Office of the PRC publicly stated that The Gym Boss and his team went to Shanghai on their own, welcoming Taiwan compatriots to exchange with China. [^4]

The _South China Morning Post_ reported that The Gym Boss described this trip as self-funded travel, hoping to show Taiwan viewers the "unedited" face of Chinese society; he also stated in live streams that Shanghai's construction and quality of life were better than he had originally imagined. [^2] The key is not whether viewers agree with his observations, but that "unedited" itself still involves choices: which streets, malls, city constructions, and escorts he chose also determined which China the viewers saw.

Yahoo News recorded The Gym Boss's departure, the "Peace Ambassador" statement, the itinerary, and the Taiwan Affairs Office's response to his independent travel and cross-strait exchange. [^4] _The Reporter_ scraped data on public live streams, viewership, and donations, and traced the Chinese influencers, media, officials, and cross-border content networks appearing in the itinerary. [^5]

These data can be placed side by side, but cannot be directly pieced into a single answer. The Gym Boss's own account is self-funded and folk exchange; the Chinese official side says he traveled independently; investigative reporting focuses on the escorts and content networks. The article can present how these statements collide, but does not decide for the reader what nature this trip actually is.

Another investigation by _The Reporter_ further used the case of Li Xiang, a Chinese influencer who hosted The Gym Boss in Hangzhou, to analyze the traceable links between his media platforms, united front organizations, and cross-border content networks. [^6] This makes the "China Trip" not just a travelogue of which cities a person walked through, but also a question: are the people in the lens, those arranging the scenes, and those providing the platforms all jointly deciding what the audience sees?

## Live Streaming is Not a Transparent Glass

"Full-process live streaming" sounds like removing editing, but it is actually still a form of choice. Which city to start from, which street scene enters the frame, who can speak in front of the lens, and what stays out of the frame are all part of the content. Immediacy does not mean no framework; no editing does not mean no framing.

This difference is particularly important in cross-border content. Taiwan viewers see several visible aspects of the Shanghai trip; Chinese viewers might see how an influencer from Taiwan describes China; the Taiwan government sees a live stream crossing information controls and cross-strait political contexts. [^7] These observations can coexist, but none can single-handedly cover the entire reality.

In July 2025, The Gym Boss appeared on the screen of China's Central Television (CCTV), with the mosaics on his arm tattoos post-processed. Taiwan media recorded the community mocking it as "Invisible Wings"; The Gym Boss himself stated he could understand it and considered appearing on CCTV something to be happy about. [^8] Here, at least three layers of fact cannot be mixed: the image being censored, how netizens referred to it, and how The Gym Boss interpreted it. As for CCTV's specific internal decision-making, existing articles do not provide sufficient evidence for this piece to draw a conclusion.

The mosaic is worth observing not because the tattoo itself can prove a person's political stance, but because it places a body once used to build a "dare to speak, dare to fight, dare to act" image into another set of public image rules. For some Taiwan viewers, this is like entering another market and accepting the other side's image rules; for The Gym Boss, it might just be an experience of appearing on a large platform. Both interpretations should be marked as interpretations, not written as the same fact.

## The Boundaries of "Daring to Speak"

The Gym Boss's directness is both brand asset and a source of risk. In October 2025, _Taipei Times_ reported that he discussed China's "decapitation" tactics against Taiwan in a live stream, using expressions like "cut Lai Ching-te's head off"; Chen Zhi-han told media that his words were taken out of context, that he was restating a military concept and opposing war, while the Criminal Investigation Bureau had already launched an investigation. [^9] Here, the investigation cannot be written as a conviction, nor can his defense be deleted; the public significance lies in the fact that irony, restatement, and threats in live streams can produce different effects after leaving the original context.

In November of the same year, _Taiwan News_ reported that former partners accused The Gym Boss of receiving money from the Chinese side and criticizing the Taiwan government via live streams, also recording Chen Zhi-han admitting to seeking financial opportunities in the Chinese market, stating he misjudged the situation, and denying the relevant accusations. [^10] The article only writes this as accusations and responses carried by the media, not determining facts for either side. For a figure like The Gym Boss, the verification challenge lies in the fact that fan support, corporate interests, political speech, and judicial or workplace disputes appear simultaneously in the same personal brand.

## The Two Faces of Influencer Publicness

The Gym Boss's story should not be simplified to "a fitness influencer suddenly became a political figure." He still speaks through his body. _TaiwanPlus_ reported he participated in a Brazilian Jiu-Jitsu competition, winning the first match but withdrawing from subsequent events due to arm injury, also recording his promotion of the sport through participation. [^11] The body is still his public language, but this language now discusses sports, enterprise, cross-strait issues, media, and national identity simultaneously.

His influence comes not just from follower count, but from a seemingly contradictory combination: he lowers the threshold for understanding by saying "I am not an expert, I just say what I see"; precisely because he is seen as unadorned, some viewers may also treat intuition as a more credible judgment than institutions. This relationship might be encouraging during fitness teaching, but requires higher data transparency in public policy and cross-strait politics.

The gap in viewership across different platforms and stages also reminds us that volume does not equal stable audience, nor does it equal long-term trust. Viewers can support his exchange with China, or question the choices and escorts of the trip; can accept his claim of being misunderstood, or demand full live streams, cash flow, and interest relationships be examined. Publicness is not an honor automatically gifted by fan count, but an accountability that must be borne after every speech.

Chen Zhi-han walked from a gym boss to a live streamer, and then to the protagonist of cross-strait exchange, not by leaving the original business model, but by pushing the model onto a larger stage. The gym provides bodily authority, live streaming provides immediate trust, e-commerce provides income, and political conflict provides reasons for continuous viewing. These four stacked together formed the role of "The Gym Boss," which is larger than a personal resume.

Therefore, understanding The Gym Boss does not require first answering "which side he is on." A more useful question is: on each stage, what does he choose to put into the lens, what does he leave out, and when viewers demand verification, how much is he willing to hand over. As for how to name the gap between the 2019 anti-red media mobilization and the 2025 China live stream, this piece gives no answer—public records can confirm time and statements; naming is a judgment the reader must make themselves.
