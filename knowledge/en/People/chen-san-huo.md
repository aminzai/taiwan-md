> **30-second overview:** Chen San-huo originally followed his elder brother to temples and slept on construction sites as a cut-and-paste apprentice. In 2002, while restoring Fengyuan's Ciji Temple after the 921 earthquake, he picked up a discarded broken vase, accidentally created a Damo figure, and later turned "breaking and then finding the shape" into his own method. This shift mirrors a craft paradox: to preserve cut-and-paste, one cannot simply copy the past unchanged; sometimes it must first lose its original form.

In 2002, Fengyuan's Ciji Temple in Taichung was still being restored after the 921 earthquake. Chen San-huo, born in Tainan's Madou Township, picked up a discarded broken vase on the construction site and assembled a Damo figure. The piece was not pre-drawn with an outline and then filled with ceramic shards, but rather began from the fragments left by the breakage. This chance encounter later became his most recognizable creative method.[^1]

He later named this approach "knocking instead of cutting": first striking old vases, wine bottles, or ceramic jars, then tracing the curves and textures of the shards to find figures, folds of clothing, or animal shapes. Cut-and-paste thus shifted from temple ridge decorations to a three-dimensional art form that preserves the damage of materials.[^2] This is not pretending waste is new material, but making cracks part of the composition.

📝 **Curator's note:** The most easily misunderstood aspect of traditional craftsmanship is the assumption that "preservation" means "no change." Chen San-huo's answer is the opposite: some skills can only continue to be seen if the way materials are used is first changed.

## Apprentice Starting with Glass Cutting

Chen San-huo was born in 1949 in Tainan's Madou Township. His father, Chen Tien-ao, worked in oil painting and color decoration, originally intending for him to learn wood carving. But during a summer visit to Madou to see his father, he was drawn to the craft of glass cutting practiced by his elder brother Li Shi-yi's master, transforming hard materials into the curved lines of a roof ridge. After graduating from junior high school, he apprenticed under Li Shi-yi to learn temple cut-and-paste.[^3]

Li Shi-yi was a descendant of Hong Kunfu's lineage. Chen San-huo's apprentice life was unlike a craft classroom: he followed his elder brother across Taiwan to visit temples, sleeping on the ground and waking up early to cook. The artist file preserved by the National Taiwan Craft Research and Development Center even recorded an episode where he mistook midnight for dawn due to the bright moonlight and got up to cook.[^3]

This lifestyle broke technique down into many unromantic details: identifying the thickness of different ceramic shards, knowing whether the clay skeleton can bear weight, distinguishing the direction of a figure's robes, and facing sun and rain on the rooftop for long periods of squatting. Cut-and-paste is not just sticking beautiful shards on; it first requires scaffolding, clay, and shaping, with ceramic pieces merely being the final clothing.

According to data from the Tainan City Cultural Heritage Management Division, Chen San-huo specializes in ceramic stacking and splicing, with works appearing in Tainan's Zhengzi'li Fangan Temple, Xin'ning's Zhenwu Hall, Fengyuan's Ciji Temple, Dajia's Zhenlan Temple, and Beigang's Chaotian Temple. These names together reveal the scale of his work: he is not imagining temples in a studio, but leaving his craftsmanship on the rooftops of actual temples.

## The Slow Craft on the Temple Ridge, Nearly Lost to the Next Generation

Cut-and-paste is a craft that bends iron wire into scaffolding, uses clay to build the rough shape, and then pastes ceramic shards or glass. Traditional methods require a clear form first, then cutting colored ceramics into the required parts with tools. Figures, deities, birds, beasts, and historical figures are thus fixed on the ridges and walls of temples.

Its crisis did not occur suddenly. The Tainan City Cultural Heritage Management Division records that from the 1960s, the lower-cost, faster-completion "pouring slip" method rose, causing the traditional slow-craft cut-and-paste to decline. International media reports also point out that cut-and-paste has a history of several hundred years in Taiwan, but the refined techniques require long-term training; when demand decreases, the master-apprentice system weakens accordingly.

In the 1980s, rising wages and changes in temple construction made the time-honored production methods harder to maintain. After Li Shi-yi passed away in 2003, Chen San-hoo felt the fragility of this craft's life anew. He asked himself, if the history of cut-and-paste might be lost, what could he preserve first. This question pushed his work from "completing a temple" to "finding the next field for the craft."

📝 **Curator's note:** The disappearance of a craft usually does not happen overnight, but through each person learning a little less, waiting a little less, and investing a little less time. True innovation sometimes lies in making something worth waiting for within the increasingly narrow seam of time.

## "Following Fortune, Not My Will" Is Not Abandoning Control

Chen San-hoo's creative shift is often oversimplified as "turning a broken vase into art." But breakage itself is not his entire method. The National Taiwan Craft Research and Development Center points out that naturally broken shards still require judgment: the force of the strike, the point of impact, the thickness and curves of the shards all affect whether the final piece can stand.

This is also why "chance" cannot be understood as randomness. Traditional cut-and-paste determines the form first, then cuts materials into parts that conform to the shape. Chen San-hoo lets the shards break first, then identifies possible forms from the fragments. Both methods require skill, but the relationship of power between creator and material is redistributed.

When the National Craft Achievement Award was presented in 2021, the National Taiwan Craft Research and Development Center used the inscription "Following Fortune, Opening Hands" to express respect, and the media recorded Chen San-hoo's creative concept of "following fortune, not my will." This phrase does not tell people to stop judging, but acknowledges that a broken vase will not entirely follow the author's original intention.

In Tainan's Zhengzi'li Fangan Temple, Taiwan Panorama's English report saw the concrete results of this method: the robes of the deity Xiantian use black wine jar shards, the clam spirit shells in the Nezha story come from everyday white plates, and even commercial lettering on tableware can be seen. Ordinary objects did not lose their identity, but brought local life into the deity's garments.

## From Temple Components to Exhibition Works

Chen San-hoo did not become an artist only after leaving the temples. He learned how materials withstand wind and rain, how figures are seen from a distance, and encountered the body language of the Eight Generals, divine generals, and local beliefs in the restoration. The data from the craft center describes how he based his work on the facial patterns of the Madou area, creating the Eight Generals series, allowing hard ceramic pieces to show the wrinkles, fluttering, and gradients of clothing.

This transition also changed the viewing distance. The cut-and-paste on the rooftop is for the entire neighborhood to see, requiring bright colors and exaggerated outlines to resist distance. The works in the exhibition allow the audience to approach and see the patterns on broken shards, the curves of wine bottles, and the traces left by hand-sculpting. Chen San-hoo placed the same set of techniques in two scales, without erasing the temple experience.

When the Ministry of Culture introduced Chen San-hoo's solo exhibition in 2024, the exhibition featured 32 pieces along with tools and materials, divided into four areas presenting his creative starting point, technical shift, sustainable material exploration, and imagination for craft innovation. The key to this exhibition is not moving the temple ridge into a white box, but letting the audience see a pile of discarded trays, bottles, jars, and striking tools before seeing each piece.

The report from Taiwan News placed his career in another perspective: he has restored or created over 100 temple projects, later using recycled ceramics, vases, and glass bottles as main media, and was selected from 21 senior craftsmen to win the 2021 National Craft Achievement Award. The numbers reveal the scale, while the broken vase reminds us where the turning point occurred.

## Preservers Are Not Endpoints

In 2020, the Ministry of Culture identified Chen San-hoo as the preserver of the national important traditional craft "cut-and-paste craft," becoming the first "living national treasure" preserver of this craft. In 2021, he won the National Craft Achievement Award.[^4] [^8] These recognitions provided public resources and a name for the craft, but also raised a more difficult question: after a craft is listed, who will spend years learning it?

Chen San-hoo's approach is to take the inheritance out of the master's personal body. He founded the "Cut-and-Paste Family" to teach apprentices and participated in campus craft promotion. Since 2021, the Cultural Heritage Bureau has organized the "Important Traditional Craft Cut-and-Paste Learning Program," providing guidance to selected apprentices.[^4] The Central News Agency also reported that when receiving the award, he understood the honor as a reminder to himself to let more people understand cut-and-paste and pass it on.[^8]

This path is still unstable. The demand for temple projects, whether apprentices can make a living from it, and whether traditional themes will be fixed into an unchangeable style are problems no award can solve. Chen San-hoo's own file is also honest: temple cut-and-paste work has decreased, and he still needs to farm vegetables and fruits to make a living.[^3]

Therefore, his innovation is not a beautiful success story. It contains labor market pressure, the loss of a senior brother, the reuse of waste, and a craftsman's insistence that materials should not remain merely "materials." The future of cut-and-paste is not only on the ridge, but also in whether the next apprentice can understand: the shape of a broken shard is not an error, it may be where the work begins.

📝 **Curator's note:** What Chen San-hoo truly preserves may not be just the method of cut-and-paste, but the right of a craftsman to negotiate with materials. When a vase breaks, the creator does not rush to repair it back to its original form. He first listens to what the shards can still become.

Cut-and-paste has never been mere decoration. It layers the faith of temples, local aesthetics, the time of master and apprentice, and the weather on the rooftop into one surface. Chen San-hoo added a crack to this surface, and people can see both sides of the crack: one side is the technique that needs to be preserved, the other is the technique that will remain only as a specimen if it no longer dares to take risks.

What he picked up at Fengyuan was not just a vase that could be used, but the beginning of a working method. First let the ceramic pieces lose their original form, then search for figures, clothing, and fate among the fragments. This "following fortune, not my will" craftsmanship ultimately still requires a pair of hands that knows exactly what they are doing.
