> **30-Second Overview**: Starting from the Fengshan Tropical Horticulture Experimental Station, Chen Wen-Yu cultivated "Fengshan No. 1" seedless watermelon and founded Nongyou Seeds. His uniqueness was not just in the number of varieties, but in defining breeding success as whether farmers could secure a more stable life through better seeds.

In 1925, Chen Wen-Yu was born into a farming family in Yongkang, Tainan. In 1962, he cultivated "Fengshan No. 1" seedless watermelon. In 1968, he co-founded Nongyou Seeds with friends. These three years neatly divide one person's life into three scales: the difficulties seen in the field, the technology solved in the laboratory, and the path a company later took across borders.[^1] [^2]

However, what Chen Wen-Yu truly left behind was not just the title "Watermelon King." What is more worthy of inquiry is: **Why would a breeder prioritize "making farmers earn money" over corporate profit?** Seeds are usually too small to appear in news photos, yet Chen Wen-Yu treated it as a public commitment—the variety had to reach the field and ultimately translate into harvests that farmers could see.

## Starting from the Sighs of the Farmland

![Chen Wen-Yu Cultivating Oriental Melons in the Field](/article-images/people/chen-wen-yu-melon.webp)

_Image: Wikimedia Commons file page describes this image as Chen Wen-Yu cultivating oriental melons; author Uiuc1028, CC BY-SA 3.0. Image not altered by this article._[^11]

In his youth, Chen Wen-Yu helped in the fields and witnessed the helplessness of his parents facing natural disasters and unstable harvests. After graduating from the Newfeng Agricultural College Research Department, he joined the Fengshan Tropical Horticulture Experimental Station, initially focusing on vegetable breeding before shifting his attention to watermelons. According to _Taiwan Guanghua_, he surveyed soil, water, climate, and crop suitability from south to north, observing in the fields by day and compiling reports in the laboratory at night.[^1]

This experience was crucial because Chen Wen-Yu did not conceive of varieties based on "what the market lacked," but rather asked what the land could endure. For him, breeding was not about adding a new color to fruit on paper; it had to simultaneously address pests, climate, transportation, and whether farmers could afford the seeds.

📝 **Curator's Note: Chen Wen-Yu’s starting point was not making Taiwanese agriculture prettier, but reducing the risk for farmers of "planting without knowing if there will be a harvest."**

## Fengshan No. 1: Technology is Not the End Point

![Cross-section photo of seedless watermelon in the public domain](/article-images/people/watermelon-seedless.webp)

_Image: Wikimedia Commons file page explicitly labeled as "Seedless watermelon," with original description from purchasing in Atlanta, USA in 2005; product from Nicaragua; author SCEhardt, public domain. This is an illustrative photo of a seedless watermelon, not Fengshan No. 1, nor a historical photo of Chen Wen-Yu taken in Taiwan._[^17]

In the 1950s, some watermelon seeds used by Taiwanese farmers faced issues with disease resistance and reproduction. Chen Wen-Yu thus dedicated himself to seedless watermelon research. This also explains why "seedless" is not a single laboratory achievement, but an industrial process composed of parent selection, chromosome doubling, hybridization, field pollination, and harvesting segregation. Cucurbit breeding data from North Carolina State University notes that growers typically configure diploid and triploid plants in the field and distinguish between seeded and seedless fruits during harvest and sale.[^13] After about three years of effort, he cultivated "Fengshan No. 1" in 1962. _Taiwan Guanghua_ reported that he used chromosome doubling and crossbreeding to produce sterile seedless watermelons. English reports summarized this achievement as the "world's first seedless watermelon."[^1] [^3]

There is a verifiable discrepancy here that cannot be easily smoothed over: different sources present varying regional and global rankings for the "first." This article adopts the most conservative statement directly verifiable by Chinese reports, calling it **Taiwan's first seedless watermelon, "Fengshan No. 1."** The term "world's first" is only retained when quoting English reports to avoid misrepresenting a media abbreviation as an undisputed scientific historical fact.[^1] [^3]

The story of seedless watermelons should not just be written as "an invention." From a breeding perspective, modern seedless varieties are usually the result of triploid hybridization: mating a tetraploid mother plant (2n=4x=44) with a diploid father plant (2n=2x=22) to produce a triploid (2n=3x=33). The three sets of chromosomes cannot be evenly distributed during meiosis, causing the male and female gametes to lose normal function, thus preventing mature seed formation. The triploid itself cannot provide viable pollen, so the field still requires diploid plants for stimulation. The real turning point occurred in Erlun, Yunlin. Based on land surveys, Chen Wen-Yu determined that the sandy fields under Xila Bridge were suitable for planting this cold-sensitive watermelon, and he drove from Fengshan north to assist farmers through the local agricultural association executive, Wang Liang-xiong.[^1]

The sandy fields later yielded watermelons, which were exported to Hong Kong with assistance from the contemporary agricultural organizations. Farmers only gave him the title "Watermelon King" after realizing the income was better than rice. If this title were placed only at the end of a success story, it would be like a trophy. When put back into those barren sandy fields, it was more like a work certificate: Chen Wen-Yu had to personally attend and help farmers turn technology into harvest.[^1]

## A Company That Learned to Deliver Seeds to the Field First

![Watermelons from a small organic farm in Tainan](/article-images/people/tainan-organic-farm-watermelon.webp)

_Image: Watermelons from a small organic farm in Tainan, Taiwan, taken in November 2009; author Fred Hsu, CC BY-SA 3.0. This is a scene from Taiwanese agriculture, not a historical photo of Chen Wen-Yu at work._[^12]

In 1968, the 43-year-old Chen Wen-Yu left the experimental station and co-founded Nongyou Seeds with friends. Official documents positioned him as a pioneer in breeding vegetable hybrids for Taiwan, recording his publication of horticultural and seed periodicals, the establishment of the Nongyou Social Welfare Foundation, and the founding of a farmers' hospital in Myanmar to provide free medical services.[^4]

Nongyou’s subsequent official award pages described the company's core value as "one seed, one responsibility," listing records such as Chen Wen-Yu receiving the National Third-Class Jingxing Medal, the Asia Pacific Seed Association National Award for Seed Contribution, and a Presidential commendation. These awards prove his institutional recognition but cannot independently verify every industry figure. The numbers still need to be cross-referenced with their original sources.[^5]

The book _Interviews with Chen Wen-Yu and Lin Jun-yi_, published by the National Historical Archives, provided another important clue: the official introduction placed Chen Wen-Yu in the context of Taiwan's agricultural seed breeding moving internationally, specifically mentioning "Diana" yellow-skinned watermelons and the AAS award, as well as Nongyou’s global research bases, farmers' hospitals, and social welfare foundation. This is not a summary that can replace word-for-word interviews in a book, but it proves Chen Wen-Yu's story was not pieced together solely from later legendary articles.[^6]

## The Corporate View of "Making Farmers Earn Money First"

![Photo of ripe watermelons on Wikimedia Commons](/article-images/people/watermelons.webp)

_Image: Ripe watermelon, author Steve Evans, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0; this image is used to represent the watermelon crop itself, not a historical photo of Chen Wen-Yu or a Taiwanese farm._[^14]

_Smile Taiwan_ recorded interviews with Chen Wen-Yu in his later years. He distinguished seeds from general consumables, stating that "the responsibility of one seed" was "providing good income for farmers through developed varieties." In the same report, he also discussed cultivating the yellow-skinned red-fleshed "Diana" watermelon, repeatedly facing green-skinned individuals, yet describing the tenacious spirit with a phrase like, "I just want to be different from others!"[^7]

_Upstream and Downstream_ placed this business philosophy on a larger geographical scale: Nongyou promoted seeds and cultivation techniques in Northern Thailand, China, and India, and established a farmers' hospital in Myanmar. The report also retained the less beautiful parts of agricultural globalization, such as imitation issues in the Chinese market and cross-border operating risks. These difficulties should not be cut because "helping farmers" is not just a pretty corporate slogan; it means bearing costs within unfamiliar systems, transportation, and markets.[^8]

The English _Taipei Times_, in its report on Chen Wen-Yu's passing, quoted him saying: "My goal is to create the world’s most delicious watermelon." The same article also recorded another statement closer to his work ethic: "It takes good seedlings to produce good varieties. We provide farmers with good seeds to help them reap better harvests."[^3]

When these two sentences are viewed together, it is clear that his goals were not contradictory: the most delicious watermelon was the breeder's imagination, but enabling farmers to have a better harvest was the reason this imagination was allowed to exist.

## Seeds Go Out, But They Carry Limitations Too

Chen Wen-Yu's story is often condensed into "Taiwanese seeds going global." Nongyou’s official documents and agricultural industry cases show that the company long invested in research farms, seed stock management, variety development, and international markets across different locations. Agricultural cross-domain integration cases also point out that breeding costs include not just land and labor, but also parent management, isolation breeding, storage, and intellectual property management.[^5] [^9]

These details make "success" more concrete. Seeds are not simply transplanting Taiwanese experience overseas unchanged. They must confront local soil, climate, pests, consumption habits, and farmers' cash flow. Cross-border seed production is therefore both a technical task and a translation task: translating the potential of a variety into a risk that another place is willing to undertake.

The Ministry of Agriculture’s commendation report after Chen Wen-Yu's passing mentioned his contributions to fruit breeding, farmer technical guidance, rural economy, and overseas public welfare, listing his life achievements in national history. This official narrative gave him a national-scale positioning. But returning to the field, the hardest question remains small: can this batch of seeds grow well on this land?[^4]

## Why This Story Is Still Worth Writing

Chen Wen-Yu's deeds have been reported many times; this article does not treat "Watermelon King" as a new discovery, nor does it reassemble all circulating figures. After rereading these articles, the more meaningful angle is to place technology, enterprise, and public welfare on the same line: he first understood the problem in the farmland, then brought research results to the farmers' side, and only then did the company become an international organization.

This line also explains a phenomenon that is easily overlooked: agricultural breeding outcomes often appear under someone else's name at the dinner table. Consumers remember the sweetness, flesh color, and seedlessness of the watermelon, but they do not know how its parents were preserved, how varieties were isolated, or how trials were eliminated. Nongyou’s industry cases mention that seed production must face land isolation, labor input, parent management, and intellectual property protection. Behind a "miraculous variety" is actually a system that is easy to make mistakes with and requires long-term maintenance.[^9]

This also connects Chen Wen-Yu to today's agricultural issues. As the climate becomes more unstable, what farmers need is not just a single "star variety," but seed stock and knowledge that can be adjusted according to different regions, seasons, and markets. The thinking left by Chen Wen-Yu may not be about "finding another Watermelon King," but about acknowledging that every piece of land has its own problems, and the breeder must be willing to spend time looking.[^9]

In his later years, he still treated variety selection as on-site work: slicing, tasting, recording, and then deciding whether it would proceed to the next stage. This action seems slow compared to the fast, large-scale language used when discussing agricultural technology today. But precisely this slowness makes a variety not just a product name, but a commitment that farmers can trust.

## Deconstructing "Success"

Chen Wen-Yu's story contains several types of success that are often grouped together but should actually be separated. The first is breeding success: the ability to cultivate stable varieties under experimental conditions. The second is field success: what farmers can grow in their own soil, climate, and work rhythm. The third is market success: when fruits can be transported and sold, yielding enough profit for farmers to want to plant again. Only the fourth is social success: whether technology and income leave a longer-lasting agricultural capacity.

_Taiwan Guanghua_ wrote about Nongyou's variety selection, where staff must check sugar content, texture, weight, rind hardness, flesh color, and seed condition. If any one of these fails, it may be eliminated. This selection is not turning nature into a controllable machine; it is acknowledging that every variety still requires repeated on-site negotiation.[^1]

Official industry cases further point out that every material in the seed stock library must be differentiated, preserved, and tracked. Management errors can result not only in company losses but also in farmers receiving varieties that do not match what was claimed. Breeders are therefore not just creating new things; they are maintaining a record chain that prevents mistakes from reaching the field.[^9]

This is where Chen Wen-Yu's story best fits today. When we say agriculture needs technology, we cannot only think of faster equipment or larger output; we must also consider who preserved the seed stock, who recorded the parents, and who was willing to try again on a land that was not favored. The value of technology lies not just in whether it can be displayed, but in whether it can be trusted.

## From Fengshan to the World Is Not a Straight Line

Chen Wen-Yu's internationalization did not start with a beautiful map. In the early days, Nongyou first accepted commissioned production from abroad before gradually establishing its own research farms, varieties, and brands. Industry cases mention that the company established research farms in Fengshan, Tainan, Pingtung, and Chiayi, utilizing different soil types and climates for breeding. This dispersion was not decorative expansion but testing the variety under various environments.[^9]

_Taiwan Guanghua_ also wrote about Nongyou's selection grounds having watermelons from Brazil, South Africa, Central Asia, etc. This image better illustrates the essence of breeding work than "exporting to many countries": seeds from all over the world were first brought in for observation, then subjected to crossbreeding, trials, and elimination, before potentially going to a farm in another country. Internationalization is not just sending products out; it also involves bringing external differences back.[^1]

Nongyou's award pages record the company continuously receiving recognition as an excellent exporter from the Ministry of Economic Affairs starting in 1976, and listing the Presidential Agricultural Innovation Award in 2008. These corporate honors and Chen Wen-Yu’s personal medals cannot be mixed, but together they show that breeding, export, organization, and branding had gradually become a single industrial system.[^5]

Public welfare was not just an added layer of warmth to the story. Official Ministry of Agriculture documents state that Nongyou Social Welfare Foundation's aid extended overseas, establishing a farmers' hospital in Myanmar. _Smile Taiwan_ presented Chen Wen-Yu's reaction when reading letters from patients through interview details. For him, if seeds only helped the company gain market share, the story was only half complete. The other half was whether those who previously lacked medical care, nutrition, or stable income truly gained a path.[^4] [^7]

## What He Left for Today Is Not a Miraculous Seed

Chen Wen-Yu passed away on December 7, 2012, at the age of 88. Nongyou Seeds continues to operate based on his philosophy, and Taiwanese agriculture faces new pressures: climate change, seed stock preservation, variety intellectual property, rural population aging, and how farmers can achieve stable income in a high-cost environment.[^3] [^5] [^9]

Therefore, what Chen Wen-Yu is most worth remembering may not be how many varieties of watermelon he cultivated, but that he moved the evaluation standard of breeding one step into the field. Sweetness, color, and appearance are certainly important. But if a variety cannot give farmers more choices on their own land, the technology is incomplete.

He once wrote: "Nongyou, these two characters I like more than rich or noble."[^8] The power of this sentence lies not in making the entrepreneur a saint, but in leaving a verifiable commitment: seeds must ultimately return to people and land—to a place where someone is willing to plant, someone can harvest, and someone can live because of it.

This commitment also has its boundaries. Nongyou's corporate documents present the company's own history and values, while official commendations represent the government's affirmation of Chen Wen-Yu. Neither can replace independent farmer income statistics nor prove that every overseas promotion was equally successful. Therefore, when reading about Chen Wen-Yu, one does not need to deify him as a person who changed all agriculture, nor negate the tangible traces left by breeding, field guidance, and public welfare facilities because of reverence in biographical language.[^4] [^5] [^9]

The gaps between sources must also be preserved. _Taiwan Guanghua_ in 2007 was a Chinese feature article with on-site interviews and photography. The Ministry of Agriculture's commendation is the government's formal tribute to the deceased. _Taipei Times_, however, organized company and public welfare information in English in an obituary. They each answer different questions; one cannot treat the rhetoric of one piece as a statistic from another. This is why this article does not adopt absolute statements like "world's first," which cannot be verified word-for-word from the original text.[^1] [^3] [^4]

A more reliable understanding is: he made a working method last for a long time. First, look at the land; then, conduct breeding. First, let the varieties pass through farmer trials; then, talk about the market. First, have the seeds verified in different environments; then, send them abroad. This method is not romantic and is often slow, but it makes "Taiwanese agriculture going global" more than just a slogan—it becomes a path made up of batches of seeds, research records, field instruction, and farmers' letters.

Next time we slice a seedless watermelon, perhaps we do not need to think of it as a monument to some genius. A reading closer to Chen Wen-Yu is to imagine the tables that were eliminated behind that flesh, the unsuitable pieces of land, and a person who was still willing to bring technology back to the field. Seeds are small. The responsibility they demand is never small. It is precisely because of this that Chen Wen-Yu's story belongs not only to agricultural history but also to those who are willing to do one more verification before handing over a technology to someone else. This patience is the work that is least visible, yet cannot be omitted, before the seed truly crosses borders. It reminds us that truly reliable innovation often endures long waits in the details, and thus deserves to be re-verified by generation after generation of farmers, and repeatedly reminded to us by every piece of land that needs adaptation, and reminds those who come later.

## Seeds Are Not Ancillary Products

![Watermelons growing in a field](/article-images/people/growing-watermelon.webp)

_Image: Watermelons growing in a field; author Mihaib21, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 3.0; this image is used to represent the crop growth stage, not a historical photo of Chen Wen-Yu's work site._[^16]

Chen Wen-Yu’s case also helps us reunderstand the often vague term "agricultural industry chain." Farmers see seedlings, leaves, flowers, and fruits in the field. Seed companies must handle parents, pollination, isolation, purity, germination rate, packaging, and storage even earlier. If any link is inaccurate, what farmers receive may not be the expected variety. Agricultural cross-domain integration cases point out that Nongyou later had to improve the storage and information flow of its seed stock because seed management cannot rely solely on memory and paper.[^9]

This "invisible infrastructure" is also an aspect of Chen Wen-Yu's story that is less discussed. Taiwan often places agricultural achievements in terms of output, exports, or award-winning varieties, but what truly allows a company to breed long-term is the ability to preserve failed parents, record every cross, differentiate different generations, and still find that material years later. For breeding, data is not an administrative burden; it is the starting point for the next success.

## From Fengshan No. 1 to Triploid Technology Today

When Chen Wen-Yu promoted seedless watermelons in the 1960s, he faced the field conditions and market demands of Taiwanese farmers at that time. Modern breeding research further breaks down triploid production into steps such as chromosome doubling, parent cultivation, pollen configuration, and trait selection. NC State University data reminds us that triploid seedless watermelon does not completely lack pollination but requires diploid plants to provide pollen stimulation for fruit formation. This detail prevents consumers from misunderstanding "seedless" as requiring no reproductive process.[^13]

Therefore, Chen Wen-Yu's historical position is not just about having cultivated a famous variety, but also about bringing a highly specialized breeding problem into the production site that farmers could use. From the experimental station to the sandy fields in Erlun, and then to Nongyou’s overseas research bases, this path transformed abstract genetic and cultivation knowledge into a technology that could be taught, replicated, and tested by the market.

The other two are freely licensed photos of Taiwanese farms and general watermelons. They cannot replace interviews or field records, nor can they directly infer that the photo location was a place Chen Wen-Yu worked.
