# Hsu Cho-yun: Ninety-Five Years of History, Written with Two Fingers—and Wang Leehom’s Maternal Granduncle

> **30-second overview:** Born in Xiamen in 1930, Hsu Cho-yun came to Taiwan with the Nationalist government in 1949 and moved to Pittsburgh in 1970. Born with limb disabilities and a lifelong wheelchair user, the historian nevertheless typed nearly forty books with the only two fingers through which he could still exert force. He was elected to the 13th class of Academia Sinica academicians in 1980 and received the sixth Tang Prize in Sinology in 2024—the first laureate, under a strict definition, to have emerged from Taiwan. He donated the entire NT$50 million award to establish the Hsu-Sun Scholarship.[^1] His eldest sister, Hsu Liu-fen, was Wang Leehom’s grandmother, while his second-eldest sister, Hsu Wan-ching, was Li Chien-fu’s mother. One family headed by a Republic of China Navy rear admiral from Wuxi thus connected a University of Chicago Sinology classroom, the chair’s office of National Taiwan University’s Department of History, the words and music of “Descendants of the Dragon,” and the kinship term “maternal granduncle,” searched more than two thousand times without generating a single click.[^2] Hsu died in Pittsburgh on August 3, 2025, at the age of 95. When the Central News Agency interviewed him at 94, he said: “At the moment I draw my last breath, I will still be learning.”[^3]

In June 2024, a Central News Agency reporter flew to Pittsburgh to interview Hsu Cho-yun, who had just learned that he would receive the sixth Tang Prize in Sinology. At 94, he sat in a wheelchair with both hands bent inward. His eyesight had deteriorated to the point that he could no longer turn the pages of a book himself, and he depended almost entirely on his wife, Sun Man-li, for mobility and daily assistance.[^4]

Yet every morning, he still sat down at his computer and used the only two fingers through which he retained enough strength to type, continuing to read and write. The book he was working on was _Jingwei Huaxia_: a history of ancient China exceeding 300,000 Chinese characters, written over three years and revised through eight drafts. In the end, those two fingers typed the entire manuscript.[^5]

When the reporter asked how he felt about receiving the prize, Hsu did not speak about the honor itself. He said: “At 94, I want to submit my answer sheet to society. I hope society will say to me: You have passed. But I cannot tell myself that I have passed. At the moment I draw my last breath, I will still be learning.”[^3]

One year later, on August 3, 2025—the early morning of August 4 in Taiwan—Hsu died in his sleep in Pittsburgh at the age of 95. His final Weibo post, published on July 24, recalled the War of Resistance Against Japan in 1938, the eve of the Battle of Taierzhuang, and the moment Sichuan troops passed through Shashi. The following day, his grandnephew Wang Leehom posted a social-media tribute to his “seventh granduncle.”[^6]

Readers wanted to understand the family relationship between Wang and the historian, but Taiwan.md had never built that bridge. This article expands from their genealogy to the family of a Republic of China Navy rear admiral from Wuxi, the words and music of “Descendants of the Dragon,” the University of Chicago training that brought social-science methods into historiography, and the long river of _The Everlasting River_, which sought to “place China back within the world.” Together, these threads reopen Hsu’s life and work to a new generation of readers.

## Twins on Gulangyu, Xiamen, and Wartime Flight Through Shashi, Hubei, Sichuan, and Chongqing

Hsu Cho-yun and his twin brother, Hsu Yi-yun, were born on September 3, 1930, on Gulangyu in Xiamen, Fujian.[^7] Their ancestral home was Wuxi, Jiangsu. Their father, Hsu Feng-tsao, was a Republic of China Navy rear admiral who had participated in the 1911 Revolution. Their mother, Chang Shun-ying, came from an official family in Wuxi. Educated herself, she was not only a wife and mother but also the spiritual center that held the large family together.[^8]

One twin, the younger brother Hsu Yi-yun, was born able-bodied. The other, the elder brother Hsu Cho-yun, was born with deformities in all four limbs; his hands and feet turned inward, and even limited walking required two crutches. Such a contrast between twins is exceedingly rare in medicine.[^9] For a family in 1930s China, it meant that through decades of relocation, war, and displacement, Hsu Cho-yun would always be the child who had to be carried, supported, and cared for.

In 1935, after their father was reassigned, the family moved from Xiamen to Shashi in Hubei. Two years later, the Marco Polo Bridge Incident erupted, thrusting seven-year-old Hsu into one of the most turbulent periods in modern Chinese history. The family subsequently moved repeatedly as refugees among Shashi, other parts of Hubei, Sichuan, and Chongqing. This uprooted childhood became one of the underlying tonalities of his lifelong historical writing.[^10]

📝 **Hsu’s final Weibo post returned to the eve of the Battle of Taierzhuang:** “I watched these young soldiers walk past me one by one. At that moment, I changed from a child into an adult. I vaguely understood that these young soldiers passing through Shashi were heading straight from Xinyang to Taierzhuang—and that, in the end, not one of them would make it home. Not a single one would return.” Beneath it, he added: “A child becomes an adult not through age, but through a change in state of mind.”[^11] He published the post on July 24, 2025. Ten days later, he died in Pittsburgh. He spent his life writing about ancient China, Han agriculture, and Western Zhou feudalism, yet both the beginning and end of his life remained joined to that riverside road in Hubei in 1938.

Amid war, his limited mobility paradoxically gave Hsu more time to read. He later described himself as “a fortunate man”: “I met good teachers when I studied and a good wife when I married. Even my congenital disability benefited me—it gave me more time at my desk.”[^12]

In 1949, the Hsu family came to Taiwan with the retreating Nationalist government, and nineteen-year-old Hsu entered the Department of History at National Taiwan University. Intellectuals of that generation carried their scholarship across the Taiwan Strait from China, reestablishing in Taipei academic traditions that had taken shape in Beiping and Shanghai. In this environment, Hsu built the Sinological foundation that would allow him to engage Western scholarship.[^13]

## Under Herrlee G. Creel at the University of Chicago: Bringing the Social Sciences into Chinese History

Hsu graduated from NTU’s Department of History in 1953 and earned a master’s degree from its Graduate Institute of Liberal Arts in 1956. That year, he traveled to the United States for advanced study at the University of Chicago, lived at the Chicago Theological Seminary, and studied under Sinologist Herrlee G. Creel.[^14]

American scholarship on China was then undergoing a methodological transformation. Traditional Sinology had centered on textual interpretation, philology, and evidentiary scholarship. “China Studies,” by contrast, was beginning to introduce tools from the social sciences—including sociology, anthropology, economics, and political science—and to treat China as an object of analysis within modern academic disciplines. Hsu received his doctorate in 1962, precisely at this turning point.[^15]

His dissertation was subsequently revised as _Ancient China in Transition: An Analysis of Social Mobility, 722–222 B.C._, published by Stanford University Press in 1965. The book used quantitative methods to analyze social mobility during the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods: which aristocratic families disappeared from the political stage, which rising classes advanced through military achievement or learning, and how the social structure loosened on the eve of Qin-Han unification. After reading it, Harvard Sinologist John K. Fairbank called it “a little classic.”[^16]

📝 **The book also planted a methodological seed in Taiwan’s historical profession.** Hsu was 32 when he returned to Taiwan in 1962. He first served at Academia Sinica’s Institute of History and Philology, where he had held a concurrent assistant research fellowship since 1956 and was promoted to associate research fellow in 1962. In 1964, he simultaneously became a professor and chair of NTU’s Department of History, serving through 1970.[^17] During those six years, he did something that would profoundly reshape historical scholarship in Taiwan: **he brought social-science methods, schools of thought, and research questions into Taiwan’s history classrooms.** As department chair, he organized workshops and international conferences, introduced methodological debates from American historiography at NTU, and trained a cohort of students who would later occupy important positions in historical scholarship across Taiwan, China, and Hong Kong.[^18]

Three representative works that he subsequently completed—_Han Agriculture: The Formation of the Early Chinese Peasant Economy_ (University of Washington Press, 1980),[^19] _Seeking Antiquity_ (Linking Publishing, 1982),[^20] and _The History of the Western Zhou_ (Linking Publishing, 1984)[^21]—can be understood as three applications of this methodology:

| Work                              | Period addressed                  | Methodological instrument                                                                                                                      |
| --------------------------------- | --------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| _Han Agriculture_                 | Han dynasty                       | A quantitative reconstruction of the mutually reinforcing relationship among peasant life, market-economic networks, and intensive agriculture |
| _Seeking Antiquity_               | Antiquity through the Qin and Han | The evolution of a civil bureaucracy linking the paired domains of center and locality, politics and society                                   |
| _The History of the Western Zhou_ | Western Zhou                      | The role of lineage groups in constructing the origins of a “Huaxia” consciousness                                                             |

Hsu described the questions addressed by these three books as the “three primary colors of Chinese culture”: not three unrelated studies, but three cross-sections of the same fundamental questions pursued continuously by one historian over thirty years.[^22]

## Half a Century in Pittsburgh: A Macrohistorical Method That Rejected Sinocentrism

In 1970, at the age of forty, Hsu immigrated to the United States with his wife Sun Man-li and their eight-month-old only child, Leo Hsu, joining the University of Pittsburgh’s Department of History.[^23]

He spent twenty-eight years at Pittsburgh from 1970 to 1998, advancing from visiting professor (1970–72) to tenured professor (1972–82) and university professor (1982–98). After retiring in 1998, he remained university professor emeritus until his death. The University of Pittsburgh retained his title for a total of fifty-five years.[^24] At the same time, he never left Taiwan’s academic community. He served as a corresponding research fellow at Academia Sinica’s Institute of History and Philology from 1981 to 1997 and, after 1989, held a succession of distinguished research appointments through 2006. Academia Sinica in Taipei, the Pittsburgh campus, and lecture circuits across Taiwan, China, and Hong Kong formed the three coordinates of the latter half of his academic career.[^25]

In Pittsburgh, he undertook two structural projects. The first was to continue deepening the methodology of applying the social sciences to Chinese history. The second was to deploy that method at a larger scale: not merely to examine one dynasty or China alone, but to compare China within the history of world civilizations.[^26]

📝 **The term “macrohistory” later became most closely associated with Ray Huang, but Hsu’s macrohistory was of a different kind.** Huang asked why China had been unable to move beyond traditional modes of governance, as in _1587, A Year of No Significance_ and _China: A Macro History_. Hsu asked how Chinese civilization was itself shaped through contact with other civilizations—the steppe, India, Islam, and the West—while also shaping them in turn. His central conviction was a rejection of Sinocentrism: understanding China required placing it back within the context of world history, not treating it as a self-contained, isolated, closed system evolving solely according to its own internal logic.[^28]

In 1989, Hsu helped establish the Chiang Ching-kuo Foundation for International Scholarly Exchange. Its creation followed a 1986 joint petition to President Chiang Ching-kuo from Hsu, Yu Ying-shih, Kwang-chih Chang, and other professors of Chinese heritage teaching in North America. Concerned about the decline of overseas Sinological research, they proposed that Taiwan fund an international academic grant-making institution to advance the field.[^29] After the foundation was established on January 12, 1989, Hsu served as chair of its American Regional Committee, a position he retained until shortly before his death—a tenure of thirty-six years.[^30]

Among the students Hsu trained during his Pittsburgh years were Wang Fan-sen, who later directed Academia Sinica’s Institute of History and Philology and served as the academy’s vice president, and Tu Cheng-sheng, who served as minister of education and director of the National Palace Museum.[^31] Together with historians of his generation such as Hsing I-tien and Chen Jo-shui, with whom he collaborated over many years, “Hsu Cho-yun’s student circle” formed a highly influential network in the historical profession across Taiwan, China, and Hong Kong from the 1980s through the 2010s.

## The Family Tree: From Hsu Feng-tsao to Wang Leehom, Three Generations of Sinophone Cultural Elites

Hsu Cho-yun was the seventh of nine siblings. This was why Wang Leehom called him his “seventh granduncle” in his tribute.[^32]

Laid out in full, the family history forms an overlooked map of Taiwan’s postwar culture:

| Birth order           | Name            | Principal identity                                                                                                                                                                                                                     | Link to the next generation                                                                                                                            |
| --------------------- | --------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Eldest sister         | Hsu Liu-fen     | Economics graduate of Tsing Hua University; chair of the Department of Accounting and Statistics at Taipei College of Commerce; author of _Principles of Accounting_ and an English-Chinese/Chinese-English accounting dictionary[^33] | → Married Wang Hsin-ming → son Wang Ta-chung, a neurologist → grandson **Wang Leehom**[^34]                                                            |
| Second-eldest sister  | Hsu Wan-ching   | Graduate of National Southwestern Associated University                                                                                                                                                                                | → Married Li Mo, a former deputy minister of education and economic affairs → son **Li Chien-fu**, original singer of “Descendants of the Dragon”[^35] |
| Seventh, elder twin   | **Hsu Cho-yun** | Academician of Academia Sinica and historian                                                                                                                                                                                           | Subject of this article                                                                                                                                |
| Seventh, younger twin | Hsu Yi-yun      | Chemical-engineering scholar and former chair of the Atomic Energy Council, Executive Yuan[^36]                                                                                                                                        | —                                                                                                                                                      |
| Others                | —               | —                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      | —                                                                                                                                                      |

In other words, Hsu played two roles within the family: he was Wang Leehom’s “seventh maternal granduncle” and Li Chien-fu’s maternal uncle. Wang and Li are cousins: Hsu Liu-fen was Wang’s grandmother, Hsu Wan-ching was Li’s mother, and the two women were sisters.

The Hsu family came from an official lineage in Wuxi. Their father, Hsu Feng-tsao, was a Republic of China Navy rear admiral, while their mother, Chang Shun-ying, came from a scholarly family. In the next generation in Taiwan, their descendants spread across accounting, the offices of deputy ministers, academia, the popular-music industry, and nuclear policy. By the third generation, the family connected to the words and music of the 1980 song “Descendants of the Dragon”—written and composed by Hou Dejian and first performed by Li Chien-fu, the son of Hsu Cho-yun’s sister Hsu Wan-ching—and to Wang Leehom, a defining figure in 2000s Sinophone popular music. These were two branches of the same Wuxi family.[^37]

📝 **This family line merits serious treatment for reasons beyond celebrity gossip.** It precisely illustrates how a second-generation _waishengren_ intellectual family that came to Taiwan with the Nationalist government in 1949 dispersed its cultural capital, within three generations, across several of postwar Taiwan’s most important fields: scholarship through Hsu Cho-yun, education policy through Li Mo, the accounting profession through Hsu Liu-fen, nuclear technology through Hsu Yi-yun, and popular music through Wang Leehom and Li Chien-fu. Roots in China, growth in Taiwan, academic training in the United States, and circulation throughout the Sinophone world: one family, five outlets.

On August 5, 2025, Wang Leehom posted a social-media tribute to his “seventh granduncle,” recounting the childhood of his father, Wang Ta-chung, a neurologist and graduate of NTU’s medical school:[^38]

“My father’s warmest childhood memories included the bedtime stories his seventh uncle read to him. His seventh uncle, Hsu Cho-yun, had been frail and sickly since childhood and was born with disabilities, but his inner world was extraordinarily rich and his wisdom was beyond compare.”

“Although he was the seventh of nine siblings and his body was impaired, what he left the world was the towering figure of a great man seen from behind.”

“He may have been small and thin in stature, but his spirit stood as tall as a mountain. Everything he left behind will never be forgotten. Mr. Hsu Cho-yun, may you rest in peace. Thank you, Seventh Granduncle. May God bless you and grant you rest.”

The detail about bedtime stories was later amplified by numerous media outlets. Every evening, Hsu would invent another installment for his young nephew, Wang Ta-chung. Many were improvised and concealed lessons about life.[^39] An uncle unable to run or jump with his own legs held his young nephew through stories. Decades later, that nephew’s son spoke to global Sinophone audiences through popular music. From the generation of Republic of China Navy rear admiral Hsu Feng-tsao to that of Golden Melody Award-winning singer Wang Leehom, one man stood between them, writing Chinese history with two fingers. The line itself is a microcosm of twentieth-century Chinese diaspora history.

## _The Everlasting River_: Writing China as a Great River for an Age of Ordinary Readers

In 2006, Hansheng Publishing in Taipei released _The Everlasting River: Transformations and Developments in Chinese History and Culture_.[^40]

The 270,000-character book was Hsu’s history of China for an “age of ordinary people.” In his preface, he wrote: “Readers today differ from those of the past. In this age of ordinary people, nearly everyone with at least a high-school education may take an interest in history. This book is a history written for this generation of Chinese readers.”[^41]

📝 **Making scholarship accessible to ordinary people is a higher demand, not a lowering of standards.** Writing for high-school graduates while still earning the approval of university professors is a rare ability in any language. The strategy of _The Everlasting River_ was to abandon a linear chronology organized around dynastic succession and instead make the “expansion and contraction of culture” its central axis. Chinese civilization appears as a great river, growing from a trickle into a torrent, absorbing tributaries along the way while also changing course, flooding, and running dry.

Hsu began with the questions ordinary readers actually ask. What did common people eat in medieval China? How did the cultures of the Yangtze and Yellow River regions differ? Why did Taiwan not appear in Chinese history until the modern period? What did Confucius mean by _ren_, or humaneness? Why did China not undergo Western-style industrialization? Each question could have become an accessible scholarly essay in its own right, but Hsu wove them together into a single book.[^42]

After publication, _The Everlasting River_ circulated widely in Taiwan, Hong Kong, China, and overseas Chinese communities, selling in the millions over the following two decades. Simplified-character editions were successively introduced by SDX Joint Publishing and Guangxi Normal University Press in China.[^43] It was a popular work, but one accepted by scholars as well: a history book that professors and high-school graduates could both read was a rare achievement in the twenty-first-century Chinese-language world.

Hsu then used the same civilizational macrohistory framework to write three companion volumes:

**_Self and Other: The Boundaries Between the Internal and External in Chinese History_ (2009, China Times Publishing / Chinese University Press)**[^44]: This book directly addressed the historical question of how China defined itself and others. Hsu proposed six systems for analyzing the formation and dissolution of relations between “self” and “other”: interactions between China and other nations; between the Chinese heartland and peripheral peoples; between the central state and local society; between upper and lower social strata; within networks of market exchange; and between mainstream “orthodoxy” and cultural challengers labeled “heterodox.” His language was measured: “In the ‘internal/external’ and ‘self/other’ relations of complex systems, the most important distinction lies not in ethnicity, but in cultural cognition.”[^45] Against the intensely contested political identities of Taiwan, China, and Hong Kong in 2009, this was a deliberate effort to create historical distance.

**_Discourses on Huaxia: The Transformation of a Complex Community_ (Guangxi Normal University Press, 2015)**[^46]: This book advanced the argument one step further. Hsu maintained that China should not be defined through a “freeze-frame”—what it looked like under one dynasty at one point in time—but understood as a “process”: how China changed. He analyzed this process as the dynamic interweaving of four variables: political authority, economy, society, and cultural ideas.

**_Jingwei Huaxia_ (CITIC, 2023; expanded Hong Kong edition from Joint Publishing, 2024)**[^47]: This was the last major book Hsu completed, at age 93, by typing with two fingers and revising the manuscript eight times. When Xu Zhiyuan interviewed him in Pittsburgh in 2019 and returned in 2023, the book was still being written. The expanded traditional-character edition published in 2024 added two long essays totaling approximately 20,000 Chinese characters, on “the Great Flood” and “interactions between the northern steppe and the Central Plain.”[^48]

📝 **Together, these four books constitute one historian’s comprehensive examination of the concept of “China.”** They move from the river metaphor of _The Everlasting River_, through the internal and external boundaries of _Self and Other_ and the process-based definition in _Discourses on Huaxia_, to _Jingwei Huaxia_ and its expanded geographical vision from continent to ocean. In his later years, Hsu explicitly recast “Chinese history” as “the history of China’s relationship with the world.” This was also the assessment offered by the 2024 Tang Prize selection committee: “Professor Hsu’s historiography reveals the broadest patterns. His specialized studies of ancient China uncover essential questions in long-term history, while his interpretations of general history emphasize cultural accommodation and exchange and seek China’s place in the world.”[^49]

## A Guide to the Works: Forty Books, Five Approaches

Hsu wrote nearly forty books over his lifetime, covering ancient history, Han society, Western Zhou feudalism, overseas Sinology, questions of Chinese identity, observations of contemporary America, and late-life reflections on finding inner stability. First-time readers need not begin with his dense academic monographs. The following guide suggests five approaches, arranged by reading sequence:

**1. An introduction: Chinese macrohistory for ordinary readers**

- **_The Everlasting River: Transformations and Developments in Chinese History and Culture_ (Hansheng, 2006)**[^40]: His most widely circulated book. Its 270,000 Chinese characters present a cultural history of China without dynastic boundaries, viewed through the expansion and contraction of civilization and the metaphor of a great river.

**2. Advanced reading: civilization’s internal and external boundaries**

- **_Self and Other: The Boundaries Between the Internal and External in Chinese History_ (China Times Publishing, 2009)**[^44]: An examination of Chinese identity through a six-system framework.
- **_Discourses on Huaxia: The Transformation of a Complex Community_ (Guangxi Normal University Press, 2015)**[^46]: A process-based definition of “China” through the interweaving of political authority, economy, society, and culture.
- **_Jingwei Huaxia_ (expanded Hong Kong edition, Joint Publishing, 2024)**[^47]: Revised eight times, with an expanded framework “from continent to ocean.”

**3. Representative academic works: bringing the social sciences into Chinese history**

- **_The History of the Western Zhou_ (Linking Publishing, 1984; second edition, 1986; revised third editions, 1990/1993; expanded Beijing edition from SDX Joint Publishing, 1994; new expanded edition, 2020)**[^21]: A study of how lineage groups constructed the origins of “Huaxia” consciousness.
- **_Han Agriculture_ (English edition, University of Washington Press, 1980; later translated into Chinese as _Han Agriculture: The Formation of the Early Chinese Agricultural Economy_)**[^19]: A quantitative reconstruction of Han peasant life and networks of market exchange.
- **_Ancient China in Transition_ (Stanford University Press, 1965)**[^16]: A revision of his dissertation and a quantitative analysis of social mobility during the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods, praised by John K. Fairbank as “a little classic.”
- **_Seeking Antiquity_ (Linking Publishing, 1982)**[^20]: The evolution of the central and local civil bureaucracy from antiquity through the Qin and Han dynasties.

**4. Autobiography and late-life thought**

- **_The Course of the Heart_ (Wenxing, 1964; Biography Literature, 1979; Xiamen University Press, 2015)**[^50]: An autobiography written at age 34, tracing his intellectual journey from Xiamen to Taipei and Chicago.
- **_Sixty Years of Change in America: Observations of a Chinese_ (Linking Publishing, 2019)**[^51]: His account of half a century in the United States and diagnosis of American society.
- **_Turn Inward and Settle Yourself_ (Beijing Daily Press, 2022, written with Feng Junwen)**[^52]: Reflections composed at age 89, offering a book of inner grounding for a restless age.

**5. Popular works and interviews**

- **The five-volume _Hsu Cho-yun Talks About History_ series**[^53]: A popular presentation of his macrohistorical approach.
- **_Ten Days of Conversations with Hsu Cho-yun: The Contemporary World Order and Humanity’s Future_ (Hong Kong Joint Publishing)**[^54]: A collection of his late-life reflections on the global order.

📝 **If you can read only one book, begin with _The Everlasting River_.** To understand his historical method, the preface to the expanded edition of _The History of the Western Zhou_ is more powerful than the book itself. For his overall view of China, _Self and Other_ is the most concentrated. To understand Hsu as a person, read _The Course of the Heart_ together with _Turn Inward and Settle Yourself_.

## The 2024 Tang Prize: Donating the Entire NT$50 Million Award to Establish the Hsu-Sun Scholarship

On June 20, 2024, the Tang Prize Foundation announced the recipient of the sixth Tang Prize in Sinology: Hsu Cho-yun. The award totaled NT$50 million, including a NT$10 million research grant.[^55]

Under a strict definition, he was the first Tang Prize in Sinology laureate to have emerged from Taiwan. The first five laureates—Yu Ying-shih of Harvard, Stephen Owen of Harvard, Naoki Hazama of Kyoto, Wang Gungwu of Singapore, and Erik Zürcher of Leiden—had all completed their secondary education and foundational university training overseas. Hsu, by contrast, came to Taiwan with the Nationalist government in 1949, graduated from NTU’s Department of History in 1953, and earned a master’s degree from NTU’s Graduate Institute of Liberal Arts in 1956. He was a scholar who had genuinely “grown up in Taiwan.”[^56]

⚠️ **The Tang Prize selection committee’s citation directly identified the core of Hsu’s historical work:** “Professor Hsu’s historiography reveals the broadest patterns. His specialized studies of ancient China uncover essential questions in long-term history, while his interpretations of general history emphasize cultural accommodation and exchange and seek China’s place in the world. His writings convey concern for country, nation, and humanity as a whole. He is a broadly learned historian who combines erudition with a commitment to improving the world.”[^49]

The action that received even wider coverage, however, was what Hsu did next. He donated the entire NT$50 million award to establish the Hsu-Sun Scholarship, supporting young doctoral researchers in Sinology around the world.[^1] The scholarship was jointly named for Hsu and his wife, Sun Man-li, recognizing the support she had provided throughout fifty-five years of his academic career.

📝 **The significance of this act extended beyond generosity.** Hsu had helped establish the Chiang Ching-kuo Foundation in 1989 and spent decades overseeing grants for Sinological research in North America. Cultivating the next generation of Sinologists was one of his lifelong projects.[^29] Donating the entire Tang Prize transformed the question of “what his generation had accomplished” directly into “whether the next generation would have the opportunity to continue.” For a 94-year-old historian with failing eyesight who depended entirely on others for mobility, it was the most concrete act of intellectual succession available to him.

Tang Prize Week took place in Taipei in late September 2024. Unable to attend for health reasons, Hsu delivered his laureate lecture, “Finding a Path Through a World in Confusion,” by video. That same month, _Thirteen Invitations_ assembled Xu Zhiyuan’s two interviews with Hsu in Pittsburgh, beginning in 2019, into the first episode of its eighth season. The subject Hsu discussed most was his unease about the contemporary world. He saw the fractures in American society, confusion in China, and anxiety in Taiwan. Describing the current international order, he said that he “could not help thinking of the late Qing.”[^57]

## A Cross-Strait Academic Bridge: Maintaining Distance Beyond Sinocentrism

One of Hsu’s most frequently cited positions in his later years was his criticism of Sinocentrism.[^58]

“I hope I will no longer hear rhetoric about how ‘awesome China is,’” he said.[^59] This position made him “not Chinese enough” in the eyes of Chinese nationalists and “too Chinese” to some historians centered on Taiwan. Yet this was precisely the position he sought: instead of choosing a side within any nationalist narrative, he used the longer timescale of “China’s relationship with the world” to examine contemporary Cross-Strait political anxiety.[^60]

⚠️ **A remark from his September 2024 interview with the Central News Agency was widely quoted:** “Trump’s talk of ‘protection money’ resembles the behavior of thugs in the foreign concessions.”[^61] He made the comment around the time of his sixth Tang Prize laureate lecture. Hsu compared the contemporary American posture toward its allies to that of foreign powers in China’s treaty-port concessions during the late Qing. The analogy was itself an application of his macrohistorical method: viewed on the scale of a great river, present anxiety about the international order has historical points of comparison.

Hsu always retained a distinctive emotional attachment to Taiwan. In his 2024 Central News Agency interview, he told a Taiwanese reporter:[^3]

“Taiwan fills me with longing and regret. I have always hoped that one day the waters of the Taiwan Strait will be calm, that brothers on both sides will shake hands, and that neither side will remain hostile or divided from the other.”

“I have no regrets. I should fulfill this responsibility for Taiwan.”

Before his death, he had purchased a burial plot in Wuxi and arranged for his gravestone to be carved in advance, hoping to be interred with his parents. “Fallen leaves returning to their roots” was his chosen conclusion for his own life.[^62] On the gravestone, he quoted Lu You’s poem “To My Son”: “I grieve only that I shall not see the Nine Provinces united.” Commentators on both sides of the Taiwan Strait read different meanings into the line. Some interpreted it as regret over the absence of Chinese unification. Others saw a deeper sorrow: that no political narrative could ever contain the displacement of his entire life.

📝 **It was an extraordinarily difficult position to occupy.** Hsu attended university in Taiwan, earned his doctorate in the United States, spent half a century in Pittsburgh, reached a million readers in China through _The Everlasting River_, and held the status of an Academia Sinica academician in Taiwan. Each identity placed expectations on him, yet none could contain him completely. In the end, he chose the words “I should fulfill this responsibility for Taiwan.” He defined that responsibility himself, across the levels of political position and national identity: to use a historian’s long timescale to offer people in Taiwan, China, and Hong Kong a common temporal measure through which to understand one another.

Hsu repeatedly said: “History tells us that whenever a civilization is at its strongest, it is also nurturing the crisis within it. Conversely, whenever it is at its weakest, it is often burying the seeds of renewal.”[^63] He spent his life writing Chinese history, but what truly concerned him was the act of looking between the upper and lower reaches of the river. Dynastic strength and decline were only the surface. What a 95-year-old historian typed with two fingers was a temporal sedative for a restless age.

## Death at 95, Leaving a River for Future Readers

On August 3, 2025, Hsu died in his sleep in Pittsburgh, United States, at the age of 95. His wife, Sun Man-li, was at his side.[^64]

In its press release, Academia Sinica offered this assessment of its 13th-class academician: “Academician Hsu Cho-yun mastered both Chinese and Western learning and was a world-renowned leader in historical scholarship. He specialized in ancient Chinese, social, and cultural history, producing abundant and profoundly original work. His vision was expansive: he examined ancient Chinese history through the lens of world history without allowing China to delimit his inquiry.”[^25] The PRC’s Taiwan Affairs Office took the unusual step of publicly describing his death as “regrettable.” This simultaneous mourning on both sides of the Taiwan Strait was itself the final footnote to the distinctive position Hsu had occupied.[^65]

On August 5, Wang Leehom posted a tribute to his “seventh granduncle.” That same day, Li Chien-fu commemorated his “uncle.” The original singer of “Descendants of the Dragon” and the singer-songwriter of “Because Love Is in the Heart”—descendants of the same Hsu family home in Wuxi—said farewell across three generations to the boy who had once walked with two crutches.[^66]

In early August, the University of Pittsburgh’s Department of History published a brief “In Memoriam” titled “Renowned Historian Passes Away at 95.” The university where Hsu had spent fifty-five years sent him off with that concise headline.[^67]

Yeh Kuang-shih, a former minister of transportation and a collaborator at the Chiang Ching-kuo Foundation, recalled his final meeting with Hsu: “Hsu Cho-yun was not only a great scholar; his conduct and way of dealing with people were also extraordinary. He was an essential driving force behind the establishment of the Chiang Ching-kuo Foundation.”[^68]

Hsu left nearly forty books. His earliest, _The Course of the Heart_, appeared in 1964, when he was 34. The expanded edition of his last, _Jingwei Huaxia_, appeared in 2024, when he was 94. Across sixty years, he followed the same great river of Chinese history, first with a pen and later with two fingers.

✦ In 1938, Sichuan troops passed before him on Linjiang Road in Shashi, Hubei, on their way to Taierzhuang.  
In 1949, the flag of the Nationalist government flew in the wind above the deck at Keelung Harbor.  
In 1962, in a University of Chicago library, he first applied social-science methods to the Spring and Autumn and Warring States periods.  
In 1980, in an Academia Sinica conference hall, he was elected to its 13th class of academicians.  
In 2006, the first printing of _The Everlasting River_ appeared in the window of a Hansheng bookstore.  
In 2024, at his desk at home in Pittsburgh, he typed the eighth draft of _Jingwei Huaxia_ with two fingers.  
On July 24, 2025, he wrote on Weibo: “A child becomes an adult not through age, but through a change in state of mind.”  
On August 3, 2025, he drew his final breath in his sleep.

“At the moment I draw my last breath, I will still be learning.” He said it in an interview at 94. One year later, it came true.

What he left was a river that continues to flow, without a final answer.
