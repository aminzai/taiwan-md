> **30‑second overview:** Chou Tien-chen, born 1990 in Taipei, is Taiwan's top men’s singles badminton player. He won the 2014 French Open, becoming the first Taiwanese player to capture a BWF Super Series men’s singles title.[^2] In August 2019 his world ranking rose to No. 2, the highest ever for a Taiwanese men’s singles player.[^1] He is the first Taiwanese player to defeat “the world’s king of badminton” Lin Dan on the international stage.[^13] He represented Chinese Taipei at three consecutive Olympic Games, reaching the quarterfinals each time.[^2] In early 2023, a routine colonoscopy revealed early‑stage colon cancer; he underwent surgery and, within days, flew abroad to compete, enduring a nearly year‑long slump.[^3] The following year, at age 34, he reached the quarterfinals at the Paris Olympics.[^10] At 36, he remains one of the oldest regulars in the world’s top tier.[^12]

![Chou Tien-chen lunging to save a shot at the 2018 Chinese Taipei Open, a classic moment of his pull‑and‑hang fighting style](/article-images/people/chou-tien-chen-taipei-open-2018.webp)

## A Flashy Child

Chou Tien-chen’s badminton journey began with a simple wish from his parents: to give their energetic son an outlet for running, jumping, and “releasing electricity.”[^1] After his interest emerged, he transferred in third grade to Shuanglian Elementary School, which had a dedicated badminton team.[^1]

His first coach, Chen Wen‑ping, did not give him a flattering assessment—his technique was “too flashy” and his footwork lacked power.[^1] His temperament was also problematic: competitive, self‑important, frequently clashing with teammates, and not very coachable.[^1] At that time, no one could foresee that this show‑off, restless boy would one day become world No. 2.

The turning point came in sixth grade, when he won his first national title and met the coach who would reshape his life: Indonesian‑born Zheng Yung‑cheng, a former world No. 4 player.[^5] From Shuanglian Junior High to Keelung High School, Zheng guided him, tempering his fiery spirit into patience.[^5] Coincidentally, Zheng also served as one of the early coaches of Taiwan’s badminton queen Tai Tzu‑ying, linking the “big brother–big sister” of Taiwanese badminton to the same Indonesian mentor.[^1] Chou later said he is most grateful to his coach for grinding away his childhood impulsiveness.[^1]

## Ten Straight Losses, Then World No. 2

In 2009, at age 19, Chou joined the CTBC Badminton Team and entered the national‑team system, officially beginning his professional career.[^1] The start was far from sweet; Taiwanese men’s singles had long been a supporting act on the world stage, and no one had paved a path for him, so he had to forge his own, match by match.

The most humiliating stretch came in the first half of 2014. Facing top‑level opponents, he suffered ten consecutive defeats, unable to break through the barrier until the Canada Open in July halted the losing streak.[^6] He later reflected that his technique was still immature and his on‑court thinking was lacking; he often simply returned the shuttle without strategic intent.[^6] He set a personal rule: after each loss, he must extract at least one point of improvement.[^6]

His comeback was swift. In October 2014, at the French Open, he became the first Taiwanese player to win a BWF Super Series men’s singles title.[^2] In 2016, he ended a 17‑year wait by capturing the Chinese Taipei Open title, the first home‑grown champion in years.[^2] At the 2018 Jakarta Asian Games, he earned a men’s singles silver medal.[^2]

2019 was the pinnacle. At the Indonesia Open final, he defeated Denmark’s Anders Antonsen to claim his first Super 1000 title.[^1] In August that year his world ranking rose to No. 2—the highest ever for a Taiwanese men’s singles player.[^1] The following year at the All‑England Open, reigning king Kento Momota withdrew after a traffic accident; Chou entered as the top seed but fell to Denmark’s Viktor Axelsen in the final, taking silver.[^1] At the 2022 World Championships he added a bronze medal.[^2] While still with CTBC, he helped Chinese Taipei win the men’s team bronze at the 2024 Thomas Cup.[^1]

## A Style Called “Grinding”

Chou is not a player who simply smash his opponents out. His signature is a pull‑and‑hang, rally‑intensive style: he patiently searches for seams in multi‑shuttle exchanges, displays delicate net touch, and finishes points with decisive attacking shots.[^1] Early in his career he relied on stamina to wear down opponents; later he shifted to a defense‑first foundation, waiting for the right moment to strike, with timing that is hard to read.[^16]

![Chou Tien-chen lowering his centre of gravity and moving with precision during a rally at the 2022 Taipei Open](/article-images/people/chou-tien-chen-taipei-open-2022.webp)

This “grinding” style earned him a tongue‑in‑cheek nickname: “the Shadow King.” On court he often appears breathless, as if his stamina is about to give out; he may lose a game before rallying back to win the decider. Chinese fans therefore dubbed him the “Shadow King.” Chou clarified that the nickname reflects his genuine fatigue, not a performance for an audience.[^14]

It was this resilience that, in 2015 at the Chinese Taipei Open, made him the first Taiwanese player to defeat “the world’s king of badminton” Lin Dan on an international stage.[^13] In his era, the men’s singles field was dominated by legends Lin Dan, Lee Zhong‑wei, Chen Long, and Kento Momota; Chou often pushed matches to a third game, losing only by the narrowest margins.[^16] Lacking any overwhelming natural talent, he relied on consistency and an unwillingness to quit to grind his way into the world’s top five.

## Appendicitis, Church, and a Slow‑Maturing Personality

During a slump in 2012, Chou was invited by his physiotherapist to attend church.[^15] The real turning point came the following year: before the 2013 India Open, he suffered severe abdominal pain, fought through the match, and was later diagnosed with perforated appendicitis that had been present for days.[^1] After surgery, during rehabilitation, he was baptized and became a Christian.[^1]

Faith subsequently became part of his routine. He often visits local churches during travel breaks, and sings hymns before matches or during recovery periods.[^1] Chou says faith accelerated his mental maturity, transforming the once‑combative child into someone who can stay calm under pressure.[^15] While his on‑court effort appears strenuous, off‑court he relaxes, joking with teammates and showing a lighter side.[^1]

## Coaching Himself

Starting in 2019, Chou made a rare decision among elite athletes: he stopped hiring a full‑time coach and became his own coach, arranging his own training and devising tactics independently on court.[^18] Even at the Olympics, he sometimes competed without a coach, pursuing a “non‑conventional” preparation path.[^19] He sums up his reliance on a higher power with a single sentence: “God is my coach.”[^18]

This arrangement demands extreme self‑discipline. During the COVID‑19 pandemic, he lived for an extended period at the Kaohsiung National Sports Training Center, isolating in a “bubble” to focus on preparation; he says the isolation actually helped his concentration.[^19] Commentators consistently describe him with the same set of words: hard‑working, disciplined, humble. In a sport that often glorifies raw talent, he has turned “doing the daily work” into his greatest weapon.

## A Routine Checkup Reveals Colon Cancer

In early 2023, while on a competition break back in Taiwan, Chou scheduled a routine health check. The colonoscopy revealed early‑stage colon cancer.[^3]

He promptly underwent surgery to remove the tumor. To ensure clean margins, the operation was especially deep, leaving an absorbable clip inside his intestine to hold the wound closed.[^3] Fortunately, the cancer had not spread. He barely paused his career: a few days after surgery he flew abroad to compete, continued training, though his diet was heavily affected and his body took a long time to recover.[^3] Over the next year he repeatedly fell in the early rounds, failing to reach the quarterfinals for seven consecutive months, prompting public speculation about whether a 34‑year‑old was “too old.” He knew the issue lay in the serious illness, not his age.[^3]

He chose not to disclose the diagnosis publicly until November 2023, when he won the German Heil‑Oskar Open and first shared his story in church.[^3] In February 2024 he captured the Thailand Masters title at age 34 and 27 days, making him the fourth oldest men’s singles champion in World Tour history after Lee Hwan‑hee, Lee Zhong‑wei, and Lin Dan.[^11] On the podium he revealed his cancer battle: “I made it through.” He said the experience cemented his belief that, no matter the setback, perseverance always leads to an answer.[^3]

## Paris: Quarterfinals Against the Wind

The Olympics remain an unfinished chapter for Chou. At Rio 2016 he reached the quarterfinals but lost 0‑2 to Malaysia’s Lee Zhong‑wei.[^1] At Tokyo 2021 he again reached the quarterfinals, losing a close three‑game match to China’s Chen Long, 14‑21 in the decider.[^1] In the summer of 2024, he made his third Olympic appearance.[^2] Fresh from cancer surgery, he roared in the group stage, energising the Chinese Taipei team to a perfect record and advancing to the round of 16.[^10] In the first knockout round he faced Japan’s top player Kenta Narahoka, winning convincingly 21‑12, 21‑16 to reach the quarterfinals, matching his personal Olympic best.[^2] There he met India’s Lakshya Sen; after a grueling 75‑minute battle he fell short, his Olympic medal dream slipping away once more.[^2]

Reaching the quarterfinals in three consecutive Olympics places him among only four players in badminton history.[^20] Yet this quarterfinal run carries a different significance: it was achieved by a man who, less than a year earlier, had been on an operating table, just removed a tumor, and returned to the world stage.

## Records Still Unwritten

Having beaten cancer, Chou does not treat his comeback as a finale. In 2024 and 2025 he completed a back‑to‑back victory at the Arctic Open in Finland.[^8] In 2025, at age 35 and 150 days, he reached the final of the Indonesia Open, defeating the world’s king of badminton en route and setting a new record for the oldest player to contest a Super 1000 men’s singles final.[^7] The same year he achieved a new high‑age world‑ranking milestone, being described by the badminton community as a “role model for younger players.”[^9]

![Chou Tien-chen powering a smash at the 2024 Taipei Open, the same year he also won the Arctic Open](/article-images/people/chou-tien-chen-taipei-open-2024.webp)

As of June 2026, the 36‑year‑old remains ranked No. 6 in the world, the oldest regular in the men’s singles elite.[^12] In a sport where many retire by age 30, he has extended his professional lifespan dramatically. The new generation now views defeating him as a career milestone, yet he still stands there, inviting the next wave to chase higher.

## Off‑Court Chou

Chou has long incorporated public service into his career plan. He has said he wants to deepen his charitable work while he still has influence, uncertain whether retirement will afford the same energy.[^17] Over the years he has supported children with rare diseases and education in Taiwan’s remote areas, deliberately keeping a low profile: “Charity isn’t done for the cameras; whether the media are watching or not, doing it is what matters.”[^17]

He does not maintain a fan page and rarely uses social media.[^1] For a player who has been world No. 2, reached three Olympic quarterfinals, defeated Lin Dan, and returned from cancer, the lack of hype lets his achievements speak for themselves. Chou’s path has always been a slow grind, not a flash of innate talent: through patience, faith, and an unyielding spirit, he lifts Taiwanese badminton higher, inch by inch.
