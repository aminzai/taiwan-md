# Ku's Dream: From Foreign Lenses to a Program Taiwan Can Be Understood by the World

> **Thirty-Second Overview**: Ku (酷), who is from Nanxi, France, and has lived in Taiwan for a long time, returned to Taiwan in 2017 and became a full-time creator in 2018. He first filmed Taiwanese food, French high school students, military training, and island challenges from a foreign perspective, before expanding "Chinese that even Taiwanese don't understand" into the six-episode reality show _Chinese Monster_ (中文怪物) in 2025. This program featured 100 foreigners competing for a NT$300,000 prize; with a production budget of about NT$5 million, the first episode surpassed 2 million views in two days. Ku's story is worth reading not just because he made Taiwan popular, but because he forces Taiwan to re-examine: when foreigners learn Chinese, are we encouraging them, or are we perpetually keeping them in the role of the guest? ## First Look at a Person, Then a Channel

In September 2025, one hundred foreigners living in Taiwan stood before the camera, waiting to answer a seemingly simple but actually difficult question: Whose Chinese is the best? The grand prize was NT$300,000 for _Chinese Monster_. [^1]

This was not a language cram school graduation ceremony. There was elimination, there was competition, and there was a question posed by a Frenchman: when someone has worked hard to speak your language, do we treat them as a guest, a performer, or a true member of this society?

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> This article does not label Ku solely as "a foreigner introducing Taiwan," but rather tracks how he turned an external perspective into a production methodology; while also retaining the unskippable question: when an individual channel grows into a team, must the creator also accept higher public scrutiny?

"I hope to make an international-level program for Taiwan that everyone in the world wants to watch," Ku said in an interview. [^2] This statement sounds like a creator's ambition, but it is also a test paper for the Taiwanese visual industry: among all the videos we have made about ourselves, is there one that someone who doesn't speak Chinese would be willing to sit down and watch?

## One Name, Three Moves

Ku was born in Nanxi, France, with the given name Richard Quentin Paul Camille. During his university years, he interned at the Taipei Hotel for three months before spending two and a half years studying abroad in South Korea. He returned to Taiwan in 2017 and decided to become a full-time YouTuber the following year. [^4] The name he used in Korea was Ku, and later incorporated the pronunciation of the Korean character "夢" (dream), _kkum_, into his Chinese name, becoming "Ku's Dream." [^2]

His first channel positioning was complex: a Frenchman living in Taiwan filming in Korean to introduce Taiwan to Koreans. This setup was not easy, but it perfectly illustrates Ku's early viewing position.

Later, he gradually shifted his audience from Korea toward Western audiences who were culturally closer. The channel evolved from personal observation and Vlogs to life challenges, cultural exchanges, and large-scale projects. [^3] This path did not have a single completed positioning statement; rather, it resembled someone constantly filming, correcting, and finding the next question based on audience feedback.

## Taiwan First Revealed Through Food

What Ku was most easily remembered for early on was the "First Time Eating Taiwanese Food" series. Stir-fried dishes, night markets, snacks, taro—these things that Taiwanese people know well and don't need to explain suddenly needed translation through the lens. The audience waited to see foreigners frown or nod, but they were also waiting for an answer: why do we care so much about these foods? [^5]

These types of videos are easily categorized as "Foreigners Reacting to Taiwan." But what makes Ku's work more interesting is that he gradually turned surprise into a concept, rather than just a reaction. The French high school student in Taiwan series turned cultural experiences into a coming-of-age story. The military challenge put foreigners' physical fitness alongside the memory of conscription among Taiwanese men. The island tour deliberately sought out relatively niche, absurd, or places not suitable for tourism promotion. [^5]

He also traveled across all of Taiwan to rest stops while filming, hitchhiking from one place to the next. The core of this action was not the summary "Taiwanese are friendly," but repeatedly asking strangers for a ride. As the camera followed the car, Taiwanese kindness transformed from an adjective into a concrete scene: someone stopped, someone was willing to detour, and someone took a foreigner to the entrance of the next journey. [^2]

Ku particularly loved taro, a preference that later became part of his personal brand, affiliated with convenience store collaborations, and dedicated taro shops. [^8] The narrower the topic, the more concrete Taiwan becomes. When the lens no longer tries to represent the entire island, differences in local culture, taste, and people have a chance to emerge.

## From Cultural Observation to Content Methodology

The precursor to _Chinese Monster_ was "Chinese that even Taiwanese don't understand" and Chinese listening challenges. Ku invited guests to listen to Taiwanese commercials, identify tones, and semantic meanings, making the frustrations most commonly encountered by foreigners learning Chinese into the game rules of the show. [^2] [^5]

This transition is crucial. The food series relied on the reaction of "first time eating." The Chinese series, however, placed differences into a repeatable set of rules.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> If "foreigners viewing Taiwan" only stops at surprise, the content quickly becomes formulaic; Ku's real turning point was transforming surprise into rules, forcing Taiwanese audiences to re-understand their own language. Who can understand an advertisement? Who can distinguish tones? Who can comprehend a sentence where a Taiwanese person thinks they spoke clearly even after swallowing words? The everyday language of Taiwan suddenly became an obstacle that everyone must face.

In February 2025, RTI English reported on the competition being prepared for _Chinese Monster_, stating that the event would challenge 100 foreigners in Mandarin, with a grand prize of NT$300,000. Ku hoped participants would connect more deeply with Taiwanese language and culture, allowing Taiwanese people to view their own culture from a new angle. [^1]

In an interview, Ku discussed a habit that few Taiwanese notice: as long as the person looks foreign, we often immediately switch to English, even if the person can answer in Chinese. [^2] This is usually done out of kindness, but it might keep someone who has spent time learning Chinese perpetually in the position of "a great foreigner," unable to become an ordinary person who doesn't need special care.

So, while _Chinese Monster_ ostensibly seeks "the best foreigners at Mandarin," it more deeply asks: are Taiwanese willing to treat Chinese as a door, rather than just a stage where foreigners get applause for saying "hello"? Ku even argued that as Taiwan becomes more open, society's expectations of foreigners learning Chinese can also rise. [^2]

## One Hundred People, Five Million Yuan, Seventeen Cameras

_Chinese Monster_ has six episodes with a production budget of about NT$5 million. The show used 17 cameras and over 30 surveillance cameras, designing stages based on tones, nuance, listening skills, and real-time reactions, keeping each episode around 40 minutes long. [^3] [^9]

_The Economist_ reported that the first episode surpassed 2 million views in two days, with channel traffic growing by nearly 350%. Ku admitted that for the team's first complex large-scale production, the post-production material reached 17TB, and the second episode was even edited until moments before going live. [^9]

What is most noteworthy about these numbers is not just "how much money was spent," but how it forced a YouTube channel to undertake work previously belonging to television stations: pre-production, on-site coordination, participant management, editing, sponsorship integration, and managing audience emotions after broadcast.

Ku and two core partners repeatedly discussed, overturned, and restarted the competition format, stages, and show structure. Another interview recorded that 68 staff members and 17 cameras were mobilized on site. Ku argued for designing the scenario first, and letting the on-site reactions develop the story. [^2] [^3]

In the second stage, participants decided who advanced themselves; the team leaders chose themselves, which was an unexpected turn not planned before filming. Authenticity here does not mean a complete lack of design, but rather that the design leaves room for surprise. [^3]

Ku transitioned from shooting alone to being the general director facing dozens of staff members and 100 participants. After the show went live, he admitted that he skipped the intermediate practice stages, going straight to the finish line by high-speed rail, and acknowledged that the work still had many shortcomings. [^10]

This self-description is more memorable than "comparable to Netflix." Because of the difficulty in large-scale production, it's not just about setting up the scenes, but about making sure every person placed in the show can be seen without being cut into a consumable character for the audience.

## Why YouTube Can Produce What TV Cannot

After _Chinese Monster_ became popular, the discussion quickly turned to another question: why cannot Taiwanese television stations produce similar shows? A producer and TV executive interviewed by _The Economist_ pointed out that their audiences, viewing methods, revenue sources, filming techniques, and post-production methods are all different; they cannot be compared solely on technical specifications. [^9]

Ku observed that large French YouTubers often have production companies assisting with planning, whereas in Taiwan, creators often still have to find the TV station staff themselves to form a team. [^3] This indicates that the Taiwanese creator environment is still figuring things out: individual channels have audiences, speed, and emotional support. But when production costs increase, work originally sustained by individual will gradually becomes an issue of teams, contracts, and processes.

Ku's judgment on content quality was direct: "Is it meaningful if it only has quality but doesn't care what the audience wants to see? And if it only chases traffic but the content is trash, that's also meaningless." [^3] This sentence breaks down the dilemma creators are often forced to choose between: content must be seen to have an impact. After being seen, something worth remembering must remain.

The original intention of _Chinese Monster_ was not just the show itself. Ku stated he hoped to encourage more foreigners to learn Chinese, and even come to Taiwan for practice because of the program. [^9] This gave the show an added ambition of cultural education: it made Chinese entertaining, but did not treat those learning Chinese merely as a source of comedy.

## Taiwan-France Exchange Is Not Just Laughter on Camera

Ku's cultural exchange has also left the YouTube screen. In 2021, he launched the "Million Yuan Dream Project," supporting young people from Taiwan and France to achieve cross-national dreams through prizes. Qiu Zhenting (邱振霆), a student from the French Department at Fu Jen Catholic University, was one of the winners from Taiwan, and the project was described by the university as part of promoting Taiwan-France cultural exchange. [^11] Ku's official Facebook video also preserved public records of recruiting and explaining this project. [^12]

This event shares a similar logic with _Chinese Monster_: turning "cultural exchange" from a beautiful slogan into a project that requires resources, participants, and follow-up action. The former sends people to another country to fulfill dreams; the latter concentrates people of different nationalities and different Chinese levels in one competition venue. Culture is no longer just a background to be introduced, but something participants must personally experience.

In 2023, Ku obtained permanent residency in Taiwan. [^13] This change in status added weight to his story. He was no longer just "a Frenchman living in Taiwan" as a show concept, but someone who works, lives, builds a team here, and must be scrutinized by local public rules.

## What the Audience Sees is Only a Small Part

After the success of the large-scale program, Ku began to encounter another problem that editing cannot solve. During the broadcast, some participants were subjected to online attacks. He admitted in an interview that what the audience sees is only a small part of the participant, but they might treat the edited image as a complete personality. [^2]

He also noted that female participants who faced severe attacks were not unique to Taiwan. [^2] When amateurs are thrust onto high-traffic stages, the production team cannot just say "that was the audience's choice." How the show is edited, how titles are given, and who is cast as a conflict center all affect how the audience understands a real person.

This is where Ku's statement, "what the audience sees is truly only a small part," deserves the most scrutiny. [^2] Creators know that the camera makes choices, but audiences often forget they are seeing the result of those choices. When a production team uses the reactions of real amateurs to create drama, their responsibility for the aftermath increases.

## The Controversy of 2026: When Dreams Become Employment Relationships

In April 2026, "Ku's Dream" sparked controversy due to recruitment announcements and public statements from former employees. United News Agency reported that the dispute involved issues like job content, work boundaries, resignation, and labor insurance. These contents are allegations compiled by media and statements from parties involved; they cannot be written as facts confirmed by authorities or courts. [^6]

On April 8th, Ku released a public explanation on Threads. He stated that the employees in question worked for about a month and a half, that the company had mistakenly used health insurance reporting forms, which were later corrected and retroactively covered. He also denied making any derogatory remarks about Taiwanese people, admitting that the recruitment text used the term "glass heart" (玻璃心) inappropriately, and apologized and corrected it. [^7]

Therefore, this incident has at least three layers. First, whether the employer handled insurance, working hours, handover, and resignation according to law should not be solved solely by the creator's personal charisma. Second, media and audiences, when relaying allegations, should distinguish between "former employee statements," "company responses," and "official determinations." Third, when a channel evolves from individual creation into a team requiring hired employees, the dream is no longer just the founder's private ideal.

There is a symmetrical relationship here that is often overlooked.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Cultural exchange is not an immunity card. It can start with goodwill, but it must ultimately return to linguistic equality, labor regulations, and the rights of the subjects depicted. The Ku on camera can demand higher expectations from Taiwanese people regarding Chinese learners; the Ku behind the camera must also accept the expectations of the audience, employees, and society toward the creator. The former is the reciprocity of cultural exchange; the latter is responsibility within a power dynamic.

## After One Program Left YouTube

The life cycle of _Chinese Monster_ did not end with its initial explosion in popularity. Reports from July 2026 indicated that the six episodes accumulated nearly 20 million views and were licensed for broadcast on Hami. However, because the premiere platform was not television, the team could not qualify for the Golden Bell Awards. [^15] This detail shows that online programs can have the production scale of traditional TV, but the system may not be ready to handle it.

Ku did not immediately announce a second season. Reports quoted him saying that while there is no immediate chance for the next season, the team still needs time to accumulate planning, filming, editing, and narrative experience. [^15] This choice must be viewed through the lens of traffic logic: when a program succeeds, the market usually demands faster replication. But creators may need more time to process failure, costs, and responsibility.

## The Days When the Channel Was Hacked

In February 2026, the "Ku's Dream" channel with 2.28 million subscribers was hacked. Taipo reported that Ku clicked a verification link seemingly related to a collaboration with MrBeast, after which his account was taken over. After assistance, the channel was recovered on the evening of February 3rd, and 448 videos were saved. [^16]

This incident turned "the channel" from an abstract brand into a very concrete asset: 448 videos, long-accumulated viewers, algorithmic history, and data invested by a team over many years. To the audience, the channel was just an avatar that suddenly disappeared on the homepage. To the creator, it was a database of years of work that could be removed in a few hours.

It also sheds light on an aspect of _Chinese Monster_ that is less visible. When people talk about large programs, they often discuss NT$5 million, 17 cameras, and 100 participants, but they rarely discuss account permissions, collaboration invitations, cloud files, and backups. When a YouTube channel functions like a small production company, cybersecurity is no longer just a personal habit; it is the infrastructure of the content industry.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Whether a channel can be seen by the world depends on its content; whether a channel can survive the next incident depends on permissions, backups, and systems. Ku's Dream moved from "how to make it bigger" to "how to save what has already been made."

## How One Foreigner Made Taiwan That Belongs Not Only to Foreigners

The greatest value of Ku's Dream is not just that "it looks different because it's foreign." The novelty of foreigners fades; any reaction of "first time eating" can become a predictable format. What truly advanced the channel was his transformation of an external perspective into a production methodology: deepening small topics, placing cultural differences into game rules, and making Taiwanese people also subjects of questioning.

From a hitchhiking video to a 100-person Chinese competition, Ku has been doing the same thing: finding details in Taiwanese life that can be translated, competed over, and re-viewed. These details may not be grand, but they allow someone unfamiliar with Taiwan to stay first because of tones, taro, fried chicken, or a strange advertisement, and finally realize they are understanding an island.

The dream in "Ku's Dream" is no longer just about filming Taiwan for foreigners. It is more like throwing a question back at Taiwan: can we make our language, daily life, and programs good enough that others are willing to keep watching without first having to understand Taiwan?

This question was not resolved by the high traffic of _Chinese Monster_. When a Frenchman uses Chinese to remind Taiwan "it can be better," and when a YouTube channel begins to have the scale of a TV program, the dream truly enters the difficult part. Instead of asking if the next work will be bigger, it is better to ask if it will be more honest. Instead of asking if there will be more viewers, it is better to ask whether those who were filmed can still maintain their voice after the show ends.

## A Question Worth Staying For After Thirty Seconds

Ku's Dream has allowed Taiwanese people to see their food, accents, and life, and it has allowed foreigners to see that learning Chinese is not just an exam of "how well one speaks." It turned the gaze of strangers into a program, turned the traffic of the program into industry ambition, and pushed that industry ambition back onto the creator: after scaling up, who is responsible for the people who were filmed? Who is responsible for labor within the team? Who is responsible for the harm caused by a single sentence?

This might be the least like tourism promotion in Ku's story. Cultural exchange will not always only involve welcome, emotion, and warmth. It also includes linguistic power, viewing choices, production costs, and employer responsibility. When a foreign creator decides to live long-term in Taiwan, what they gain is not just a stage to introduce Taiwan to the world, but a status that must face rules alongside Taiwan.
