> **30-Second Summary:** In 1876, Ding Richang's administration of Taiwan was not an isolated project but a blueprint connecting coastal defense, telegraphy, railways, coal mining, colonization, and civil governance. The Tainan to Qihou/Anping telegraph line, completed in 1877, marked the beginning of modern communication in Taiwan. However, the railway and armored ships were never finished due to financial and systemic limitations. Reading Ding Richang is not just about what he did, but also about why a cross-departmental plan could only complete a small part within the late Qing administrative machinery.[^1] [^2]

In the winter of 1876, Governor-in-Charge Ding Richang traveled by sea from Fuzhou, arriving at Jijin, and subsequently visiting Su'ao, Hou Shan before returning to Tainan Prefecture. This inspection was not a mere local tour; it placed an official familiar with Westernization into the tense situation of Taiwan following the Bohan Incident.[^1]

The focus of this timeline is not to portray Ding Richang as the starting point for all later construction, but rather to place his concepts within the decision-making sequence of the time. Coastal defense anxieties preceded a revaluation of ports, mineral resources, and transportation. The telegraph was completed first, which does not mean other plans were unimportant, but rather that it was the most likely project to be executed with limited funds.[^1] [^2] [^6]

The following year, the telegraph line from Tainan Prefecture to Qihou, and then to Anping, was completed, spanning about 95 kilometers. This marked Taiwan's introduction of telecommunication technology and is the clearest line left by Ding Richang in Taiwan.[^2]

But what Ding Richang truly wanted to lay out was never just a single line. He intended to connect telegraphy, railways, coal mining, naval vessels, and colonization into a comprehensive coastal defense system. What was ultimately completed was the telegraph. The railway and armored ships remained stuck in questions of money, systems, and time.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> Ding Richang’s story is not as simple as "modernization existed during the Qing Dynasty"; it is about **a person seeing a system but only being able to complete the finest line first.** Telegraphy, railways, coal mining, and coastal defense were interconnected in his memorials, yet they were disassembled into different bureaus, budgets, and priorities during execution. This gap is more worth reading than the word "pioneer."

## Looking at Taiwan through Coastal Defense, Not Local Administration

Ding Richang was born in Fengshun, Guangdong, in 1823, and died in 1882. His particular significance within the late Qing Self-Strengthening Movement lies not only in advocating for learning Western technology but also in viewing military affairs, finance, transportation, and civil governance as a single problem. A monograph published by the Institute of Modern History, Academia Sinica, understands his achievements and setbacks within the context of the entire transformation of the Self-Strengthening Movement.[^3]

In 1868, Ding Richang had already proposed making Taiwan a center for South Seas coastal defense. After the Bohan Incident in 1874, when the Qing court re-discussed coastal defense, he more clearly advocated for stationing armored ships in Taiwan and placing Taiwan within the overall defensive line of the East Coast.[^4]

A statement he later left can almost be considered an opening address to the entire plan: "Only if Taiwan is prepared can the coast be without worry; if Taiwan is unstable, the whole situation is likely to shake."[^2]

This idea was rooted in its historical context. When Japan invaded Southern Taiwan in 1874, the Qing court began viewing Taiwan not merely as a periphery but as a node for maritime traffic and Southeast defense. The Taiwan that Ding Richang saw was an island that needed to be connected to fleets, mineral resources, roads, and communication networks.

However, "seeing the system" does not mean controlling the system. The finances of Taiwan had to be coordinated with Fujian Province and various South Seas regions; projects required finding people familiar with new technologies; military equipment faced scrutiny from different bureaus. The memorials cited in academic papers repeatedly mention difficulties in raising funds, delays in disbursing payments, and disputes over funding between railways, mining, and coastal defense.[^2]

These obstacles gave Ding Richang's reforms a modern paradox: the more he tried to make things a mutually supporting system, the more he had to cross boundaries that the existing administrative machinery was least equipped to cross.

This is why his vision for Taiwan differs from the railway projects later associated with Liu Mingchuan. Liu Mingchuan was someone who scaled up provincial construction. Ding Richang earlier argued: without transportation and communication, coastal defense is merely a troop deployment on paper.[^5]

## A Telegraph Line Made the Island Shorter

At the end of 1876, Ding Richang personally inspected Taiwan. Academic research indicates that he assessed Taiwan's coastal defense position and transportation conditions together, demanding the establishment of telegraph lines, railways, and mining operations so that communication, freight transport, and troop deployment would no longer be fragmented.[^2]

The path of the telegraph line was specific: from Tainan Prefecture to Qihou, and then from Tainan to Anping. Historical records on Taiwan's transportation kept by the National Archives also list this 1877 project as the beginning of Taiwan's adoption of telecommunication technology.[^6]

Viewed today, 95 kilometers may not seem like an astonishing figure. But in 1877, it represented whether orders could reach the port faster, whether news of foreign threats could return to the prefecture more quickly, and whether an island had begun to possess its own real-time communication.

This line also illustrates Ding Richang's working method. He did not wait for a complete modern nation to appear before starting construction. He first seized the nodes that could change the overall pace and then linked those nodes to military and financial matters. The telegraph was not an isolated technological display; it was the nervous system of the coastal defense plan.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> **The modernity of the telegraph is not in how it looks today, but in the fact that it was the first thing to make Taiwan’s distance a manageable problem.** The route from Tainan Prefecture to Qihou and Anping connected ports and commands. It meant coastal defense no longer relied solely on ship speed or post station relay, but began relying on a reusable network.

## Railways Did Not Disappear, They Were Replaced by Another Future

In his memorials, Ding Richang stated: "Taiwan must build railways and telegraph lines for communication, freight transport, and troop deployment."[^2] This sentence places the railway in an unromantic context: it was not scenic travel or merely a commercial investment, but something placed on the same operational table as the military, coal mining, ports, and administrative commands.

He once advocated for moving materials from the Suzhou-Shanghai railway dismantled in the mainland to use in Taiwan and discussed routes with engineers. The _Late Qing History of Westernization Materials_ compiled by Academia Sinica includes Ding Richang's memorials concerning Taiwanese track gauge, mining, and telegraphy, clearly noting that he was one of the earliest advocates for railways in Taiwan.[^1]

However, a railway required more than just tracks. It needed money, engineers, stable administrative authority, and long-term maintenance systems. Ding Richang also had to compete for funding with armored ships, gun batteries, artillery, telegraphy, and mining. Historical records compiled by researchers show that while the Qing court recognized these matters as important, it also faced the financial dilemma of "simultaneous undertakings requiring immense expenditure."[^2]

A sharper contradiction was that Ding Richang later advocated reallocating funds originally intended for Taiwan's track gauge to purchase armored ships. The railway could only serve Taiwan, but the armored ships could support a larger scope of coastal defense. Thus, constructions that were supposed to mutually support each other were forced into a list where only one item could be done first.[^2]

This was not simply "visionary failure." It was more like the most difficult arithmetic of late Qing reform: every item could convince someone, but no reliable purse could pay for them all simultaneously.

## Coal Mining, Taxation, and Civil Governance Were One War

After arriving in Taiwan, Ding Richang first noted the Jijin coal mines, then included sulfur, kerosene, and iron ore in his inspection. The bibliography of historical materials from Academia Sinica shows that he memorialized the Bajou coal mine in Jijin and proposed coordinating Taiwanese track gauge and mining.[^1]

In his conception, coal mining was not just an industry. It could supply steamships and machinery, and it could find financial resources for coastal defense. If mining lacked transportation, what was dug up could not be moved. If railways lacked mining, the construction cost was harder to convince the court of.

The same logic appeared in taxation. Later materials compiling Ding Richang's governance of Taiwan mention that he dealt with excessive taxes, civil administration, and agricultural development. These measures cannot be directly equated with modern welfare policies, but they show that he knew coastal defense did not only happen on gun batteries; whether the populace was willing to bear tax burdens and administrative costs was also part of defense.[^7]

The problem was that these reforms were constrained by Qing bureaucratic governance. Ding Richang's policies of "colonization," mining, and pacification were still governmental projects named, planned, and promoted by officials; local society was not a blank slate waiting to be filled. Research materials also remind us that Ding Richang’s time in Taiwan was short, and many plans were limited by insufficient funds and bureaucratic constraints, resulting in limited practical effectiveness.[^2]

Furthermore, most of the surviving documents are official memorials, administrative compilations, and later research. They are good at telling us what the government wanted to do, but they cannot necessarily present how local residents, indigenous communities, and those whose resources were conscripted responded. This is a gap that must be preserved when reading Ding Richang: we cannot equate planning with reality just because the blueprint was meticulously drawn.

From this perspective, the significance of the telegraph line is also more complex. It did indeed shorten the distance of administrative and military intelligence, but it also served faster governance and mobilization. Technology does not choose its use; the people and systems that use it determine for whom speed is accelerated.

Therefore, his plan cannot be written as a straight path to modernization. A more accurate description is: he was the first person to list many issues that would later be handled separately on the same blueprint in Taiwan.

📝 Curator's Note: **The most difficult moments of reform are often not when one doesn't know what to do, but when every task requires fighting over the same pot of money.**

## Half a Year of Inspection Left a Shadow Longer Than His Tenure

After being appointed Governor-in-Charge of Fujian, Ding Richang was responsible for all matters in Taiwan. Existing archives show that by November of the first year of Guangxu, there were edicts regarding his appointment to manage pacification and affairs in Taiwan during winter and spring.[^8]

His time in Taiwan was not long. Academic papers compile it as arriving in the winter/spring of 1876 and leaving due to illness in 1877. This gives his story a strange proportion: short tenure, grand plans, few completed projects, but many later cited blueprints.[^2]

A summary from the Institute of Modern History, Academia Sinica, states that Li Hongzhang praised Ding Richang as "a rare and capable official in Westernization who could handle difficult times," and this same summary does not shy away from his difficulties and setbacks.[^3] This juxtaposition is important because it refuses to place Ding Richang solely into the category of "pioneer" or "failure."

He did promote telegraphy, and he did bring railways and mining into the core discussion of Taiwanese governance. But armored ships never formed an army according to his blueprint, and the railway did not form a complete network during his tenure. The compilation of national archives also points out that transportation construction in Qing-ruled Taiwan was often driven by local officials independently, lacking central governing bodies and complete planning, thus limiting development and continuity.[^6]

This limitation was not a personal flaw of Ding Richang, but part of the systemic conditions. He could propose cross-departmental plans, but he did not have a stable administrative machinery to turn those plans into long-term projects.

Later, when Taiwan established its own province, Liu Mingchuan took over larger-scale provincial administration and construction, which is often written as the completion of Ding Richang's vision. However, their positions of power, financial conditions, and historical circumstances were different. Attributing all subsequent achievements to the foresight of the predecessor obscures a truly important question: how many institutional transformations must a single plan undergo before it can become roads, telegraph lines, or mines?

Ding Richang's name appears not only in memorials but also in local architecture and plaques. These relics cannot directly prove that all governance measures were successful, but they remind us that officials' activities were preserved by local society in different ways. Archives preserve administrative language; plaques preserve relationships, gifts, and local memory. When placed together, the person is not just a string of titles.[^4]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
> A telegraph line can be measured in kilometers, but a plaque cannot be measured only by its size. The former shows how the state accelerated commands; the latter lets us see how locals incorporated an official into their space. Ding Richang’s history of Taiwan moves between these two scales: above is the coastal defense blueprint; below are the traces left by people and place.

## Reading Engineering Imagination from Memorials

What is noteworthy about Ding Richang's memorials is that they were not requests for funds for a single project. Next to telegraphy, there appeared railways; next to railways, there was coal mining; and coal mining interacted with steamships, armaments, and ports. This writing transformed Taiwan from a place needing official management into an entity requiring surveying, transport, communication, and investment.[^1] [^2]

This engineering imagination also had its dangers. When officials put land, ports, mineral resources, and population into the same defense calculation, local residents were easily reduced to tax sources, labor, or objects needing pacification. Therefore, this article does not directly label Ding Richang's pacification and colonization as progressive policies, nor does it treat the "civilization" language in official archives as a self-description of local society. What can be confirmed is that he did include these matters in his governance plan, not whether the locality changed exactly according to the plan.[^2] [^8]

From today's perspective, this distinction is important. Modernization is not just the sum of wires, tracks, and machines; it also includes who has the right to decide where a line runs, who mines which resources, and what local knowledge gets written into official reports. The materials left by Ding Richang show us how an official proposed a systemic solution, and they also show us the people who were not mentioned in the plan.

## Looking Back Today, Pioneer is Another Myth

Ding Richang is often placed between Shen Baozhen and Liu Mingchuan. This position easily creates a neat historical relay: Shen Baozhen begins, Ding Richang continues, Liu Mingchuan completes. But real history is messier. People leave office, money runs out, materials are repurposed, and later constructions do not always follow the original design.[^1]

The most valuable part of the _Late Qing History of Westernization Materials_ is that it puts memorials, imperial edicts, and subsequent projects together. Ding Richang's suggestions and Liu Mingchuan's later telegraphy, railways, and machinery bureau can be seen in relation to each other, but they cannot be compressed into "Ding Richang predicted everything."[^1]

The English Wikipedia page for Ding Richang places his governance of Taiwan, diplomacy, military reform, and Self-Strengthening Movement within a single biographical narrative, listing the modern Chinese studies bibliography it relies on. This reminds us that when reading an official, one must not only read their plans but also distinguish what is directly recorded in the archives from what later generations have woven into a single character image.[^9]

The most powerful image left by Ding Richang may not be a completed building, but the telegraph line stretching from Tainan to Qihou and Anping. It was thin, yet it tied together an entire set of unfinished problems: Who is responsible for Taiwan's security? Who pays for modernization? Is communication for the people, the military, or both simultaneously?

In 1877, the telegraph line reached its destination first. The railway, coal mining, and armored ships did not. This was not a perfect ending, but it was the best entry point to understanding Ding Richang: **Taiwan's modernization began not with completion, but when someone first drew problems that were previously separated onto the same map.**
