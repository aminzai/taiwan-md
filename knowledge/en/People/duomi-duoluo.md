# Domidolora: Turning "Mistakes" into Public Performance—Who Has the Right to Correct Whom Behind the Laughter?

> **30-Second Summary**
>
> In September 2024, Domidolora corrected the Japanese copy of Taiwan's beverage brand Hayao and Cha as a "Japanese Police," leading to a brand apology, text revision, and an offer of 87% off. [^1] In June 2026, he applied a similar standard to works by Huang Shanliao, moving from linguistic errors to literary criticism. [^2] The key aspect of Domidolora is not just his daring commentary, but turning "pointing out what is wrong" into a public performance with a role, rhythm, and audience participation; after the laughter subsides, the questions left are: who has the right to judge, and can the judged party respond?

In September 2024, a video put the Japanese copy of Taiwan's beverage brand Hayao and Cha under scrutiny. Domidolora corrected grammar while repeatedly shouting "Beh Qih Ai" (a colloquial exclamation). Hayao and Cha later apologized below the video, stating they had hired a Japanese teacher to revise the text and offered an 87% discount. [^1] This was not typical brand crisis management: a YouTuber living in Tokyo became the enforcer in the video through catching errors, while the brand rewrote the caught mistakes into its own promotional language.

In June 2026, his commentary on writer Huang Shanliao once again sparked discussion. Reports indicated that Domidolora discussed multiple works by Huang Shanliao in one go, using the phrase "worst ever" in the video title, while Huang Shanliao himself responded that he would continue to learn and grow. [^2] These two events, separated by nearly two years, shifted the target from a beverage's slogan copy to a bestselling author's book, but the role remained unchanged: he always first found something "wrong," and then staged the correction as a play.

This is where Domidolora is most understandable. Common discourse tends to categorize him as a "caustic celebrity influencer," but this description reverses the cause and effect. He did not start with a toxic persona and randomly attack things; a closer description is that he tied together his critical standards, speaking rhythm, and a recognizable character, allowing the audience to know why they should stay to watch the next typo, line of dialogue, or book.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Domidolora's brand is not "he dares to criticize," but rather that he processes "I disagree" into a viewable format; while the audience laughs, they are also invited to temporarily stand beside his standards.

## Role Precedes Resume

Public reports introduce Domidolora as Lin Wei-ting (林煒庭), a YouTuber who studied in Japan. Reports also mention that he attended Tsinghua University (青山學院大學) and has N1 Japanese Language Proficiency Test experience, and has been exposed to the training system of Japanese comedians. [^3] These credentials explain why he is capable of discussing Japanese, but they do not explain why he turns Japanese into a comedy.

His channel does not only handle language. From critiques of "feces-like" works to controversial pieces, he gradually made "evaluating why a work is ugly" the content itself. [^3] The significant shift in this trajectory was moving professional knowledge from the classroom into the character: N1 is no longer just an exam score, but an ID card that the "Japanese Police" can present; reading experience is no longer just personal taste, but evidence that can be judged on camera.

What the article page directly addresses is how he gained recognition in Japan through manga and cultural commentary after the Hayao and Cha incident in 2024. [^1] For a character study, this selection is important: there is no need to write unverified credentials as established facts just to fill out a timeline.

## "Japanese Police" Is Not a Teacher; It's an Entrance Method

DailyView’s description of the Hayao and Cha incident was specific: Domidolora corrected the copy as "Japanese Police," ending each correction with "Beh Qih Ai"; Hayao and Cha admitted in comments that the copy had issues, stating they had completed revisions and were preparing to update their official website. [^1] What is truly interesting here is not how many mistakes the brand made, but how the errors were placed into an audience-familiar rhythm: discovery, arrest, judgment, apology from the perpetrator.

Hayao and Cha's response also prevented the performance from ending in humiliation. The official account posted "we will work hard to improve because of so many mistakes" in the comments, and the brand responded with 87% off for "Beh Qih Ai." [^1] Domidolora was thus not just correcting the other party, but also giving them a chance to return to the stage using the same language. Criticism did not disappear, nor did commercial operations. The combination of both created the viral power of this video.

Calling him a "Japanese teacher" misses this point. A teacher's job is to guide students to the correct answer; a Japanese Police officer's job is to make errors visible in public. The former requires stability, completeness, and patience; the latter requires a clear perpetrator, a repeatable catchphrase, and a punchline that the audience can grasp within seconds. Domidolora chose the latter, so his most successful content may not be the most complete language lesson, but rather the scene that makes people immediately understand "this is really weird."

## From Japanese to Works: Standards Are Portable

The 2026 Huang Shanliao incident shows that "Japanese Police" is merely a superficial role. ETtoday reported that Domidolora released a video critiquing Huang Shanliao's works on June 5, and Huang Shanliao responded later by saying he would continue to learn. [^2] Subsequent reports from Yahoo described this sudden rise in subscribers after he named Cai Aga (蔡阿嘎) and Huang Shanliao as him continuously gaining viewers. The report also mentioned that he had gradually accumulated an audience through critiques of controversial works and negative manga reviews early on. [^3]

The common structure here is not "Japanese" or "literature," but the publicization of standards. Domidolora first tells the audience what standard he uses to look at something, and then demonstrates how a work fails to meet that standard. When the target was Hayao and Cha, the standard was Japanese copy; when the target was a novel, the standard became narrative, text, and reading experience. The subject can change, but the character's action does not: turning private judgment into public trial, and turning the trial into a shareable video.

Another example from the Liberty Times illustrates this portability. Domidolora once shared an experience in a boxing class in Japan where a Chinese student demanded a "sparring match," and the video was dug up again to cause discussion. He edited the situation where the Japanese coach stopped the conflict into a pun related to the "First Island Chain." [^4] This is not Japanese teaching, nor is it a book review, yet it remains the same content machine: a specific scene, an unexpected action, and a judgment that the audience can immediately relay.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** What he truly sells is the speed of judgment, not the answer. When viewers share the video, they are often sharing not just "which Japanese phrase was wrong," but also "see, we now have a common joke and a common standard."

## Controversy Pushes the Character Off-Screen

The hardest part of Domidolora to simplify comes from the sexual harassment controversy after his collaboration with Li Yu-xi in 2023. Mirror Media reported Li Yu-xi's accusations and Domidolora's rebuttal on social media at the time. The report also presented his statement admitting some jokes might have been uncomfortable, but denying physical boundary crossing. [^5] This content cannot be rewritten as "who told the truth," because it was still a stage of mutual clashes between parties.

Subsequent reports in December 2024 indicated that the first instance trial at Banqiao District Court in New Taipei ordered Domidolora to compensate Li Yu-xi 100,000 yuan. The report also noted Domidolora's statement about appealing and his public reaction to the ruling. [^6] A report from October 2025 stated that the second instance found the related actions did not meet the level of sexual harassment, overturning the original judgment, with no appeal allowed in the case. The same report listed the dialogue, video footage, and statements of both parties involved in the judgment. [^7]

This legal battle cannot be used to back up Domidolora's commentary style, nor can it be compressed into an entertainment headline like "comeback victory." It truly exposes another risk: when a creator speaks with a judgmental tone for a long time, audiences easily transplant the "this is wrong" from the video onto real-life personal disputes. But judicial procedures deal with evidence, actions, and legal composition, not who speaks better or whose editing has more rhythm.

In 2023 reports, Domidolora said his channel was originally intended only to make comedy videos but had "consecutively caused a disturbance." [^5] This phrase carries an uncomfortable accuracy: his content relies on making friction funny, but real-life friction is not always recoverable after the laughter ends. From brand copy to sexual harassment allegations, what separates them is whether the subject of criticism can withstand the same performance rules.

## After Being Seen, Who Can Still Be the Audience?

Domidolora's popularity did not start from "he was qualified," but from the audience being willing to temporarily lend him that qualification. DailyView’s article recorded netizens calling him an N1 Japanese master, and also noted that Hayao and Cha chose to publicly correct after being criticized. [^1] These two reactions together form the complete picture of online influence: on one side, the audience gives standards to the creator; on the other, the criticized party decides whether or not to accept those standards.

Therefore, Domidolora is not simply a person who became famous by complaining. He made criticism into a fixed format: first establishing his own standard, then finding a visible error, and finally using laughter to pull the audience to his side. This format allows language, manga, books, and daily events to enter the same stage, and it makes every "catch" of his carry an unresolved question: when we laugh in agreement with him, are we truly understanding an issue, or are we handing over judgment to someone who is better at performing?

His most powerful aspect is that this question was not concluded by the video. Domidolora caught typos, finished book reviews, and edited boxing jokes; what remains is not a perpetually correct police officer, but a group of viewers deciding in front of the screen who can be corrected next, who can rebut, and when to stop laughing.

## How the Audience Is Drawn In

Domidolora's videos often break down complex judgments into several steps that the audience can follow. First, an object appears: a slogan, a book, manga, or a dialogue from a boxing class. Next, he points out the gap between that object and common sense. Finally, he packages the judgment with a catchphrase, reaction, or pun. This format lowers the barrier to entry; viewers do not need to understand the entire background first but can find a moment worth relaying in the clip.

In the Hayao and Cha incident, the brand's apology was not incidental news in the video, but part of the plot. DailyView quoted the brand’s comments, where Hayao and Cha admitted "so many mistakes" and said they would work hard to improve; then offering an 87% discount, turning the corrected vocabulary into a discount. [^1] An external critic was thus placed within the brand's own narrative; errors that might have only had negative effects became an image project of "we accepted this joke."

A similar structure also appears in book reviews, but the party being corrected is no longer a brand that can be immediately revised. ETtoday’s report wrote about Huang Shanliao's response after criticism, not judging literary value for either side. [^2] This difference reminds readers: while critical content can be entertaining, the consequences of the critique depend on whether the target is a brand, an author, a media outlet, or someone with a personal relationship with the creator.

This is why "caustic" is not a sufficient explanation. Causticity only describes tone; it does not describe the distribution mechanism; Domidolora's videos are shareable because they simultaneously provide a character, standards, and nodes that can be cut into short clips. The audience is not passively receiving a string of abusive adjectives, but learning how to view an error within the video's rhythm.

## How One Name Replaces Another

Character reports often summarize influencers by subscriber counts, controversies, and the total number of viral videos. Yahoo reported Domidolora's early direction, study background, and 2026 subscriber growth on one line, explaining how his visibility rapidly increased after commenting on Cai Aga and Huang Shanliao. [^3] But subscriber count only shows that more people saw him; it does not explain why the audience remembers him.

What is remembered better are the titles. "Japanese Police" compresses a person's expertise into a role that can be repeatedly used in titles, comments, and brand responses. DailyView even listed related tags as "Big Tail YouTuber," "Japanese Police," and "Beh Qih Ai"; these words are not credentials, but they are closer to how an online personality exists than their resume. [^1]

This characterization has both advantages and costs. The advantage is that the audience can quickly recognize his position in unfamiliar subjects. The cost is that every appearance is expected to be faster, heavier, and more like the last one. When a creator's recognition is built on correction, silence, reservation, or admitting uncertainty tends to look uncharacteristic. Thus, the person is chased by their own format.

What truly continued between the 2024 brand copy and the 2026 book review was not a specific joke, but the fact that "the standard must be seen first." This makes Domidolora's channel look like an always-open inspection room: the subject can change, but the sign at the entrance does not; every time the audience walks in, they know someone will point out something wrong.

## A Little Uncertainty Must Remain Before Conclusion

The easiest mistake when writing about Domidolora is to organize him into a neat success timeline: studying abroad, learning Japanese, starting videos, becoming "Japanese Police," and going viral through controversy. This line can quickly provide background, but it cuts away all the accidents, loss of control, and costs.

For example, reports on the sexual harassment allegations require a reading experience completely different from brand copy. Mirror Media's 2023 report contained Domidolora's point-by-point responses; the 2024 report recorded the first civil trial awarding 100,000 yuan in compensation; and the 2025 report detailed the second instance overturning the original judgment. [^5] [^6] [^7] ETtoday's 2024 article also used "Awarded 100k for Sexual Harassment" as a title to cover the progress at that time. [^8] These articles can form a timeline, but they cannot be pieced together into a simple moral conclusion.

A creator's public role is often larger than what the creator can control. Domidolora can decide how the video is edited and how the catchphrase is spoken, but he cannot decide how the brand, the criticized author, the media, and the audience will accept this character. The reason the Liberty Times story about the boxing class was circulated again is not because it provided new expertise, but because the audience put that "First Island Chain" joke back into the public context of 2026. [^4]

Therefore, reading Domidolora cannot only ask "is what he says right?" It must also ask: where did his standards come from? Who can respond? What kind of mistake is suitable to be made into a joke, and what kind of harm should not be treated the same way? If we only leave the word "daring," the person is flattened; if we treat all controversies as traffic strategies, we also lose the facts.

Domidolora turned "catching mistakes" into a public performance; this is why he was remembered, and it is the weight he must bear. The audience can temporarily borrow his standards in laughter, but they do not have to keep the power of judgment in his hands forever. When the next video lights up the subtitle "Japanese Police deployed," true maturity is not immediately following the verdict, but first seeing clearly: was this mistake pointed out this time a copy error, a work flaw, a systemic issue, or someone's boundary?
