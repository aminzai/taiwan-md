> **30-second overview:** Gua Gi's real name is Chiu Wei-chieh. In 2017, he and his colleagues originally wanted to turn "Candidate for Village Chief" into a reality show. Later, they pushed the plan into the 2018 Taipei City Council election, winning 11,786 votes in Taipei's third electoral district as an independent candidate[^1]. Public Television Service described him as the first YouTuber to enter politics in Taiwan[^2]. His political career did not merge the internet and politics into one, but instead laid the gap between the two bare before the audience: videos can use editing to create rhythm, but the council and the market require long-term endurance. In 2025, "Don't Look at Work" stopped updating and disbanded, so this experiment that started from "democratic unboxing" finally had to face team loss, traffic impact caused by political statements, and millions of dollars in operating deficits[^3].

## A Thought That Was Originally Just Meant to Be Filmed

In June 2017, Chiu Wei-chieh and his young colleagues at the company were chatting, and the other party suggested turning "Candidate for Village Chief" into a real-person reality show. This idea did not start with a sense of solemnity about "I want to enter politics." Everyone imagined following the garbage truck to canvass, interacting with neighbors, and turning the local election into a possibly funny show. Chiu Wei-chieh later recalled that at the time, the judgment was that the village chief probably couldn't win, so it was more suitable as a low-cost project[^4].

The real turning point occurred when they were shooting the Universiade promotional video. A city government staffer told him that the village chief might not win, but the city councilor had a chance. Chiu Wei-chieh went home and recalculated the election situation in Songshan and Xinyi District, where he had lived for many years, and found that the threshold was not completely unreachable. The Initium Media's pre-election interview recorded this process, and also noted that at that time, "Don't Look at Work" had already accumulated more than 380,000 subscribers, so the program plan changed from a parody theme to a real election project[^4].

There is a detail here that is easy to be covered up by the later victory narrative. Chiu Wei-chieh did not first have a political career and then use the internet as a propaganda tool, but first treated politics as a content topic, and was eventually forced by his own plan to answer a real question: if the audience is willing to watch someone pretend to run for office, is he himself willing to really take the consequences of not winning, being attacked, and having to serve the constituents?

## "Democratic Unboxing" Is Not Making Politics Simple

In his first-person article in 2018, Chiu Wei-chieh described this election as a "democratic unboxing." He had no internal polls, and was not sure whether the channel's audience would turn their liking of the video into votes. What he could do was to write his political positions, visit the market, give street speeches, and bring the people who watched on the screen to the specific electoral district and personal relationships[^5].

This approach seems to fit the intuition of the internet era. Creators have a group of audiences they can directly reach, and the audience then convinces their parents or friends. But elections are not an extension of subscription numbers. When an internet audience walks into the market in Songshan and Xinyi District, he is no longer just a person who likes the post, but may also be a citizen who greets, questions, and asks for help. Chiu Wei-chieh wrote in his article that starting two weeks before the election, some citizens held their children and said they supported him, and some people shouted encouragement from the car window. This change is not a conversion rate that can be measured directly by the algorithm, but a trust relationship that must appear repeatedly in the local space[^5].

The data from the Central Election Commission brings this story back to a verifiable scale. In 2018, there were multiple candidates competing in Taipei's third electoral district, Chiu Wei-chieh ran as an independent candidate with the number 12, got 11,786 votes, a vote rate of 5.27%, and was listed in the elected list[^1]. This number can prove that he was elected, but it cannot alone prove that "internet traffic turns into votes." These are two different propositions, the former has election data, and the latter still needs more complete voter research to be established.

The Tech Orange reprinted the Central News Agency report, pointing out that Chiu Wei-chieh once estimated that his internet influence could only be converted to about 3,000 votes, and emphasized that if he did not actually participate in the election, the number of fans would not automatically turn into more than 10,000 votes. He spent about 1.2 million yuan on the election, refused many traditional media interviews, and did not appear on political commentary programs, setting "democratic unboxing" as the main axis of the election[^11]. This self-estimate is important because it pulls the YouTuber's participation in politics back from the success myth to a testable proposition: traffic may provide a starting point, but it cannot replace local contact and voter persuasion.

## From Channel Boss to Internet Person in the Council

Public Television Service News Lab looked back, Chiu Wei-chieh was elected Taipei City Councilor in 2018 with about 1.48 million yuan in election expenses, and then received more than 3,000 constituent services under the name "Magic Mirror." Public Television also pointed out that after he left office as Taipei City Councilor, the farewell video he published once received 1.16 million views[^2]. Putting all this data together presents not a simple "YouTuber becomes a councilor" story, but that content creators must relearn how to organize work: reply to individual cases, handle administrative procedures, and face problems that will not disappear just because the video is cut.

He also tried to bring his own live broadcast syntax into public institutions. The Judicial Yuan's Judicial Weekly reported that the Taiwan High Court once cooperated with Chiu Wei-chieh, broadcasting "The Cost of Swearing at the Exit! Real Court Simulation" on the "Gua Gi Live" channel, explaining the crimes of contempt of court, defamation, citizen judges, and court proceedings through plots and judges[^6]. The importance of this cooperation is not to turn the judiciary into entertainment, but to recognize that public institutions need new interpretation interfaces. Gua Gi's role thus changed from a content producer to a translator between the institution and the internet audience.

Translation does not mean neutrality. In addition to the audience, the content creators who entered the institution also include their own tone, rhythm, and suspicion of authority. This makes it easier for him to explain stiff topics to non-professional audiences, but also makes him have to bear the backlash of making politics sound too much like a show. For Gua Gi, the difficulty of public communication is not "how to make everyone laugh," but "how to make everyone understand and still be willing to continue to ask questions."

## The Audience Is Not the Voter, and the Voter Is Not the Traffic

The most valuable part of Gua Gi's campaign is not to prove that anyone with a subscription number can enter the council, but to make the friction between the two identities visible. The channel's audience is familiar with how Chiu Wei-chieh speaks, but the local constituents need to confirm whether he can continue to appear, handle details, and respond to questions even when there is no applause.

Public Television's report put the "Magic Mirror" more than 3,000 constituent services into this experience, which just explains the second act of political participation. The first act was to gather the viewers, and the second act was to let each specific case enter the administrative procedure. Both need communication, but the former pursues attention, and the latter must leave a record and follow-up responsibility[^2].

An earlier report in 2019 showed that "serving as a magic mirror" is not just a slogan for display. The website first handles personal privacy, and then makes the cases public, so that citizens can see what problems the councilor's team receives, which cases are not accepted, and which items are being processed. In the early stage of the website, there were about 20,000 non-repeated visitors, 140 cases received, and after excluding cases that cannot be executed or do not meet the acceptance principles, about 80 cases were accepted[^12].

Chiu Wei-chieh also said that the code of this system can be opened to other representatives. This detail adds an engineering meaning to "democratic unboxing": it is not only to live-stream the councilor's work to the audience, but also to try to sort out the constituent service into a traceable, public, and even replicable tool. For a councilor from a content production background, the real innovation is not necessarily that the tone is more interesting, but that the original service process that relies on personal trust is left with a structure.

This is also why "democratic unboxing" cannot be simply understood as broadcasting the council to everyone. What it really opens up is the creator's own way of working: what can be made public, which cases need to be protected, and which issues cannot be ended with a good joke. When internet politics enters local service, transparency is not only the existence of the camera, but also whether the audience can understand how long it will take for a thing to be completed, where it gets stuck, and who must be responsible.

Chiu Wei-chieh openly admitted in his first-person article that he might make wrong judgments, and might not be truly known by his supporters. This self-deprecating tone is contrary to the reliable image that the internet brand usually needs to maintain, but it is also closer to the actual state of the democratic system. Councilors are not automatically correct people after being subscribed, and creators are not people who can always represent the audience after getting the votes[^5].

## Joy Cannot Party and Political Negative Emotions

In 2019, Chiu Wei-chjie, along with Justin Queen and Retina, etc., applied to form the Happy Cannot Party. The English Taipei Times reported that the party's core appeal was to improve the happiness of Taiwanese people, and listed generational justice, marriage equality, and housing justice as concerns. Chiu Wei-chjie said at the time that politics should not only be left with hatred and negative emotions, and politics can also be the work of bringing happiness back[^7].

This attempt is easy to be seen as a YouTuber-style joke, but it also reflects his feelings after entering politics. Entering politics from entertainment content did not give him a clearer public role, but instead exposed him to more hostility and malice. The English report of Taiwan News recorded that he once said that after participating in politics, he felt hatred, malice, and negative emotions, and was worried that some articles he wanted to share would be removed for political reasons[^8].

The Happy Cannot Party did not become a long-term party, but it provides an important clue to understanding Gua Gi. This is not him trying to escape politics with jokes, but he is trying to advocate that "emotion" itself is also political content. Happiness, fatigue, cynicism, and the feeling of being attacked cannot be simply treated as private responses outside of policies. But bringing emotions into politics will also make the frustration of politics return more directly to the creator himself.

## The Channel Is Like an Ancestral Home, and Also Like a Company That Needs to Be Checked Out

"Don't Look at Work" was founded at the end of 2015. In March 2025, Chiu Wei-chjie announced that the channel would stop updating and the company would be disbanded. The Digital Times reported that the channel had accumulated more than 900,000 subscriptions at that time, with a total of more than 400 million views. The report sorted out three interrelated reasons: core members gradually left, political statements affected the number of views, and the company still had about 4 million yuan in funding gap at the end of the year, and the Golden Bell Awards also caused a loss of about 2 to 3 million yuan[^3].

The report of The Initium Media added his emotional description. Chiu Wei-chjie compared "Don't Look at Work" to an "ancestral home," and even if members left one after another, he still expected everyone to come back one day. He also admitted that as the boss, he should have ensured financial stability earlier, but missed the stop-loss opportunity due to long hesitation, and said "failure is on me"[^9].

Here is the most concrete contradiction of Gua Gi's story. Internet creation allows him to quickly gather the audience, launch elections, make judicial live broadcasts, and even create a party named after happiness. But when the channel becomes a team that needs to pay salaries, year-end bonuses, severance pay, and event costs, the most effective personal charisma cannot automatically turn into stable organizational governance. Creators can start a project with intuition, but they cannot maintain a ten-year company with intuition alone.

## After the Lights Go Out, the Golden Bell Awards Did Not Disappear with It

The disbandment of "Don't Look at Work" does not mean that the creator network it has built at the same time disappears. The Mirror Weekly reported that in the 7th Golden Bell Awards in 2025, the new executive committee took over the selection, Huang Shih brothers, Dcard, and Spiritual Greens continued to serve as members, and Gua Gi still served as an executive committee member, with the theme of the ceremony set as "rebirth"[^10]. This is an interesting organizational change: the award originally initiated by a single team began to find a structure that can bear the long-term responsibility more than the original founding team.

Therefore, Gua Gi's creative legacy is not only about what videos he has filmed, or how many votes he has received. More importantly, it shows how internet creators can enter public life, and also makes people see that this entry is not a successful crossing, but a series of conversions that must pay the price. From the reality show plan to council service, from judicial live broadcast to the Happy Cannot Party, and finally to the channel disbandment, every step turns "audience" back into "member," "voter," "stakeholder," or "co-bearer."

Chiu Wei-chjie wrote in his campaign article, "Don't believe me, and don't believe any political figure," and reminded supporters to give him direct criticism[^5]. This sentence, read after the channel went dark in 2025, does not seem to be self-deprecating, but rather a reminder to all personal brand politics. When a person is expected to be a superhero, his most honest work is not to maintain the myth, but to let the supporters see that the judgment will make mistakes, the team will leave, the accounts must be settled, and finally even the most family-like channel may close.

Gua Gi left behind is not a template of "YouTuber enters politics and is bound to succeed." A more realistic statement is that he carried out a political experiment that was originally born for filming to the end, until reality required it to hand over the votes, administrative records, organizational costs, and failure responsibility. This is also the most valuable part of Taiwan's internet politics: not that it looks new, but that after being watched, it still has to live in the same city.

He left more questions than answers. When creators enter the institution, whether the audience will follow, still depends on each specific event, not a beautiful identity label.

This uncertainty is not a defect of the story, but the condition of democratic life itself.
