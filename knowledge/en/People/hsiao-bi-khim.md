# Bi-khim Hsiao

## January 20, 2021, the United States Capitol

On the morning of January 20, 2021, on the west steps of the United States Capitol. Joe Biden was about to be sworn in as the 46th President of the United States. In the VIP section of the ceremony sat a woman with an Asian face — 49 years old, short hair, wearing a dark suit.

Her name was printed on the nameplate pinned to her chest: **Bi-khim Hsiao**. Title: **Taiwan's Representative to the United States** (Representative of the Taipei Economic and Cultural Representative Office).

This seat was not a courtesy. She had been **formally invited by name** by the **Joint Congressional Committee on Inaugural Ceremonies** of the U.S. Congress.[^1] This was the first time since the U.S. derecognized the Republic of China in 1979 that a Taiwanese official representative to the United States had attended a U.S. presidential inauguration as a formally invited guest.

Forty-two years of diplomatic deep freeze melted a small corner in that invited seat.

The person who made this happen was a woman born in Japan, schooled in Tainan, raised in New Jersey, and graduated from Oberlin. Nothing in her background fits the standard résumé of a Taiwanese politician: she was not a first-generation member of any provincial group, nor a scion of a distinguished Taipei family; she was not a democracy fighter imprisoned during martial law, nor a second-generation business heir entering politics; she was a child who spent more time on the East Coast of the United States than in Taiwan, and later decided to come back and put this island on Washington's map.

> **30-second overview**: Bi-khim Hsiao was born in Kobe, Japan in 1971. Her father, Hsiao Ching-fen (蕭清芬), was the president of Tainan Theological College and Seminary; her mother, Peggy Cooley (蕭邱碧玉), was a music teacher of North Carolina heritage. Elementary school in Tainan, high school in New Jersey, a B.A. in East Asian Studies from Oberlin College, and a master's degree in political science from Columbia University. She joined the DPP's representative office in the United States in 1995, and in 2000 became a presidential adviser and English secretary. A legislator in the 5th, 6th, 8th, and 9th terms, in 2015 she defeated Wang Ting-sheng with 53.77% in Hualien — where the DPP had never won a single-member district seat — winning office. In 2020 she was appointed **the first female Representative to the United States in Taiwan's history**; during her tenure she secured the formal invitation to Biden's inauguration, Speaker McCarthy's meeting, and the establishment of the U.S.-Taiwan Initiative on 21st Century Trade. In November 2023 she resigned as Representative to run with Lai Ching-te, was elected vice president in January 2024, and took office in May of the same year. Her personal brand is the “Cat Warrior” spirit (戰貓精神).

## Born in Kobe, elementary school in Tainan, high school in New Jersey

To understand the political foundation of Bi-khim Hsiao, you first have to understand her life background.

She was born on August 7, 1971 in Kobe, Japan.[^2] Her father **Hsiao Ching-fen** (蕭清芬) was a Tainan native and a Presbyterian pastor who later earned a doctorate in theology from Princeton Theological Seminary in the United States, and in 1978 returned to Taiwan to serve as president of Tainan Theological College and Seminary (台南神學院).[^3] Her mother **Peggy Cooley** (蕭邱碧玉) was from North Carolina, a European American, and a music teacher.

This was a typical “trans-Pacific Presbyterian family” — the father taught at the seminary, the mother served in church music, and the children grew up in the seminary dormitory. The campus of Tainan Theological College and Seminary sits in the heart of the old prefectural city; in the 1970s, Tainan was a slow-paced city where fluent Hokkien was spoken and seminary students would still go out onto the streets singing hymns.

Hsiao attended the affiliated elementary school of Tainan Normal College (台南師範專科學校附屬小學) and then Houjia Junior High School (後甲國中). Her Hokkien was learned in Tainan; it was not something she crammed through remedial classes. These two stages of schooling added up to about eight years.

The year she finished junior high, her family moved to the United States. She finished high school in New Jersey and entered **Oberlin College** in Ohio — a liberal arts college known for liberalism and the arts — earning her **B.A. in East Asian Studies** in 1993. She then went to **Columbia University** in New York for a master's degree in political science, specializing in international relations.[^4]

This academic background matters. Oberlin is a place that treats “critical thinking” as a religion, and Columbia is one of the cradles of American foreign policy. Trained in both places, Hsiao's later diplomatic style — speaking in the language familiar to American elites while also fitting “the Taiwan question” into conceptual frameworks familiar to American political scientists — was a direct product of these two educations.

From age 16 to age 24, she lived in the United States for eight years. TIME magazine later called her the “Jersey Girl VP” in a profile.[^5] The nickname was a joke, but it pointed at a truth: **this Taiwanese vice president has spent about as much time living in the United States as living in Taiwan.**

## How an American-raised girl became a Taiwanese political figure

In 1995, at age 24, Hsiao earned her master's degree in the United States and joined the Democratic Progressive Party's representative office in America as head of activities.[^6] At the time, the DPP had almost no voice in the United States — after the severing of Taiwan–U.S. ties, Washington was the turf of the Kuomintang government, and the green camp had to rely on a handful of people waging a guerrilla campaign outside the system to be heard in America. Hsiao's English, her Oberlin-plus-Columbia credentials, and her connections within the Presbyterian network made her the DPP's earliest “inside line” in the United States.

In 1996 she was promoted to deputy director of the DPP's International Affairs Department, and in 1997 to director, a post she held until 2006. Over that decade, her job was: **to introduce the DPP to Washington**. When Chen Shui-bian won election in 2000, she joined the Presidential Office as an adviser, also serving as the president's English secretary and interpreter. During that period, virtually every speech Ah-bian gave to the American media went through her translated drafts.

In that decade from 1995 to 2006, Hsiao was in fact doing something one step earlier than being a “politician” — she was building a **“DPP image” that was legible to the United States**. If the DPP wanted Americans to understand “you are not troublemakers, you are representatives of democratic transition,” it depended on people like Hsiao — fluent on both sides — cultivating that understanding over the long term.

In 2001 she began running for the Legislative Yuan, serving as a legislator in the 5th, 6th, 8th, and 9th terms.[^7] She represented the DPP's party-list seats until 2015.

That year, something happened: **she went to Hualien to run for a single-member district seat**.

## The miracle election in Hualien

Hualien County was the DPP's “desert.” Since Taiwan restored single-member district elections for the Legislative Yuan in 1992, the DPP had never won a single-member district seat in Hualien. Local politics was firmly controlled by the “King of Hualien” family (such as Fu Kun-chi) and the Kuomintang's local factions; a green-camp candidate going to Hualien to campaign was basically lambs to the slaughter.

But in 2015, the DPP drafted Hsiao to take on Wang Ting-sheng, the Kuomintang incumbent seeking a third consecutive term. A legislator who had been easily re-elected through the Taipei party-list, she went to Hualien for a head-on contest against an opponent who had worked the ground there for years. Early doubts were plentiful: “parachute candidate,” “someone from Taipei,” “can't she speak Hokkien?” (her Hokkien was excellent, but Hualien residents were used to Mandarin), “not a Hualien native.”

Her response was not verbal; it was action. She set up a service office in Hualien, commuted between Taipei and Hualien every week, and over six years traveled to every township in the county. The issues she pushed were deeply local: Hualien's transportation construction (not national-level defense and diplomacy), agricultural policy (not semiconductor policy), and medical care for rural and remote areas (not AI policy).

In the 2015 election, Hsiao defeated Wang Ting-sheng with **53.77%** of the vote, becoming **the DPP's first single-member district legislator in the history of Hualien County**.[^8] The significance of this victory was bigger than the number — it proved the DPP could win in a Kuomintang stronghold, provided that the candidate was willing to work the local ground with an almost “martyr-like” patience.

Those six years in Hualien also built her personal brand: **the cat that never gives up**. She personally disliked being called a “cat warrior,” but she accepted the “Cat Warrior” spirit (戰貓精神) label — meaning soft, flexible, but never yielding. The brand was later borrowed by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs and the Office of the Vice President, becoming the symbol of her personal style in dealing with the United States and with the world.

## Representative to the United States: putting Taiwan on Washington's map

In July 2020, the Presidential Office announced it was sending Hsiao to serve as Representative to the United States. She became **the first female Representative to the United States in Taiwan's history**.[^9]

The post of Representative to the United States is one of the most important diplomatic positions of the Republic of China (Taiwan). After Taiwan–U.S. ties were severed, the bilateral relationship was maintained through “unofficial” channels — Taiwan's office in the United States is called the Taipei Economic and Cultural Representative Office (TECRO), which in theory is not an embassy and whose head is not an ambassador. But in practice it does an embassy's work, and the Representative does an ambassador's work.

Hsiao's tenure as Representative to the United States (2020–2023) exactly spanned the late Trump administration and the first half of the Biden administration. This was the critical period of America's all-around pivot in its China policy, and the three years in which Taiwan–U.S. relations warmed the fastest. Her main achievements can broadly be divided into three parts:

**First, putting Taiwan into the official ceremonies of the U.S. Congress.** At Biden's inauguration in January 2021, Hsiao was formally invited by name to attend — the first time since ties were severed in 1979. This was an **official invitation from Congress** rather than from the executive branch; its significance lay in crossing the convention of “informal contacts.”

**Second, in 2023, getting the Speaker of the U.S. House of Representatives to formally receive Taiwan's president.** Speaker McCarthy met President Tsai Ing-wen at the Ronald Reagan Presidential Library in California — the first formal meeting between a U.S. congressional leader and a Taiwanese president on U.S. soil since 1979.[^10] The scale, venue, and participants of that meeting were all the result of long-term behind-the-scenes cultivation — Hsiao's team played the core coordinating role.

**Third, establishing the U.S.-Taiwan Initiative on 21st Century Trade.** On the surface this initiative was about trade issues (tariffs, regulation, anti-corruption); in substance, for the first time since ties were severed it **institutionalized** Taiwan–U.S. relations. Institutionalized means: whoever the next president of the United States is, they must inherit this framework. Annual bilateral trade between Taiwan and the United States has already reached about **US$160 billion** (2022 figure),[^11] and this initiative wrapped that economic link into a long-term institution, raising the cost for either side to change the relationship unilaterally.

Taken together, Hsiao's real contribution during her tenure in Washington was not “doing some big things” but **upgrading Taiwan–U.S. relations from “personal rapport” to “structure.”** Rapport can change; structure is hard to change.

## 2024: coming back from Washington to sit in the vice president's seat

On November 20, 2023, Hsiao resigned as Representative to the United States at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs. The same day, DPP presidential candidate Lai Ching-te announced her as his running mate for the vice presidency.[^12]

This pairing had an obvious logic: Lai Ching-te came from domestic politics (doctor, legislator, mayor of Tainan, premier), and international diplomacy was not his strong suit; Hsiao came from diplomacy, with domestic electoral experience honed over six years in Hualien. Together they formed a combination of “domestic policy expert + diplomatic warrior,” each filling the other's weakness.

Voting took place on January 13, 2024, and the Lai–Hsiao ticket won with 40.05% of the vote — lower than the DPP's two previous presidential victories (Tsai Ing-wen's roughly 56% in 2016 and roughly 57% in 2020), but still first in a three-candidate race. The Kuomintang's Hou Yu-ih + Jaw Shau-kong ticket took 33.49%, and the Taiwan People's Party's Ko Wen-je + Cynthia Wu ticket took 26.46%.[^13]

In her first year in office (2024–2025), Hsiao sat in an awkward position: she was “the person best at speaking to the United States,” but under the ROC Constitution the vice president has little involvement in the practical work of diplomacy — that is the president's authority, with the minister of foreign affairs responsible at the executive level. In theory, the vice president's role is “successor to the head of state.”

But she was assigned several concrete tasks: **speaking to international media, representing the government in track-two diplomacy, and keeping the government's external messaging consistent**. Her main battlefield remained the international stage, just shifted from Washington to international conferences and visit diplomacy.

After the Cheng–Xi meeting (鄭習會) incident in April 2026, when reporters pressed Hsiao during her international visits, her response was: “No meeting can bypass Taiwan's democratically elected government. Real dialogue has to be two-way, not one-way.” [^14] The statement overlapped with the content of the statement that AIT issued at the time, and it matched her usual style — not fierce, but unambiguous.

## Conclusion: can a person with many layered identities represent Taiwan?

Some people will ask: how can someone who left Taiwan after age 16, went to high school in New Jersey, and soaked in the American political system for eight years represent Taiwan? Is she “Taiwanese enough”?

This question can be answered in two ways.

The first answer is technical: the 2024 election result proved that Taiwanese voters think she is enough. The Lai–Hsiao ticket won with 40.05%, with no obvious “vice president dragging the ticket down” effect. The collective judgment made by Taiwanese voters was that this person can represent this island.

The second answer is philosophical: **“Taiwaneseness” is not defined by geography or bloodline**. A person born in Kobe, schooled in Tainan, high schooled in New Jersey, graduated from Oberlin, holding a Columbia master's degree, and working in Washington for a decade — if everything she did for thirty years was intended to put the word “Taiwan” in a more visible place on the international map, then she is Taiwanese — not because she stayed in Taiwan long enough, but because she proved with her actions that she belongs to this island's future.

This is a very contemporary definition of “identity.” It is not the continuation of a bloodline, not the constraint of a region; it is the **accumulation of choices**. Every choice to give time for Taiwan, to bear risk for Taiwan, to build relationships for Taiwan — when these choices are added together, they constitute a person's nationality.

Hsiao is a demonstration of this definition. Her layered identities — born in Kobe, Japan; a Tainan seminary family; a New Jersey high school; top American universities; a decade in Washington; six years in Hualien; one term as vice president — in the ordinary political world would be a messy résumé, but in Hsiao it became a new prototype of the Taiwanese person.

If someone asks a decade from now: what can a Taiwanese person look like? The answer might not be “ancestors who were local Taiwanese (本省) for three or more generations” or “ancestors who were second-generation veterans” or “ancestral roots in Fujian or Guangdong” — the answer might be “look at what she has done for this island over thirty years.”

This is not a comfortable answer. It strips identity away from bloodline and places it back onto **action**. Action cannot be inherited across generations; it can only be chosen, again and again.

But this may be the answer that Taiwan's future must get used to. Because in the coming decades, this island will become more and more internationalized and more and more in need of explaining itself to the outside world. Who will be able to represent Taiwan then? It will not be the person of the purest blood; it will be **the person who can explain Taiwan most clearly in the other side's language**. Hsiao is only the first.
