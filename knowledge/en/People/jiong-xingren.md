> **30-second overview**: Jiong Xingren did not become one of Taiwan's early knowledge-based YouTubers by first learning to please the audience. She brought over a decade of film criticism into the camera lens, then used the fake mustache she originally wore to hide her braces to establish a storytelling position that did not dress her up as an authority. Her channel proved Taiwan audiences are willing to pay for content, while simultaneously exposing the difficulties subscription models pose for creative autonomy.

In 2013, Yu Yue moved her years-long habit of writing film reviews to YouTube. She did not start from a fully equipped production company, but first tried to turn her own ways of watching films, reading books, and organizing materials into a voice others could watch. By 2014, her review of _Frozen_ approached one million views, and the fake mustache turned from a temporary prop for hiding braces into the trademark audiences remembered her by.[^1]

The most counterintuitive part of this story is not "a person who reads a lot became an influencer." What is truly worth noting is that she did not package herself as a lofty teacher. She said she was not doing book reviews, but "storytelling" (_shuoshu_), and wanted to lower her stance to become a friend to fellow book lovers.[^2]

📝 **Curator's note:** Influencer stories are easily written as rising traffic curves. Jiong Xingren's curve resembles more a road that repeatedly changes course: blog, film criticism, book reviews, subscription crowdfunding, then circling back to ask what content she is truly willing to do long-term.

## The Fake Mustache Is Not a Persona, It Is an Entry Point

Before Jiong Xingren started filming, she had already written film reviews for over a decade. This textual work familiarized her with how to describe a work's strengths and weaknesses, and taught her that criticism is not about shouting one's preferences the loudest, but about organizing the viewing experience into words others can catch. After 2013, she swapped this organizing work for spoken commentary, gradually building her channel.[^2]

Her original reason for wearing the fake mustache was tiny: to hide her braces. But once a small prop is remembered by the audience, it ceases to be mere concealment and becomes a fixed entry point. Black-rimmed glasses, neutral dress, low voice, and fake mustache gave her a recognizability that did not rely on lavish sets, in an era when Taiwan's YouTube had yet to form a clear professional imaginary.[^1]

A career article republished by _Kan Zazhi_ (《看雜誌》) traces that she began running a blog in 2005, once subscribed to hundreds of sites via RSS, read nearly a thousand articles daily, and translated or organized new knowledge from English and Japanese sites into text. After she began sharing commentary via video in 2014, her first video had accumulated over 5,000 views before the second went live; three months later the channel surpassed 10,000 subscribers.[^3]

These figures cannot be read as "just read lots of articles every day and you'll succeed." They are more like a work log of early online creation: when the platform lacked mature division of labor, the creator had to be researcher, editor, host, editor, and own publisher all at once.

## Reading Precedes Traffic

When Jiong Xingren speaks of reading, she speaks of childhood. Around age four she began listening to story cassette tapes; her mother would finish a newspaper page and hand it to her to continue reading. Literacy and reading thus became bound up with memories of mother-daughter time. _Parenting_ (《親子天下》) wrote this experience as the inner starting point of her later storytelling, not merely a success background.[^4]

She later launched _Jiong Storytelling_ (《囧說書》), each episode introducing one or a few books in about ten minutes. She said that from selecting books to editing could take over thirty hours; she might read five books but ultimately introduce only the one she truly liked. This ratio matters, because it shows storytelling is not moving titles and golden quotes onto the screen, but first making selections, then turning the reasons for those selections into content.[^1]

Her channel once included _Jiong Film Review_ (《囧星人影評》), _Jiong Knowledge_ (《囧知識》), _Jiong Storytelling_ (《囧說書》), _Jiong Star Q&A_ (《囧星 Q&A》), and _Jiong Star Life_ (《囧星生活》) among other segments. Profitable content was often used to support more niche topics that might not immediately recoup costs. This arrangement made her channel less like a shop producing a single commodity and more like a magazine sustained by multiple columns together.[^2]

She also did not position herself as a mentor. _Women's Fan_ (《女人迷》) recorded her saying she had studied China's knowledge programs and knew the speaker must believe in themselves to make the audience believe the content, yet she still wanted to be the audience's friend.[^2]

📝 **Curator's note:** "Friend" is not a lighter authority, but another kind of responsibility. A friend can explain complex things in plain speech, but cannot pretend to hold all answers. Part of Jiong Xingren's credibility begins precisely from this self-limitation.

## From Film Criticism to Storytelling, the Medium Changed, Not the Curiosity

_Business Weekly_ (《今周刊》) described her as someone who went from publishing house section chief and office worker to full-time self-media creator. Her early YouTube ad revenue was only about NT$3,000 per month; later she increased posting frequency, and ad revenue sharing, sponsorships, and column income combined gradually approached the level of a regular office worker. These figures remind us that a channel being seen and a channel being able to support a person have never been the same thing.[^1]

She did not suddenly switch to book reviews because film criticism stopped working. Between film criticism, book reviews, and social issue videos there was always a shared method: taking materials originally scattered across works, news, or books and reordering them into a path the audience can follow. Her work is not to digest all complexity for the audience, but to first demonstrate how to approach complexity.

In 2017, the Taipei City Employment Service Office published a _Kan Zazhi_ article recording her views on video production. She noted that articles can accommodate complex information, but video is affected by voice, expression, pace, and length. She even made low-production-value chat videos, proving it is not hard to spike traffic, but that was not the content she wanted to make.[^3]

These words carry a stubbornness that does not quite fit platform logic. She knew traffic can be designed, but refused to equate "can spike" directly with "worth keeping." What she truly wanted to keep were book reviews, film reviews, and works that matched her inner self.

## Knowledge Begins to Be Priced

Starting in 2016, _Jiong Storytelling_ became a public subscription crowdfunding case. _Business Weekly_ recorded that the project launched on PressPlay and raised NT$200,000 per month within four hours, later exceeding NT$400,000 per month. The Taipei City Employment Service Office's republished article recorded the subscription amount later exceeding NT$430,000 per month, with over 1,600 subscribers.[^1] [^3]

These amounts were not her monthly salary, nor did every yuan go directly to living expenses. The subscription plan had platform fees, fulfillment goods, production costs, and fulfillment time. _Business Weekly_ also noted that publishers provided book lists and sponsorships of several thousand yuan per episode, leading some to question whether this counted as sponsored content. Jiong Xingren stated the program's purpose was promoting reading, and book selection remained based on what she genuinely liked.[^1]

Here appeared a key shift in Taiwan's early creator economy. Audiences not only supported content by watching, but began using subscription amounts to say "I want this program to continue existing." This money did not buy a book, but bought the creator's continued time for reading, organizing, and explaining.

Yet payment brings commitment. Once a creator accepts subscriptions, they must consider whether content is open to the public or specially produced for a group of patrons. A 2018 personal analysis discussing subscription crowdfunding pointed out that the core of Jiong Xingren stopping the subscription model was not necessarily project failure, but possibly her discovery that subscription commitments gradually restricted the flexibility of other creation. This article explicitly labels its content as the author's personal reasoning, so it can only serve as external observation, not as Jiong Xingren's own words.[^5]

📝 **Curator's note:** "Taiwan starts paying for knowledge" is not a sentence of only joy. Payment lets creators survive, but also demands they deliver more. Jiong Xingren's significance lies in leaving this contradiction on the table, rather than only showing screenshots of crowdfunding success.

## How Family Confession Became Public Language

Jiong Xingren's most discussed content is not all about books and films. After the Cheng Chieh incident in 2014, she produced _Not Funny Today, Let's Talk About Love_, speaking out about her own experiences of parental divorce, family relocation, and growing up in loneliness. _The Reporter_ (《報導者》) recorded that this video garnered massive response, and let many viewers bring their own family issues into comments and replies.[^6]

_The Reporter_'s interview wrote that at age seven she moved to Shenzhen with her father, and afterward moved between different cities and families. By age 31 she had moved over 20 times. She described herself as an outsider wherever she was; this experience of having no stable belonging later became her vocabulary for understanding loneliness, family, and gender temperament.[^6]

These contents belong to her self-narration in interviews and works; they cannot be used by external authors to diagnose her, nor can family trauma be directly inferred as creative talent. A more reliable reading is: she chose to organize private experience into public language, giving the audience a chance to discuss things originally hard to speak aloud.

_CommonWealth Magazine_ (《天下雜誌》) published a _Women's Fan_ interview recording her saying: "What I was raised to be, I could not decide. But what kind of person I want to be, I can." This sentence is not a conclusion of completed healing, but a declaration of slowly reclaiming agency over her life.[^7]

Her family story is therefore not merely material for "suffering creates success." Writing it that way would instead erase her long-term labor of reading, writing, researching, filming, and communicating with audiences. Suffering can explain why she cares about empathy, but not why she can sustain content output.

## "Success" Cannot Be Calculated Only by Income

Another _Women's Fan_ piece written by Jiong Xingren herself discusses the influencer path. She pointed out that online creators, like other workers, must first provide value to the market, and rely on long-term accumulation to be seen. She also reminded that many videos under five minutes may take over ten hours to produce; when there is no traffic, creative output is likely just given to audiences for free.[^8]

She describes passion in a very unromantic way: what you love is what you are still willing to do even carrying a fatigued body, without pay. This is not encouraging endless free labor, but pointing out that those who truly survive early creation usually have an internal reason not wholly determined by traffic.[^8]

She also does not define success as making the most money. In the _Women's Fan_ interview, she said she is closer to "doing what she likes," but still admits she is far from the ideal state of maintaining harmonious relationships with those around her. This tone of not rushing to package herself as a success lets her story retain its unfinished parts.[^2]

A later self-description page describes her as a creator simultaneously running YouTube, Facebook, Instagram, Podcast, and newsletter, and reviews the _Jiong Storytelling_ subscription crowdfunding and long-term career transition. Such first-party pages are suitable for understanding how she describes herself, but their subscriber counts, income, and career achievements should still be cross-read with media reports.[^9]

## From Lens Back to Text

Eslite Online's publishing data shows Yu Yue withdrew from the internet and public view in 2019, and when she restarted in 2023, she chose text rather than video as her entry point. She organized this turn into _Boundless Textual Power_ (《無界文字力》), discussing writing, platform revenue sharing, subscription models, and content monetization. This later direction connects back to her early blog experience: when video is no longer the only work form, text again becomes the common substrate for research, thinking, and income.[^9]

Her personal About page lists career experience from advertising art editor, social media planning, publishing, website engineer, to film review columnist, showing "Jiong Xingren" was never just a role in front of the camera. She also continues to run a Podcast and newsletter, placing books, society, and market views back into longer textual contexts.[^9]

This turn deserves to be added back into her early influencer story. If one only watches the YouTube period, Jiong Xingren looks like a linear figure from film criticism to storytelling. Adding the later text work reveals she has actually been testing the same question all along: how to organize complex knowledge into her own language, then find people willing to read or listen long-term.

## The Questions Left by a Tuft of Mustache

What Jiong Xingren represents is not just a name among Taiwan's early YouTubers. She brought the long-form reading of the blog era into YouTube's short-video rhythm, then turned audience viewing into subscription support. She did not turn knowledge into a cold handout, but let film criticism, book reviews, family confession, and life chat share the same voice.

This voice is sometimes sharp, sometimes fatigued, sometimes uncertain. It does not fit the imagination that "influencers must always be positive energy," yet precisely because of that it lets audiences see the creator also groping: how to talk about books without pretending to understand, how to take sponsorships without letting content lose meaning, how to accept patronage without locking oneself into promises.

As for the later licensing and platform controversies she was involved in, SET News (三立新聞網) once reported an article appearing on a China platform with the phrase "from China Taiwan"; Jiong Xingren clarified that the article was issued by the MCN company handling work licensing, without discussion with her before publication. This incident is suitable to be understood as creators potentially losing control over textual context when handing works to platforms or companies. The netizen doxxing and family speculation in the report should not be treated as factual basis for this article.[^10]

📝 **Curator's note:** If one only looks at her crowdfunding amounts, Jiong Xingren looks like a successful business case. If one only looks at her family story, she gets reduced to a wounded person. Putting both back into the creative workflow reveals the true Taiwan characteristic: how a person, between blog, YouTube, publishers, crowdfunding platforms, and audiences, gradually gropes for "what I can live on, and what I don't want to lose."

Her fake mustache ultimately was no longer just a prop to hide braces. It reminds people that online recognizability can start from a very small gap. What truly remains, however, is not the mustache itself, but her willingness to keep handing her reading, questions, and incomplete answers to the audience for examination.
