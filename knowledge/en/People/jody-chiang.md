In 1971, in the Beitou district of Taipei, the air was always permeated with an inescapable scent of sulfur. Ten-year-old Chiang Shu-hui (later known as Jody Chiang) was navigating between the brightly lit tea houses and hotels; to help her family repay her father's debts, she earned only 10 TWD per song. At that time, she had no idea that over the next 40 years, she would become the most awarded and influential diva in the history of Taiwanese music.

### From "Nakasi" to "Million-Selling Diva"

Jody Chiang's performing career began at the lowest level of _Nakasi_ (traditional tea house) busking. In that era, Tâi-gí songs were often labeled as "melancholic," "vulgar," or "working-class." Her early works, such as "The Parting Coast" (_Xībié de hǎi'àn_), though showcasing her clear and penetrating vocals, had not yet escaped the sorrowful tropes of traditional Tâi-gí music.

However, the 1992 release of the album _Voices After Drinking_ (_Jiǔ hòu de xīnshēng_) changed everything. This album sold over 1.16 million copies in Taiwan; if you were to stack the cassette tapes from that time, the height would equal 3ax 34 Taipei 101 skyscrapers. It not only broke the sales records for Tâi-gí albums but, more importantly, it brought Tâi-gí songs into middle-class living rooms and the high-end car stereos of the era.

The success of this album was no accident. Producers Tsao Chun-hung and Chen Chin-hsing injected modern pop arrangement elements into Tâi-gí songs, making the originally heavy musical style urban and fashionable. Chiang's singing style also shifted from the "crying tone" of her early years toward a more restrained and elegant interpretation. This musical revolution ensured that Tâi-gí songs were no longer just synonyms for "tragic romance," but a form of pop culture possessing true artistic value.

📝 Curator's Note: Jody Chiang's success is, in essence, a microcosm of social mobility in Taiwan. She allowed a language that originally belonged to the "underground" or the "periphery" to reclaim its cultural agency.

### The Mystery of her "Retirement" at Her Peak

In 2015, at the height of her career, Jody Chiang announced her "Blessing" farewell concert and officially retired from the industry. At the time, the public speculated that she was stepping away to leave at her most beautiful moment, or perhaps due to long-term suffering from vertigo. It was not until 2024, when she announced her comeback for a National Day performance, that a handwritten statement revealed the truth hidden for nine years: she had been confirmed to have cancer on the eve of her 2015 concert.

This war was lonely and cruel. During her nine years of retirement, Chiang underwent multiple surgeries and repeated rounds of chemotherapy; she even faced life-threatening moments involving pulmonary embolisms and massive hemorrhaging. Most painful for her was the damage to her voice caused by chemotherapy—an undoubtedly heavier blow than death for a singer who viewed singing as her very life.

In her statement, she mentioned that at the time, even basic speaking was difficult, let alone singing. Yet, she never disclosed a single word to the public, silently traveling between hospitals and wards, enduring the dual torment of body and soul alone. This "silent fortitude" is perhaps the most representative aspect of Jody Chiang's character.

📝 Curator's Note: The diva who chose to "retire" at the peak of her career was hiding not a weariness of the stage, but a nine-year secret war against cancer that nearly took both her voice and her life.

### The Resonance of Her Voice and Her Return

The reason Jody Chiang's voice can transcend generations is that she sings the "collective emotions" of the Taiwanese people. From the early period of tragedy to the mid-period urbanity, and finally to the later warmth and transcendence, her voice has evolved alongside the transformations of Taiwanese society. Her song "After Home" (_Jiā hòu_) captures the devotion of countless spouses, while "Sound of Rain" (_Luò yǔ shēng_) evokes the nostalgia of those living far from home.

In Taiwan's KTV lounges, Jody Chiang's songs remain among the most requested. Whether it is the political and business elite or the common folk, everyone finds resonance in her voice. This "Jody Chiang phenomenon" is even viewed by sociologists as a form of cultural cohesion; in a Taiwanese society marked by intense Blue-Green political polarization, a Jody Chiang concert is one of the few occasions where people of different political stances can sit together and weep collectively.

On October 5, 2024, Jody Chiang stepped back onto the stage during the National Day gala at the Taipei Dome. Despite her battle with serious illness, her voice remained as gentle as ever. This return was no longer about proving her status, but rather as she stated in her announcement: "Singing for my fans, and singing for myself."

📝 Curator's Note: Jody Chiang's return proves that a true diva does not need grand packaging; as long as she opens her mouth, the shared memory belonging to the Taiwanese people will be awakened.

### Challenges and Controversies: The Gap in Tâi-gí Music

Although Jody Chiang set an almost insurmountable benchmark, she has also expressed concerns about the future of Tâi-gí music. After winning numerous Golden Melody Awards, she proactively announced that she would no longer compete for individual awards, hoping to leave opportunities for newcomers. However, since her retirement, the Tâi-gí music scene has indeed faced challenges of market shrinkage and a talent gap.

Modern young listeners are turning more toward Mandarin or Western pop music, and the transmission of Tâi-gí songs faces a crisis of language loss. Although a new generation of Tâi-gí bands such as "Baihehua" (Lily) and "茄子蛋" (Eggplant) have risen in recent years, there is still a long way to go to reach the level of "universal resonance" achieved by Jody Chiang. How to allow Tâi-gí songs to continue evolving within modern pop music, rather than merely remaining stuck in the glory of the "Jody Chiang era," is the most severe challenge facing Taiwan's current music industry.

Jody Chiang's story begins with the sulfur mists of Beitou and ends with the applause at the Taipei Dome. She used her life to interpret what it means to "sing with one's life," and allowed the world to hear the gentlest, yet most resilient, voice of Taiwan.
