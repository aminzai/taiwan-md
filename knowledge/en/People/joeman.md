# Joeman: From Esports Commentator to Million-Subscriber Channel—A Long-Term Creator in the Lens

> **30-Second Introduction:** Joeman (Weng Chun-ming), initially sought his place between esports commentary and working at Micron, before pivoting to YouTube after an unexpectedly viral unboxing video. His story, from breaking one million subscribers in 2018 to being invited to Apple Park in 2025, is a narrative of how Taiwanese creators turn personal interests into teams, shows, and public communication capabilities.[^1] [^2] [^3]

![Portrait of Joeman sitting at an indoor desk](/article-images/people/joeman-portrait-desk-2017.webp)

In 2012, Weng Chun-ming, then 25, would return to his dormitory at one or two in the morning after weekend events, asking himself in the mirror: "Why am I doing this?" During that time, he worked as a product manager at Micron while also serving as an esports commentator for the Taiwan Esports League. Both jobs related to games he loved, yet they gradually turned his passion into stress.[^1]

The official channel page showed approximately 2.88 million subscribers and 3,600 videos in August 2026. This is a public snapshot that changes over time and cannot be treated as a fixed resume.[^4] Starting with 3C unboxings, Joeman's content gradually expanded to include comparisons of affordable versus luxury items, travel, real estate, interviews, cars, team management, and anti-drug/anti-fraud charity videos.

This path is worth watching not just as a success story of "how one person became famous," but for answering another, more difficult question: **After an audience has come to know a creator, can they still leave the most successful format?**

## Before Games Became Work: Commentary, Product Management, and the First Let Go

Joeman's public career did not start at a review desk. An ETtoday report in 2012 stated that he had already graduated from National Taipei University of Technology, working as a product manager at Micron while simultaneously serving as an esports commentator. He decided to quit commentary because juggling two jobs prevented him from spending time with his family, and the game no longer held the initial enjoyment after becoming work.[^1]

He once wrote: "This decision seemed impulsive, but I actually thought about it for a long time." The weight of this statement lies in the fact that it was not an inspirational postscript written by someone who later succeeded. It was a judgment made about career fatigue before he became a million-subscriber YouTuber.

He also said: "Facts proved I overestimated myself." Switching between his full-time job and commentary did not provide the flexibility he imagined. For someone just leaving school and trying to turn a hobby into a profession, the issue was not just time management; it was that every choice redefined the interest: is it still a game? Or is it a job that must be performed, judged, and scrutinized by an audience?[^1]

## The Accidental Turn of Unboxing Rewrote His Content Direction

An interview with _Yuanjian Magazine_ in 2019 provided another turning point detail: Joeman originally intended to pursue esports commentary and live streaming, but he unexpectedly gained popularity from a casually shot 3C unboxing video, leading him to seriously operate on YouTube. The report cited his observation that two years of traffic on Twitch was less than two weeks on YouTube, so he abandoned game streaming for more types of videos.[^2]

The contrast here is clear. He did not first decide "I am an unboxing creator" before starting to film unboxings. Rather, the platform feedback pointed him in a direction, and then he decided whether or not to walk into it. A creator's direction is sometimes formed not from self-introduction, but after receiving audience responses to certain content.

At the end of July 2018, Joeman reached one million subscribers. _Yuanjian_ summarized his content at the time as interviews, unboxings, and travel. This combination already showed that he had not locked his channel into hardware specifications; instead, he extended "I test it for you" to "I explore an unfamiliar scene for you."[^2]

He later said in an interview: "The 'common' things we do are calculated by the algorithm." This statement was not merely a complaint about the platform but an acknowledgment that content creation must constantly adjust between expression and distribution. What the audience wants, what the algorithm promotes, and what the creator is willing to do—these three elements do not naturally overlap; they can only be approached through repeated trial and error.

## Turning "Expensive vs. Cheap" into a Taiwanese Question

Joeman's most recognizable show format places two price points in the same question: what is the final difference between high cost and low cost? The reason _Joe vs. Joe_ is easily understood is not just because it puts numbers in the title, but because it allows the audience to project their own life experiences onto it.

When watching an episode of a high-cost versus affordable cooking competition, the audience may not actually intend to buy either item. What they want to know is how service, ingredients, environment, and sense of status are packaged by price. This question can be applied to teppanyaki, hotels, houses, cars, and travel plans.

Therefore, while Joeman's shows superficially compare products, at the core, they compare lifestyles. It places "what is so good about things I cannot afford" alongside "can cheap options maintain dignity." This format suits Taiwanese audiences well because Taiwan's consumption culture values meticulous budgeting while also maintaining curiosity about expensive things.

However, the more successful the format, the easier it is for the creator to be defined by the format in reverse. When the audience expects every episode to be grander, bigger, or more exaggerated, the show risks becoming merely a specification competition. Joeman later added documentary content, long-form travel, real estate, and team content, which shifted the channel from "which of two things is better" to "how one person or an industry is seen."

## From Solo Filming to Studio Operation

A 2020 interview with _UDN Tech Player_ documented the stage where Joeman's channel transitioned from solo production to multi-person collaboration. The team at that time included a photographer, editor, 3C editor, and agent. Different members handled imaging, planning, products, client communication, and post-production.[^3]

A significant self-assessment in this interview was: "I dreamed of being an internet celebrity for ten years; many people think the profession of an influencer can only be short-term, which is partly true but not entirely true. If I continuously correct my direction and develop new content, I believe a YouTuber can last a lifetime!"[^3]

This statement redefines "internet celebrity" from a short-term traffic status to a profession requiring continuous refinement. The key is not maintaining the same persona forever, but continuously developing new content so that the audience has a reason to accept the next version of oneself.

In the team workflow, Joeman usually conceived the plan, and members would discuss and execute it together. After filming was complete, the footage was handed over for editing and post-production, and Joeman would review about two or three times before uploading. Commercial collaborations were not just shooting after receiving a product; they required selecting themes, scripts, visuals, client verification, shooting, and editing—a commercial project usually took about a month.[^3]

These details show that the channel's "natural feel" is actually built on extensive processes. What the audience sees is one person speaking to the camera; behind the scenes, there are multiple discussions, version revisions, and negotiations with the industry. At a certain scale, a YouTube personal brand must rely on a team to maintain its immediacy.

Joeman also stated his channel's evaluation principle very directly: "What it tests is what it is." When clients were dissatisfied with the final result, the team sometimes chose to provide the original master file, allowing viewers to see the actual test results. This does not mean that commercial collaborations can completely exclude vested interests; rather, if the testing content loses verifiability, the channel's most important asset—trust—will disappear.[^3]

## From Product Unboxing to Apple Park: The Moment Creators Are Seen by Industry

The content currently visible on the official channel includes long-form travel, member-exclusive campus lectures, creative reflections, studio daily life, and extensive unboxings. Taking _Traveling Solo in Japan_ as an example, the video centered around a self-driving trip of over 4,000 kilometers, from the southernmost tip of Japan all the way to Hokkaido.[^4]

Unboxing and travel require two different viewing tolerances. Unboxing breaks down products into specifications, usage scenarios, and purchasing judgments. Long-distance travel retains time, fatigue, routes, and accidents. When Joeman moved from a phone on a desk to the roads of Japan, the channel's content shifted from "I test it for you" to "I accompany you."

In September 2025, NOWnews reported that Joeman was invited with other Taiwanese creators to attend an event related to the Apple iPhone 17 and quickly released unboxing videos after the launch event. The report also compiled netizens' observations on his image evolution from iPhone X to iPhone 17, making his "eight-year unboxing history" a history of both phone generations and creator physical changes.[^9]

The English media outlet _8days_ in Singapore also reported on him previewing the iPhone 17 at Apple Park. This international perspective does not mean his evaluation is necessarily correct, nor can being invited be equated with brand endorsement. What it proves is that Taiwanese tech creators have become recognizable media figures within the global product launch system.[^10]

For Joeman, this position might also respond to the young man working at Micron in 2012. Back then, he wanted to make a first-class laptop. Years later, he stood at the product launch site, becoming the person who brings products back into the Taiwanese context for viewers.[^1]

## The Body Was Also Filmed on the Channel: Creators Are No Longer Just Hosts

Another report from NOWnews in 2025 pointed out that Joeman had publicly stated that his weight was once 103 kilograms, and he lost 31 kilograms within a year through diet control and exercise, subsequently reaching 71 kilograms. He also mentioned being warned about blood sugar risks during a health check, which prompted him to start losing weight.[^11]

The reason this experience became news is that the audience has been watching the same person for a long time. As products change yearly, the host's body, clothing, and way of speaking are also changing. What viewers remember is not just the phone camera, processor, and battery life, but also the person standing in the frame during every unboxing.

This makes the creator's body part of the content, while also being a source of stress. Images can preserve change, but they can also constantly bring back the past self for comparison. For someone who has been on camera for a long time, transformation is not just about changing topics; it is also about re-processing audience expectations regarding one's appearance, life, and age.

## The Cannabis Incident: Apology, Justice, and Subsequent Actions Must Be Separated

On November 6, 2023, Joeman was searched at his residence for suspected possession of cannabis. The next day, he released an apology video on YouTube. At the beginning of the video, he said: "Hello everyone, I am Joeman. This is an apology video." Afterward, he publicly admitted to having used and possessing a small amount of cannabis in Taiwan and abroad.[^5] [^6]

_Key Opinion Network_ analyzed this video as "fast, sincere, and accurate": appearing the day after the incident without lengthy justifications and stating willingness to cooperate with the investigation. This is an evaluation of public communication by media authors, not a legal conclusion from judicial authorities. The two must be clearly separated.[^5]

The subsequent judicial process also requires precise description. A Central News Agency report in 2025 indicated that Joeman was granted one year of suspended sentence for possessing cannabis and required to perform 100 hours of community service; the New Taipei District Prosecutors Office later uploaded three collaborative videos, including an anti-fraud long video, a one-minute anti-fraud version, and a one-minute anti-drug awareness video.[^7]

The Central News Agency also reported that the investigation determined he purchased 10 grams of cannabis in Sanchong, New Taipei; Joeman said in the anti-drug video: "My house can be raided, so why wouldn't the police raid your house?" and used his own experience to call for people not to gamble on buying or selling drugs.[^7]

_United Daily News_ reported that the conditions for suspended sentence included writing a letter of remorse and 100 hours of community service. Joeman voluntarily offered to offset the hours by planning, filming, editing, and post-producing charity videos, with the condition that these videos could not be for profit.[^8] These videos were not merely image restoration content but legal awareness work completed under judicial disposition conditions.

In the anti-fraud long video, Joeman invited a famous investor who was impersonated and fraud victims to appear, also observed scamming language in stock investment communities, and cooperated with police to arrest drivers. This process guided his content production capabilities toward public education; at the same time, the incident itself did not disappear just because the videos were completed. The apology is current communication, suspended sentence is a judicial procedure, and subsequent work is another type of action that needs observation.[^7]

## How Controversy Forms: The Cost of Speed, Trust, and Judgment

Joeman's content operation has also been accompanied by other controversies. Publicly compiled data lists incidents such as the 2017 interview dispute, the 2018 misunderstanding regarding font licensing information, the 2023 discussion of convenience store co-branded products, and the 2025 video removal and apology due to filming controversial figures. Since the encyclopedia page itself notes that more reliable sources are needed, this material should be viewed as an index in research; each incident must still be verified against original reports and statements from the parties involved.[^12]

The commonality among these incidents is not that they all belong to the same type of error, but that they all occurred in an environment characterized by high-speed release, multi-person collaboration, and high audience trust. Creators are expected to react instantly, but instant does not mean skipping verification; audiences like authenticity, but authenticity does not mean being without responsibility.

In the _Yuanjian_ interview, Joeman said: "My interest is to make those who dislike me frustrated." This statement has a provocative quality of the internet age, but it also reveals his survival strategy: turning criticism into motivation for continuous exposure. However, continuous exposure is both an ability and a risk. When every choice is clipped into short videos, turned into headlines, and placed in comment sections, creators must bear not only the success or failure of their work but also the public consequences of their judgment.[^2]

## What He Truly Built Is The Ability to "Walk Through the Scene for the Audience First"

If Joeman were only called a 3C YouTuber, his true capability would be missed. 3C unboxing turns specifications into usage scenarios; the affordable vs. luxury comparison turns price differences into debatable experiences; travel planning turns geographical distance into companionship; team videos turn backstage division of labor into stories; and anti-drug/anti-fraud videos turn a personal judicial experience into public education.

These topics seem scattered, but the core is consistent: **he habitually walks through the scene for the audience first, then edits the path he walked into a viewable, comparable, and debatable version.**

This also explains why his channel grew from one unboxing video into multiple rooms. The product is the entrance; the host is the recognition; the team is the production capacity; and audience trust is the foundation shared by all rooms. The larger the foundation, the more topics it can accommodate; but if the foundation is damaged by a wrong judgment, the repair is much harder than just one video.

## Bell Awards and _Joe vs. Joe_: How Format Became a Representative Work

The official winning video of the Sixth Bell Awards listed Joeman as the channel that won the "Annual Individual Creator Award," with the winning work being "The Real Hotel Is Here! A Luxury Hotel for 150,000 NTD per Night!" from _Joe vs. Joe S2_.[^17]

This award data made _Joe vs. Joe_ not just a format comparing high and low prices, but also the representative work recognized by external creator awards for Joeman. It returns to the question at the beginning of the article: after an audience has come to know someone, can they still leave the most successful format? The answer visible now is not abandoning the format, but making the format a show capable of accommodating characters, guests, settings, and production scale.

This article does not interpret a single award as the absolute ranking of Taiwanese YouTube, nor does it equate winning with all works being recognized; the official video can only directly support three pieces of data: the award name, the winning channel, and the winning work.[^17]

## Conclusion: The Lens Still Asks Questions

In 2012, Weng Chun-ming asked himself in his dormitory at dawn why he made games into work. In 2019, he discussed how algorithms calculate "commonness" and how to disappoint those who dislike him. In 2020, he described a YouTuber as a profession that can last a lifetime, provided there is continuous correction of direction and development of new content. In 2025, he stood at the product site in Apple Park and also stood before the camera for anti-drug/anti-fraud charity videos.[^1] [^2] [^3] [^7]

When these scenes are put together, Joeman's story is no longer just about "a small person becoming a big celebrity." It is more like a continuous negotiation: how interest becomes work, how work becomes a team, how a team maintains authenticity, and how authenticity bears errors.

A video can unbox a phone, but it cannot unbox ten years of a person in ten minutes. What the audience sees with every upload; what truly determines whether the creator can continue is whether he is willing to re-examine what he is bringing into the lens before selecting the next topic.
