# Ku’s Dream: From a Foreigner’s Lens to a Taiwanese Program the World Can Understand

> **30-Second Overview**: Ku (Ku) is a YouTuber born in Nancy, France, who has lived in Taiwan for an extended period. He returned to Taiwan in 2017 and became a full-time creator in 2018. Initially, he filmed Taiwanese food, French high school students, military training, and island-hopping challenges from a foreigner’s perspective. He then expanded the concept of "Chinese that even Taiwanese people can’t understand" into the six-episode reality show _Chinese Monster_ in 2025. The show invited 100 foreigners residing in Taiwan to compete for a NT$300,000 prize, with a production budget of approximately NT$5 million. The first episode surpassed 2 million views within two days. Ku’s story is worth reading not only because he made Taiwan go viral, but because he forces Taiwan to reconsider: When foreigners learn Chinese, are we encouraging them, or are we keeping them permanently in the position of guests? [^1] [^2] [^3] [^15]

## Look at the Person First, Then the Channel

In September 2025, one hundred foreigners living in Taiwan stood before cameras, waiting to answer a question that seemed simple but was actually difficult: Who has the best Chinese? The championship prize was NT$300,000, and the show was titled _Chinese Monster_. [^1]

This was not a final presentation for a language cram school. There was elimination, competition, and a question posed by a French host: When someone has already worked hard to speak your language, do we treat them as a guest, a performer, or a true member of this society?

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> This article does not label Ku merely as "a foreigner introducing Taiwan," but tracks how he turned an external perspective into a production methodology. It also retains an unavoidable question: When a personal channel grows into a team, must the creator also accept a higher level of public scrutiny?

"I hope to create an international-level program for Taiwan, so that people all over the world will want to watch," Ku said in an interview. [^2] This sentence sounds like a creator’s ambition, but it also serves as an exam paper for Taiwan’s film and television industry: We have filmed so much about ourselves; is there any single work that would make someone who doesn’t understand Chinese willing to sit down and watch it to the end?

## One Name, Three Moves

Ku was born in Nancy, France, and his birth name is Richard Quentin Paul Camille. During university, he interned at a hotel in Taipei for three months, later going to South Korea for a two-and-a-half-year exchange. He returned to Taiwan in 2017 and decided to become a full-time YouTuber the following year. [^4] The name he used in Korea was Ku, and he later incorporated the pronunciation of the Korean word for "dream" (_kkum_) into his Chinese name, creating "Ku’s Dream." [^2]

His first channel positioning was convoluted: a Frenchman living in Taiwan filming in Korean to introduce Taiwan to Koreans. This setup was not easy, but it precisely explained Ku’s early vantage point.

Later, he gradually shifted his audience from Korea to Western viewers with whom he shared a greater cultural distance. The channel moved from personal observation and vlogs to life challenges, cultural exchange, and finally to large-scale planned programs. [^3] This path did not have a single, completed positioning statement; rather, it resembled a person constantly filming, correcting, and finding the next question from audience feedback.

## Taiwan Emerges First Through Food

Ku’s earliest and most memorable work was the "Frenchman Tries Taiwanese Food for the First Time" series. Hot pots, night markets, snacks, and taro—things that Taiwanese people are so familiar with they need no explanation—suddenly needed to be translated through the lens. Audiences waited to see foreigners frown or nod, but were also waiting for an answer: What exactly about these foods is worth our such intense attention? [^5]

Such videos are easily categorized as "foreigners surprised by Taiwan." But what is more interesting about Ku’s work is that he gradually turned surprise into a planned project, rather than just a reaction. The "French High School Students Visit Taiwan" series turned cultural experience into a long-term story. The military challenge placed foreigners’ physical fitness alongside the military service memories of Taiwanese men. The island-hopping series deliberately sought out relatively obscure, absurd, or even unsuitable places for tourism promotion. [^5]

He once traveled across Taiwan for filming, hitchhiking from one rest stop to the next. The core of this action was not the summary statement "Taiwanese people are enthusiastic," but the repeated act of asking strangers for a ride. As the camera followed the car, the friendliness of Taiwanese people shifted from an adjective to a concrete scene: someone stopped, someone was willing to take a detour, someone delivered a foreigner to the entrance of the next leg of their journey. [^2]

Ku particularly likes taro; this preference later became a personal image, a convenience store co-branded product, and a one-day taro shop. [^8] The narrower the topic, the more concrete Taiwan becomes. When the lens no longer attempts to represent the entire island, the differences in localities, tastes, and people have the opportunity to emerge.

## From Cultural Observation to Content Methodology

The precursor to _Chinese Monster_ was "Chinese That Even Taiwanese People Can’t Understand" and Chinese listening challenges. Ku invited guests to listen to Taiwanese advertisements, recognize tones and semantics, turning the frustrations most commonly encountered by foreigners learning Chinese into the game rules of the program. [^2] [^5]

This shift is critical. The food series relied on the reaction of "first-time eating." The Chinese series, however, placed differences into a set of repeatable rules.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> If "foreigners looking at Taiwan" stops at surprise, the content quickly becomes a fixed format. Ku’s true turning point was transforming surprise into rules, forcing Taiwanese audiences to also re-understand their own language. Who understands the advertisements? Who can distinguish the tones? Who can understand sentences where Taiwanese people swallow words but still believe they are speaking clearly? Taiwanese daily language suddenly became an obstacle that everyone had to face.

In February 2025, RTI English reported on the competition in preparation for _Chinese Monster_, noting that the event would challenge 100 foreign participants in Chinese, with a NT$300,000 prize for the champion. Ku hoped participants would connect more deeply with Taiwanese language and culture, and that Taiwanese people would view their own culture from a new perspective. [^1]

In an interview, Ku discussed a habit that few Taiwanese notice: as long as the other person looks like a foreigner, we often immediately switch to English, even if the person could actually answer in Chinese. [^2] This usually stems from goodwill, but it may keep someone who has already spent time learning Chinese permanently in the position of "awesome foreigner," unable to become an ordinary person who does not need special care.

Thus, _Chinese Monster_ is superficially looking for "the foreigner with the best Chinese," but deeper down, it asks: Are Taiwanese willing to treat Chinese as a door, rather than a stage where applause is owed as soon as a foreigner says "Hello"? Ku even argues that as Taiwan becomes more open, society’s expectations for foreigners learning Chinese can also be raised. [^2]

## One Hundred People, Five Million Dollars, Seventeen Cameras

_Chinese Monster_ consists of six episodes, with a production budget of approximately NT$5 million. The show utilized 17 cameras and over 30 monitors, designing checkpoints from tones and language sense to listening and on-the-spot reactions, keeping each episode to approximately 40 minutes. [^3] [^9]

_The World Journal_ reported that the first episode surpassed 2 million views within two days of its release, and the channel’s traffic grew by nearly 350%. Ku also admitted that for the team’s first time undertaking such a complex large-scale production, the post-production footage reached 17TB, and the second episode was edited until the very moment before upload. [^9]

The most noteworthy aspect of these numbers is not just "how much money was spent," but that it caused a YouTube channel to begin undertaking work that previously belonged to television stations: production periods, on-site scheduling, participant management, editing, sponsor integration, and how to withstand audience emotions after the program airs.

Ku and two core partners repeatedly discussed, overturned, and restarted the competition system, checkpoints, and program structure. Another interview record notes that 68 staff members and 17 cameras were mobilized on-site. Ku advocated designing the context first, allowing the on-site reactions to grow into the story. [^2] [^3]

In the second checkpoint, participants decided for themselves who would advance; when the captain chose himself, it became an unexpected twist before filming even began. Authenticity here does not mean completely undesignated, but that the design left space for surprise. [^3]

Ku moved directly from filming alone to the position of general director facing dozens of staff members and 100 participants. After the program aired, he admitted that he felt like he had skipped the intermediate practice phase and taken the high-speed rail directly to the terminal station, knowing that the work still had many shortcomings. [^10]

This self-description is more memorable than "comparable to Netflix." Because the difficulty of large-scale production lies not only in setting up the scenes, but in ensuring that every person included in the program can be seen, without being edited into a character convenient for audience consumption.

## Why YouTube Can Film What Television Cannot

After _Chinese Monster_ went viral, the discussion quickly shifted to another question: Why can’t Taiwanese TV stations produce a similar program? Producers and TV executives interviewed by _The World Journal_ reminded us that the audiences, viewing methods, revenue sources, filming, and post-production methods of the two are different; we cannot compare them solely based on visual specifications. [^9]

Ku observed that large French YouTubers already have production companies assisting with planning. In Taiwan, creators often still have to find TV station personnel themselves to form teams. [^3] This indicates that Taiwan’s creator environment is still exploring: personal channels have audiences, speed, and emotional support. But as production costs rise, work that was previously held together by individual will gradually becomes a team, contract, and process issue.

Ku’s judgment on content quality is direct: "If there is only quality but no regard for what audiences want to see, what is the point? And if you only choose traffic but the content is trash, it’s also meaningless." [^3] This sentence breaks down the binary choice often forced upon creators: Content needs to be seen to have a chance to create impact. After being seen, it must still leave something worth remembering.

The original intention of _Chinese Monster_ was not just about the program itself. Ku stated that he hoped to encourage more foreigners to learn Chinese, even coming to Taiwan to practice because of the show. [^9] This adds a layer of cultural educational ambition to the program: It turns Chinese into entertainment, but does not treat those who learn Chinese merely as punchlines.

## Taiwan-France Exchange Is More Than Laughter in Front of the Lens

Ku’s cultural exchange also left the YouTube screen. In 2021, he launched the "Million Dream Project," using prize money to support Taiwanese and French youth in realizing cross-border dreams. Chiu Chen-ting, a student in the French Department at Fu Jen Catholic University, was a recipient from the Taiwanese side. The project was described by the university as part of promoting Taiwan-France cultural exchange. [^11] Ku’s official Facebook video also left public records of the recruitment and explanation of this project at the time. [^12]

This matter shares the same logic as _Chinese Monster_: turning "cultural exchange" from a pretty slogan into a project requiring resources, participants, and follow-up action. The former sends people to another country to complete dreams; the latter concentrates people of different nationalities and Chinese proficiency levels in the same competition arena. Culture is no longer just the background being introduced, but something participants must personally experience.

In 2023, Ku obtained permanent residency in the Republic of China. [^13] This change in status adds weight to his story. He is no longer just "a Frenchman living in Taiwan" as a program setting, but someone who works, lives, builds a team here, and must also be tested by the public rules of this place.

## What Audiences See Is Only a Small Part

After the success of the large-scale program, Ku began to encounter another type of problem that creators cannot solve through editing alone. During the broadcast of the program, some participants suffered from cyberattacks. He admitted in an interview that what audiences see is only a small part of the participants, yet they may treat the edited image as a complete personality. [^2]

He also noted that those attacked more severely were often female participants, pointing out that this is not a phenomenon unique to Taiwan. [^2] When ordinary people are pushed onto a high-traffic stage, the production team cannot simply say "that was the audience’s own choice." How the program is edited, how titles are written, and who is arranged as the center of conflict will all affect how audiences understand a real person.

This is the most worthy point to trace back when Ku himself says "what audiences see is really only a small part." [^2] Creators know that the lens makes choices, but audiences often forget that what they see is the result of those choices. When a program uses the reactions of real ordinary people to create drama, the production team’s responsibility for subsequent impacts increases accordingly.

## The 2026 Controversy: When Dreams Become Employment Relationships

In April 2026, "Ku’s Dream" sparked controversy due to a job posting and public statements from a former employee. United Daily News reported that the controversy involved work content, work boundaries, resignation, and labor/health insurance issues. These contents are allegations and statements from the parties involved compiled by the media and cannot be directly written as facts already recognized by competent authorities or courts. [^6]

On April 8, Ku published an open statement on Threads. He stated that the employee in question worked for about a month and a half; the company had mistakenly used the health insurance declaration form, which was later corrected and retroactively insured. He also denied ever making remarks belittling Taiwanese people, and admitted that the wording of the job posting using the term "glass heart" (sensitive) was inappropriate, apologizing and correcting it. [^7]

Therefore, this incident has at least three layers. First, whether employers handle insurance, working hours, handover, and resignation according to the law should not be solved solely by a creator’s personal charm. Second, when media and audiences relay allegations, they should distinguish between "former employee’s statements," "company responses," and "competent authority determinations." Third, when a channel transforms from personal creation into a team that needs to hire employees, the dream is no longer just the founder’s private ideal.

There is a frequently ignored symmetrical relationship here.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> Cultural exchange is not a liability waiver. It can start with goodwill, but ultimately must return to language equality, labor rules, and the rights of those filmed. Ku in front of the lens can ask Taiwanese people to raise their expectations for Chinese learners; Ku behind the lens must also accept that audiences, employees, and society raise their expectations for creators. The former is the equivalence of cultural exchange; the latter is responsibility within power relations.

## After a Program Leaves YouTube

The lifecycle of _Chinese Monster_ did not stop at the viral success of the first episode. Reports in July 2026 indicated that the six episodes accumulated nearly 20 million views and were also licensed for broadcast on Hami. However, because the premiere platform was not television, the team could not enter the Golden Bell Awards with works that met the qualifications. [^15] This detail illustrates that online programs can already possess the production scale of traditional television, but the system may not be synchronously prepared to receive it.

Ku also did not immediately announce a second season. Reports quoted him as saying that the next season is not impossible, but will not be quick, because the team still needs to accumulate experience in planning, filming, editing, and storytelling. [^15] This choice is worth viewing within the logic of traffic: When a program is successful, the market usually demands faster replication. Creators, however, may need to slow down to organize failures, costs, and responsibilities.

## The Days the Channel Was Hacked

In February 2026, the "Ku’s Dream" channel, with 2.28 million subscribers, was hacked. _Taiwan Times_ reported that Ku clicked a verification link after receiving an invitation that appeared to be a collaboration with MrBeast, after which the account was stolen. With assistance, the channel was recovered on the evening of February 3, and 448 videos were saved. [^16]

This incident transformed "channel" from an abstract brand into a very concrete asset: 448 videos, long-accumulated audiences, algorithm history, and a database of files invested in by a team over many years. For audiences, the channel was just a suddenly disappearing avatar on the homepage. For creators, it was a database of years of work that could be removed within a few hours.

It also fills in a less-seen side of the _Chinese Monster_ story. When people discuss large-scale programs, they often talk about NT$5 million, 17 cameras, and 100 participants, but rarely talk about account permissions, collaboration invitations, cloud files, and backups. When a YouTube channel is already like a small production company, cybersecurity is no longer just a personal usage habit, but the infrastructure of the content industry.

> **📝 Curator’s Note**
> Whether a channel can be seen by the world depends on content; whether a channel can survive the next accident depends on permissions, backups, and systems. Ku’s Dream has moved from "how to film bigger" to "how to preserve what has already been filmed."

## A Foreigner, How to Film a Taiwan That Is Not Only for Foreigners

The most valuable aspect of Ku’s Dream is not just "foreigners see things differently." Foreign freshness wears out; any "first-time eating" reaction can become a predictable format. What truly allowed the channel to go far was that he turned an external perspective into a production methodology: making small topics deeper, placing cultural differences into game rules, and making Taiwanese people also become the ones being questioned.

From a hitchhiking video to a Chinese competition for 100 people, Ku has always been doing the same thing: finding details in Taiwanese life that can be translated, competed over, and re-observed. These details may not be grand, but they can allow someone unfamiliar with Taiwan to first stay because of tones, taro, chicken cutlets, or a strange advertisement, only to finally discover that they are understanding an island.

The dream in "Ku’s Dream" is no longer just about showing Taiwan to foreigners. It is more like a question thrown back at Taiwan: Can we make our language, daily life, and programs good enough so that others are willing to keep watching even without first understanding Taiwan?

This question did not end with the high traffic of _Chinese Monster_. When a Frenchman reminds Taiwan in Chinese that "we can do better," when a YouTube channel begins to possess the scale of a TV program, the dream truly enters the difficult part. Whether the next work will be bigger is less important than asking whether it will be more honest. Whether more people will watch is less important than asking whether those filmed inside can still keep their own voices after the program ends.

## Thirty Seconds Later, The Questions Worth Leaving Behind

Ku’s Dream allowed Taiwanese people to see their own food, accents, and lives, and allowed foreigners to see that learning Chinese is not just an exam of "speaking well or not." It turned the stranger’s gaze into a program, turned the program’s traffic into an industrial ambition, and pushed that industrial ambition back onto the creator: After scale increases, who is responsible for those who were filmed? Who is responsible for the labor in the team? Who is responsible for the harm caused by a single sentence?

This may be the part of Ku’s story that is least like tourism promotion. Cultural exchange will not always be only welcome,感动 (moved), and enthusiasm. It also includes the power of language, the choices of viewing, the costs of production, and the responsibilities of employers. When a foreign creator decides to live in Taiwan for the long term, what he gains is not only a stage to introduce Taiwan to the world, but also an identity that must face rules together with Taiwan.
