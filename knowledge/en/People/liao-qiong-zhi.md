> **30-Second Overview:** Liao Chiung-chih, born in Keelung in 1935, entered the Jinshan Opera Company at age 15, performing from "Child of Hardship" through indoor opera, outdoor opera, radio, and television. Her most significant work may not be any single play, but rather dismantling Taiwanese Opera—which had lost its stage—into vocal techniques, movements, scripts, and pedagogy for the next generation to utilize. What she left behind is not a legend of a _kudan_ (tragic female role), but a method that allows memory not to reside solely in the elderly.

Liao Chiung-chih was born in Keelung in 1935. At age 15, she was conscripted into the Jinshan Opera Company to study performing arts. The term "conscripted" better reflects her actual means of acquiring livelihood and skill than simply "apprenticed." Records from the National Center for Traditional Arts' preservation project indicate that after serving three years and four months in the opera troupe, she began working as a _dan_ (female role) in troupes such as Jinhe and Longxiao Feng. [^1]

She was later known as the "First Kudan of Taiwan," which can lead people to reduce her entire life to a crying tone. However, what makes Liao Chiung-chih's story truly unforgettable is that she experienced both the most vibrant stages of Taiwanese Opera and the era when those stages were gradually disappearing: indoor opera, outdoor opera, radio, television, and finally, the classroom and archives. [^2][^3]

The contrast in this journey is this: **Taiwanese Opera once lived through bodies like Liao Chiung-chih; later, Liao Chiung-chih had to dismantle the memory within her body and teach it to those who had never seen that stage.**

## Early Lessons by the Hearth

Early opera troupes were not art schools as imagined today. The Ministry of Culture's introduction notes that children from impoverished families were sent into these troupes; the companies provided food, lodging, and training in a dialectal art form. Liao Chiung-chih began studying performing arts at age 14 and reached the golden age of indoor Taiwanese Opera by age 18. [^3]

Her training at the Jinshan Opera Company included boiling water, cleaning, practicing marching, splits, vocal projection, and running the stage. That system was not romantic; it even involved exploitation and violence. But to only describe it as suffering is to miss something else: the vocal techniques, expressions, and movements were refined in these day-to-day details. [^2][^3]

She was thin, yet her voice could sustain a stage. Records from the National Cultural and Artistic Foundation state that she later compiled scripts such as _Shi Xei Ji_, _Shan Bo Ying Tai_, _Chen San Wu Niang_, and _Han Yue_, and documented vocal techniques, movements, and rehearsals. She did not just perform on stage; she created an index for a skill that was originally reliant on oral tradition. [^2]

## When the Stage Replaced the Radio

Liao Chiung-chih left indoor opera at age 21 to work in outdoor opera and radio. She later performed in television Taiwanese Opera. The collection records of the National Center for Traditional Arts clearly outline this progression: she joined the Wang Siming radio troupe, performing on Central Broadcasting and Police Broadcasts, before successively appearing on three television stations. [^4]

The change in medium was not merely moving the stage into the living room. Indoor opera had fixed theaters and continuous performances; outdoor opera moved with religious ceremonies and local events. Radio removed movements, leaving only sound. Television demanded that performance conform to the camera lens. Liao Chiung-chih crossed these media, and Taiwanese Opera within her body became not just a stage technique, but a language that could be rearranged across different devices. [^3][^4]

Cultural Ministry materials point out that as radio and television arrived, traditional opera theaters saw a gradual decline in Taiwanese Opera. However, Liao Chiung-chih did not treat the new media as an enemy. Her stance was that both new and traditional theater were important, and one should not be discarded for the development of the other. [^3]

For an artist who traversed four mediums, this was not abstract compromise. She knew that radio required sound before movement, television demanded expressions close to the camera, while the stage still needed a gesture understandable by the last row of audience members. Each medium altered the scale of performance, but none canceled the core of Taiwanese Opera: lyrics, melody, movements, and the back-and-forth between performer and audience. [^3][^4]

She also did not treat "tradition" as belonging to a single era. When compiling scripts, she absorbed the movements and performing methods from other genres and placed them within the melodies and language of Taiwanese Opera. This approach did not turn Taiwanese Opera into Peking Opera; rather, it presented an art form developed in Taiwan that underwent continuous exchange and transformation. [^6]

This also explains why she deserves to be written as a person, not just as a "preserver of Taiwanese Opera": she knew the genre would change, but she also knew that change without memory underneath results only in stage effects.

## The Kudan is Not Just a Face

The _kudan_ (tragic female role) is often misunderstood as a character who cries constantly. The power of Liao Chiung-chih's crying tone comes not just from her timbre, but because she integrated expressions, breathing techniques, movements, and life experience into a single action. A special article in the National Center for Traditional Arts, using _Dongshui Mudan_ (Frozen Water Peony) as an example, points out that she connected character performance back to memories of her mother and childhood, reinterpreting those life experiences. [^5]

Care must be taken here. Equating her suffering directly with artistic talent romanticizes the loss of her childhood, poverty, and being sold into a troupe. Her life experience provided material for performance, but it cannot be inverted to explain that "suffering makes one worthy." What truly turned experience into art was the training, compilation, and selection she accumulated over many years. [^2][^5]

_Dongshui Mudan_ premiered in 2008 and was later re-staged by young performers. In that play, students played a young Liao Chiung-chih, with the teacher and student performing on stage together, making the biography not just a memory, but a site of transmission. [^5]

Liao Chiung-chih's _kudan_ thus had a second layer of meaning: she performed the tragedy of the character while also allowing the audience to see how an art form incorporated a woman's loss, labor, and experience in supporting her family onto the public stage. This judgment comes from the works and pedagogical path she left behind; it does not require adding any grand label for her.

At the same time, "kudan" cannot be her only name. She also wrote plays, arranged melodies, designed teaching materials, and taught students. If readers only remember that she cried movingly, they miss how she transformed a tradition proud of improvisation and oral transmission into a form that could be studied, rehearsed, and re-staged. [^2][^4]

The reason her performance transcended eras is because she continuously sought new entry points for her craft: demonstration performances, campus clubs, transmission programs, script publications, and having students play her childhood in her works. [^5][^6]

## Writing the "Inner Organs" onto Paper

Traditional Taiwanese Opera long relied on the performer's "inner organs"—the ability of actors to remember melodies, be familiar with movements, and spontaneously organize lyrics and dialogue even without a complete fixed script. This capability was vibrant yet fragile: when an individual left the stage, what had not been recorded could disappear with them. [^4][^6]

Liao Chiung-chih began compiling performance memories into scripts and teaching materials in the 1980s. Records from the National Cultural and Artistic Foundation state that she taught traditional Taiwanese Opera at the Taipei City Education Center in 1986, and subsequently wrote scripts such as _Chen San Wu Niang_, _Shi Xei Ji_, and _Shan Bo Ying Tai_. In 1997, she published movement materials complete with illustrations and videotapes. [^2]

She did not start with complete written tools. When the collection of her scripts was reported by Central News in 2024, Liao Chiung-chih said that she worked hard to learn to read in order to pass on Taiwanese Opera; if there were characters she didn't know, she would circle them and ask experts for help. This detail speaks more to what she did than "lifelong dedication": preservation was never just about sealing the past in a glass case. She was willing to learn methods that were unfamiliar to her so that others could read her memory. [^7]

An introduction from the Ministry of Culture's Museum Island event in 2018 also recorded how her decades of accumulated plays and vocal pieces were systematically preserved through images, sound, text, and sheet music. The same page listed events such as her demonstration performance invited by Chang Hui in 1983, the founding of the Xinzhan Taiwanese Opera Troupe in 1989, the establishment of the Cultural Education Foundation in 1999, and later transmission programs. [^8]

## One Person Cannot Equal One Genre

In the late 1980s and 1990s, Liao Chiung-chih successively founded the Xinzhan Taiwanese Opera Troupe and the Liao Chiung-chih Cultural Education Foundation. These organizations mark her transition from "I can perform" to "how to make others perform," which cannot be seen merely as decoration on a resume. [^1][^8]

This turning point was not achieved by her alone. Music and opera researchers like Xu Changhui brought her into the fields of demonstration performances and cultural education, while students brought her oral experiences into text, video, and courses. Her achievements therefore cannot be explained solely by "genius." It is the result of long-term collaboration between an artist, a researcher, a student, and a cultural institution. [^2][^6]

Taiwanese Opera was once viewed as low-brow street art by some audiences, but Liao Chiung-chih brought it into schools and national theaters. This path gave this underestimated folk craft a place where it could be rigorously watched, studied, and discussed, without erasing its popular origins. [^5][^9]

The preservation project was more concrete: the National Center for Traditional Arts recorded that the Liao Chiung-chih classic repertoire preservation project consisted of five stages, completing works such as _Chen San Wu Niang_, _Wang Kui Fu Guaiying_, _Shan Bo Ying Tai_, _Shi Xei Ji_, and _Wang Baochuan_; recording about 200 important melodies, and preserving them through video, music scores, scripts, and commentary. [^4]

This methodology turned "inheritance" from a mere wish into several checkable items: who taught, what was taught, which vocal passage, which movement, what medium it was preserved in, and whether the next student could re-perform it. The genre no longer relied solely on the memory of one master, nor did that mean the master's personal style was eliminated. On the contrary, because sufficient details were left behind, later talents could understand the nuances.

When the Ministry of Culture introduced _Li Mao Huan Tai Zi_ (The Cat Swaps the Prince) in 2014, Liao Chiung-chih served as the narrator in that performance, preserving the theatrical memory of post-war indoor opera, and allowing first-generation transmission students to perform major roles. She was not merely a revered predecessor. [^9]

This arrangement is highly symbolic: she moved herself from being the sole center on stage to being between the stage and the audience. She explained the story's origins, while students were responsible for keeping the characters alive. History ceased to be just the teacher's memory; it became a task that could be taken up by the next generation. [^9]

Transmission does not succeed automatically. Records from the National Cultural and Artistic Foundation also document the difficulties of her early teaching: she initially wanted to teach everything she knew in one go, but both she and the students were exhausted. After communication, she gradually refined a teaching method that involved interaction with students. This setback is important because it shows that inheritance is not about pouring knowledge down to the next person, but about redesigning the pace of learning. [^2]

The most crucial succession on stage is allowing students to stand in different positions and make their own judgments about the same play, rather than merely replicating the teacher as another teacher. Liao Chiung-chih's transmission work shifted from "Does she perform it like her?" to "Can the student continue to think?"

## She Was Always Seeking an Audience

Ultimately, preserving a form of theater must return to the audience. In articles about artistic succession, Liao Chiung-chih repeatedly discussed audience cultivation. She brought demonstration performances into schools and had students form Taiwanese Opera clubs because preserving only performers without viewers meant the genre lacked daily life. [^6]

This was her distance from the idea that "traditional art relies only on old fans." She did not demand that young audiences understand every tune before entering the theater; instead, she connected classic excerpts, children's Taiwanese Opera, school clubs, and stage narration to provide entry points for those encountering Taiwanese Opera for the first time. [^2][^9]

These entry points also changed the class perception of art. Theater originally performed in fields and temple courtyards could enter schools, national theaters, and international demonstration venues. It retained its Tâi-gí (Taiwanese) singing and folk sentiment but no longer had to accept the judgment of being "only vulgar." [^5][^8]

## After Being Boxed Up, the Play Continues to Run

The most memorable artifacts from Liao Chiung-chih's later life are less like trophies and more like stacks of scripts that students open, vocal pieces that can be repeatedly heard, and teaching materials pointing toward gestures and footwork. She turned things that originally only circulated within the theater into materials that could be preserved, critiqued, and re-arranged. [^2][^4]

The _Liao Chiung-chih Taiwanese Opera Classics II_ completed in 2024 included seven adapted or newly composed works, a process involving two years of manuscript compilation, oral interviews, and Tâi-gí proofreading. This work did not end Liao Chiung-chih's creation; rather, it handed the scripts to the next batch of performers, allowing the Xinzhan Troupe to continue producing new plays in 2025. [^7]

Therefore, Liao Chiung-chih's importance is not just about how many audiences she moved to tears, nor just about how many cultural heritage titles she received. She shows us that for an art form to survive, it relies on preserving three things simultaneously, rather than claiming it is immutable: the details of the body, the traces in text, and the space where students can continue to rewrite.

From "Child of Hardship" to preserver, she carried the memory of that arduous theatrical world. She moved what was once known only between teacher and student into places accessible to everyone, piece by piece. Taiwanese Opera thus does not have to remain solely within Liao Chiung-chih's voice; one can still hear her teaching how to speak.

This conclusion also raises a question: Does preserving music scores, scripts, and images equal preserving Taiwanese Opera? Not necessarily. Data allows later generations to find the melody again, but it cannot replace the breathing during rehearsal, the tacit understanding between actors and musicians, nor guarantee that someone will buy a ticket and sit down to watch a play through. The Ministry of Culture's introduction therefore discussed both the value of traditional works and the development of new plays. The end point of preservation remains the audience. [^3][^6]

Liao Chiung-chih's work did not solve all problems for Taiwanese Opera, but it offered a viable answer: first, preserve what the body knows, and then give students the opportunity to perform it incorrectly, bring it to life, or make it their own version. The continuation of the genre relies on allowing today to have a concrete encounter with the past, rather than replicating an unchanged past.
