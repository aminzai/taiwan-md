> The Japanese police archives record theft, robbery, murder, and pursuit, while folk ballads, storytelling, temples, and games rewrote him into a righteous bandit who robbed the rich to give to the poor.
> This article does not whitewash him, nor does it treat legends as verdicts. Instead, it asks: Why would the name of a criminal be preserved by an entire island in its own language?[^1] [^2]
>
> **Introduction:** When reading about Liao Tien-chen, don't rush to decide whether he was a hero or a criminal. First, see how the same name changed four identities between police files, Taiwanese ballads, Bali cemetery, and digital games.

## First, Look at That Arrest Warrant

In November 1909, in the area of Bali's Guanyin Mountain. The Japanese police considered Liao Tien-chen someone who must be captured. The arrest warrant left by the Taiwan Governor's Office Police Department compressed a real person into an image that could be identified, traced, and rewarded. This image contains no words like "righteous bandit," "heroic outlaw," or "anti-Japanese hero." It is first and foremost a document of the state pursuing a criminal.[^9]

The "Report on the Arrest of Liao Tien-chen" preserved at the National Palace Museum's Taiwan Historical Archives first confirms a fact that is not romantic but very important: Liao Tien-chen was not a figure fabricated by later generations from thin air. The magistrate of Taipei Prefecture reported to the Taiwan Governor's Office how the police investigated his hideout, contacted his accomplices, and finally surrounded him on the peak of Guanyin Mountain, where Yang Lin killed him with a hoe.[^1]

In 1883, Liao Tien-chen was born in Taichung's Qingshui. In 1909, he died in Bali, at the age of 27. These two years are like a skeleton, but they still cannot explain why his name lived on for over a century.[^2]

| Materials Left Behind                  | What They Tell Us                                                               | Conclusions That Cannot Be Directly Inferred                    |
| -------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Taiwan Governor's Office related files | He was a real person, the police pursued him, and he died in the Bali area      | Cannot therefore write him as an anti-Japanese hero             |
| Post-war ballads and storybooks        | Folk turned him into a righteous bandit who robbed the rich to give to the poor | Cannot treat the plot of ballads as individual historical facts |
| Movies, TV shows, and games            | Each generation calls him back through its own medium                           | Cannot treat adapted works as biographies                       |

These three types of materials placed together, Liao Tien-chen began to have a three-dimensional outline: **the police recorded him as a social problem that needed to be eliminated, while the folk preserved him as a name that could entrust unrest.**

## He Was Not a Hero Before the Crime

Taiwan's _Guanghua_ compiled a less romantic biography based on Qingshui household registration data, Japanese newspapers, and Taiwan Governor's Office archives. When Liao Tien-chen was 8 years old, his father died, his mother remarried, and he first lived with his grandmother, later supported by his aunt. He started committing crimes around the age of 18, and at 20, he was sentenced by the Taichung District Court for three theft cases, and even escaped from prison twice during his sentence.[^3]

At the age of 22, he and Zhang Fu from Banqiao conspired to rob a tea merchant, involving 3,000 yuan, and accidentally injured his accomplice when resisting arrest. At 23, he and his accomplices repeatedly stole from wealthy families, and were found with a small gun and Japanese saw on their bodies. These details will not make him endearing, but they remind us that Liao Tien-chen was not a pure hero who was first misunderstood by the masses and then blackened by the officialdom.[^3]

In August 1909, he stole police guns, ammunition, and swords, which made him a focus of attention by the Japanese police. Then he was involved in the robbery of Lin Yuanxian's family, the shooting of detective Chen Liangjiu, and the robbery of Li Hongjia's family in Wugu. From summer to November, several cases piled up in a short time, pushing a recidivist to become the top target of the colonial police.[^2] [^3]

Here, a familiar saying needs to be put back in its place. Liao Tien-chen is often called an "anti-Japanese hero," but the existing official archives do not classify him as a political prisoner or a member of an anti-Japanese organization. The Taiwan Historical Archives' statement is closer: under the Japanese colonial rule, he was a recidivist and murderer, not an anti-Japanese bandit acting with political goals.[^1] [^2]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Writing Liao Tien-chen only as an "anti-Japanese hero" seems to elevate him, but it also hands him over to another convenient myth. The truly ugly part is that he might be a criminal, a resistor, and also someone used by later generations to speak out for society.

"Robbing the rich to give to the poor" cannot be directly used as his ledger either. What the official archives can prove is the cases, pursuit, and death. As for whether he often distributed stolen goods to the poor, it mainly comes from later oral traditions, ballads, temple legends, and storytelling. The National Taiwan History Museum adopted a more cautious statement in the collection description: his records of theft, robbery, and murder are real background, while "anti-Japanese hero, righteous bandit" is an image gradually shaped by legends.[^4]

## Files Are Not Transparent Windows

Official documents are important, but they are not transparent windows. The "Report on the Arrest of Liao Tien-chen" is a pursuit report written by the police to their superiors, and the arrest warrant is a tool for police and civilians to identify the target. They can tell us how the government described Liao Tien-chen, but they will not automatically tell us how the residents of Qingshui, Dadaocheng, or Bali understood him.[^1] [^9]

The position of Taiwan's _Ribun Shimbun_ is even more subtle. _Guanghua_ compiled reports pointed out that Liao Tien-chen appeared in the social news section more than ten times, and after the case escalated, it even occupied nearly a full page. These reports may carry the official hope that people would be afraid and not harbor criminals, but they also made his name enter alleys and corners faster than ordinary criminals.[^3]

At that time, there were indeed people providing food, residence, or tools for him, and some were even arrested by the Japanese police for it. These records cannot prove that everyone treated him as a righteous bandit, but they can prove that the pursuit was not a game for only two people, the police and the fugitive. Local residents, protectors, accomplices, news readers, and the colonial government all participated in the making of the early version of this story.[^3]

## Why Did the People Reserve a Place for Him

The answer is not only in "he stole from the rich." More importantly, he made power appear clumsy for the first time in daily stories: the police could be fooled, the police guns could be stolen, and the homes of the wealthy were not always safe. Researcher Li Ji-hua believes that Liao Tien-chen's actions may not have had a clear political program, but they carried a rebellious character that only targeted Japanese people or wealthy Taiwanese and liked to play tricks on "Ah Ben."[^3]

The colonial government used newspapers to sculpt him as a villain, but the people read "the government can't catch him" from the same batch of news. His heroization did not need to first prove that he was morally flawless. It was more like a small person retelling a bad news over and over until a little breathing room of resistance emerged.

Academic research also reminds us that Liao Tien-chen's legend is not a fixed old story. The _Study on the Transformation of the Liao Tien-chen Legend_ divides the materials into three themes of anti-Japanese, emotional righteousness, and mythology, and then observes how they continue to change from the Japanese colonial period, after the war, to after the lifting of martial law. In other words, every time later generations retell it, they are adjusting "what kind of person is worthy of being called a hero" for their own era.[^8]

| Time                             | Main Medium                                                         | Liao Tien-chen in the Story                                                                                              |
| -------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Japanese colonial period to 1945 | Police files, Chinese-language newspapers, and folk oral traditions | A recidivist who needs to be pursued, and also a name that makes colonial power embarrassed                              |
| 1945 to 1987                     | Ballads, movies, storytelling, and local sacrifices                 | A righteous bandit who robbed the rich to give to the poor, an anti-Japanese hero, and a chivalrous figure with feelings |
| 1987 to now                      | Academic research, publishing, games, and digital communities       | Re-examined, re-translated, and also re-delivered to the next generation as a historical figure                          |

This table is not ranking the legend on a "getting closer to the truth" progress ladder. It is more like a media map, reminding us that every era will put its own problems into Liao Tien-chen. The Japanese colonial period asked who was challenging the police, the post-war period asked who could represent resistance, and after the lifting of martial law, it began to question how heroization itself occurred.[^8]

## The Last Night, History Also Has No Single Version

In November 1909, Liao Tien-chen hid in the area of Bali's Guanyin Mountain. The official "Report on the Arrest of Liao Tien-chen" recorded that Yang Lin provided clues to the police, the police went to surround the area, and Liao Tien-chen was finally killed by a hoe. The Taiwan Historical Archives' collation recorded the death date as November 18, while the stele preserved at Huaxia Shrine was carved on November 19.[^1] [^2]

As for why Yang Lin betrayed him, there are at least two explanations left later. One says it was for the reward, and the other says it was to preserve the Yang family. _Guanghua_ pointed out that neither of these explanations can be easily taken as the final truth. What can be determined is that Yang Lin did cooperate with the police, and Liao Tien-chen did not die under the Japanese police's gun, but ended his life in a siege where his acquaintances and the police overlapped.[^3]

This ending does not have the clean ending common in hero movies. There was no duel that clearly divided good and evil, and there was no testament that could explain the motives for everyone. There is only a pursuit report preserved by the colonial government, a tombstone that was later worshiped, and a Yang Lin who keeps changing positions in different versions.

## After Death, He Really Started to Deform

After Liao Tien-chen died, the story did not stop, but entered another life line. The Taiwan Historical Archives recorded that Bali was originally only a small cemetery. After the 1956 film on the theme of Liao Tien-chen was released, it became a media focus. In 1958, the local area built Huaxia Shrine, making the cemetery, sacrifice, and film memory overlap in the same place.[^2]

![Liao Tien-chen Stone Statue in Bali](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/2/25/%E5%BB%96%E6%B7%BB%E4%B8%81%E7%9F%B3%E5%83%8F_%28cropped%29.jpg)

_Image: Liao Tien-chen Stone Statue in Bali's Tien-chen Park. Original image from [Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:%E5%BB%96%E6%B7%BB%E4%B8%81%E7%9F%B3%E5%83%8F_%28cropped%29.jpg), taken by Outlookxp, CC BY-SA 4.0.[^12]\_

The _New Song of Liao Tien-chen_ collected by the National Cultural Memory Library writes his story in seven-syllable lines and is sung in Taiwanese Hokkien. The collection description directly presents the transformation: in the eyes of the Governor's Office, Liao Tien-chen was a vicious criminal, but after being rendered by rural oral traditions and opera, he became an anti-Japanese hero and a legendary righteous bandit in the minds of the Taiwanese.[^5]

This does not mean that the folk "did not know" that he killed people. A more close understanding is that the folk did not put all of a person's deeds into the same drawer. He could have a criminal record, and he could also become a spokesperson for the unjust era. Heroes are not medals on the verdict, but the way the masses redistribute responsibility and dignity in the story.

In the 1990s, storytelling, radio, television, and dance gave him a new body again. _Guanghua_ recorded Wu Yue-tian's long-term narration of Liao Tien-chen, and also recorded how the audience understood him as a "righteous bandit," advocated righteousness, and vented for those who dared not speak out. The same report also reminded that the story kept adding new characters and dramatic plots as it spread, becoming more and more like a "great hero."[^3]

## From Taiwanese Ballads to Game Screens

Liao Tien-chen's next rebirth was not in the temple, but on the screen. Openbook pointed out that the 2004 Flash game _The Legend of Tianding_ became the childhood memory of many players. The 2021 work _Liao Tien-chen - The Last of the Era's Villain_ uses Taiwanese text, sound, comic style, and Dadaocheng scenes to re-deliver this name to the new generation.[^6]

When English game media _Game Developer_ interviewed the production team, the team explained that they consulted books, photos, and Dadaocheng architecture from the Japanese colonial period, and frankly stated that the game prioritized playability and did not treat every battle as a historical reenactment. This admission is very important. Games are not turning files into animations, but letting players walk through the gaps between historical materials, legends, and imagination.[^7]

Therefore, the game can use the waist belt to climb, and use the eaves to escape from the Japanese police, which does not mean that the historical materials prove every action. It means that later generations need a operable Liao Tien-chen. The ballads let people sing his story, the storytelling lets people listen to his story, and the games let people use their own hands to take him from the corner of Dadaocheng Street.

## Leaving a Name, Not Whitewashing Him

The most worth remembering thing about Liao Tien-chen is not the binary question of "whether he is Taiwan's Robin Hood or not." This metaphor is easy to understand, but it also quickly flattens Taiwan's own history. Robin Hood is someone else's template, while Liao Tien-chen's materials come from Qingshui, Taipei, Dadaocheng, Bali, colonial police, Chinese-language newspapers, Taiwanese ballads, and a constantly rebuilt cemetery.

The existing data shows us an unomittable line. Victims and violence cannot disappear just because the legend sounds good, and Liao Tien-chen's crimes cannot be rewritten as entirely just because later generations sympathize with him. But what cannot be erased is why people are willing to set up a stele, build a shrine, compile songs, tell stories, and make games for a criminal after his death.

Perhaps what Liao Tien-chen left to Taiwan is not a hero's resume, but a persistent question: **When the official files only leave the name of the crime, why do the people still want to preserve the name of a criminal?**

The answer is not to re-trial him, but to understand how memory works. What people preserve may not be Liao Tien-chen himself, but the self that once desperately wanted to see power stumble and had no other way to speak out. When we read his name again today, what is truly recalled is not only a fugitive in the mountains of Bali in 1909, but also how Taiwan turned a person's crimes, resistance, fear, and hopes into a song that cannot be finished.
