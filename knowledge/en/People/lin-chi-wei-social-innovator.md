> **30‑second overview:** Chi‑Wei Lin (English name Chi‑Wei Lin, online known as CW or CW Lin) is a Taiwanese entrepreneur, founder and CEO of Portaly, and a graduate of National Taiwan University (NTU) Electrical Engineering and UCLA Electrical Engineering Graduate Institute. From traditional industry to software and then to AI, each transition has required starting from zero and relearning. His first venture was the children’s educational board‑game brand “Shepherd Kit” (綿羊犬). In 2014 it won a silver medal in the United States Parents' Choice Awards, setting the best record for a Taiwanese entry, and raised over US$1 million via crowdfunding. In 2021 it was sold to the CommonWealth Magazine Group. In 2022 he launched Portaly (company name “Real Engine”), which within four years became Taiwan’s largest creator‑commerce platform, turning profitable in 2025 with no paid marketing, relying on organic PLG. He also serves as an AAMA Taipei Cradle entrepreneur (cohort 13) and columnist for _Manager Today_ and _Meet Startup_. The donation flow for this knowledge base is handled on his platform.

In 2014, the Parents' Choice Awards toy design competition in the United States received nearly four hundred entries and is regarded as the “Oscars” of the toy industry. A small Taipei company captured two silver medals, beating LEGO and tying with an Apple‑invested remote‑control car for second place, setting the best record for a Taiwanese entry ever. The product was Shepherd Kit, not a flashing plastic toy but a physical board game that teaches children aged 4‑10 to love mathematics, archaeology, and ecology. The founder, Lin Chi‑Wei, graduated from NTU’s Electrical Engineering department, earned a graduate degree at UCLA, declined a PhD admission, and chose not to go to Silicon Valley to design chips, instead returning to Taiwan to create children’s educational toys.[^1][^2]

He moved the board games onto crowdfunding platforms, from Taiwan’s ZecZec to the United States Kickstarter. Lin says the campaigns raised over US$1 million, shipped to more than twenty countries, and even led to a collaboration invitation from the British Museum.[^3] In 2021, Shepherd Kit was sold to the CommonWealth Magazine Group.[^3]

## Turning Knowledge into Play

Archaeology, forests, mathematics. Every Shepherd Kit game essentially does the same thing: it takes a piece of difficult knowledge from the real world and breaks it down into rules that children are willing to play with. “Taiwanese kids are good at math but they hate math,” Lin described in an interview with _Crossing_ (《換日線》).[^4]

In 2017, he achieved something no one in the industry had done before: he persuaded the National Museum of Prehistory in Taitung to license its collections—such as the Changbin and Beinan cultures—to a startup of fewer than ten people, resulting in the board game _Prehistoric Adventure_. This was the nation’s first tabletop game co‑developed by a private startup and a public museum, involving two years of back‑and‑forth communication and ten version revisions, ultimately raising more than six times the crowdfunding goal. “Can we make the knowledge inside museums easier for people in Taiwan to approach?” he asked, calling himself a “translation tofu” for museums.[^2]

The habit later became the name of his company. Portaly’s parent company is called “Real Engine.”[^5]

> **📝 Curator’s Note**  
> Looking at Lin’s two entrepreneurial phases, a hidden thread emerges: everything he creates is about “conversion.” Converting knowledge into games, influence into revenue, traffic into business. The biggest conversion, however, is himself: from traditional wood‑working toys to software, then to AI—each time starting from zero, each time reaching the frontier of the field. In the toy industry he set the best Taiwanese record for a U.S. toy award; in software he built the largest creator platform in Taiwan; in the AI era his method documents have been republished by media and used by thousands. The product has changed three times, but his ability to learn and adapt has remained constant.

## Portaly Launch Portal

After selling Shepherd Kit in 2021, Lin reset his career track. He later reflected on Medium: ten years ago he started a business for “self‑achievement”; ten years later the motive became “I want to understand the world more.” “In the world of entrepreneurship I have become an explorer.”[^13]

In 2022 Lin noticed a problem: the influencers and KOLs he had worked with scattered their content across Instagram, YouTube, personal sites, and e‑commerce pages, yet there was no single place to gather it. Abroad, tools like Linktree already addressed this, but their templates were fixed and text‑only, unable to express personal‑brand differentiation.

Thus Portaly was born. It offers modular blocks that let creators assemble a unique page. “Creators can build a one‑of‑a‑kind personal page in half an hour, breaking away from the traditional ‘template‑style’ Link‑in‑Bio impression,” Lin said in an interview with _Meet Startup_ in early 2023.[^5]

In September 2022 Portaly had only 6,000 registered users. In February 2023 it closed a US$350,000 seed round led by AVA Angel Fund.[^5] Its user list includes individual creators such as “Blue‑Pigeon” and “Trash‑Girlfriend,” as well as organizations like Eslite, CommonWealth Magazine, and the P. LEAGUE+ basketball league.[^5]

Doing business in the Taiwanese market versus overseas presents different challenges. Lin gave a vivid example in the same interview:

> “Taiwanese users ask, ‘Do you have a donate (donate) feature?’  
> Overseas users ask, ‘Can you embed a donate platform?’”

One request asks you to build the feature; the other asks you to integrate it. “How we ‘link’ these platforms will be our top priority,” he said.[^5]

## From Zero to 200,000 Users with No Marketing Spend

On 5 June 2023 Portaly launched a new feature on Product Hunt: input a URL and AI instantly generates a personal page. The team positioned it as the world’s first AI‑driven Link‑in‑Bio tool, reaching the second spot on the day’s rankings within 24 hours.[^6] The same year Portaly was selected for Meet Taipei’s “Neo Star Startup” list, which chooses 30 companies from roughly 250 applicants each year.[^7] Lin himself was admitted to the AAMA Taipei Cradle cohort 13, a long‑term mentorship program led by veteran entrepreneurs.[^8]

According to AAMA’s description, Portaly is Taiwan’s largest creator and KOC platform, with overseas traffic accounting for about 30 % of visits.[^8] Public data shows that by 2025 more than 200,000 creators were using the platform, the company became profitable in 2025, and revenue grew more than 20‑fold from 2023 to 2025—all without a marketing team or paid acquisition, relying solely on product‑driven growth (organic PLG).[^9]

> “No marketing team” sounds like a drawback in venture circles, but for Portaly it is a selling point: if a tool is good enough that creators bring peers on board themselves, there is no need to buy ads. The simultaneous achievement of profitability, zero marketing spend, and PLG is what makes the model truly hard to copy.

## Build‑in‑Public Practitioner

In recent years Lin has added another identity: a practitioner of “build in public.” He has been doing this for many years: early in Portaly he publicly shared the interview methodology used to talk to 100 top‑tier influencer customers within a month and a half.[^14] In 2024 he grew his Threads account from 120 followers to 10,000 in two months, then published the algorithm analysis and growth tactics, which were licensed by _Business Next_ (《數位時代》).[^15] In 2026, during a trip to Silicon Valley, he documented Taiwanese entrepreneurs’ situation in the AI gold‑rush and published it in _Meet Startup_.[^16] He also writes columns for _Manager Today_ and _Meet Startup_, focusing not on success stories but on the tools he actually uses: full‑AI product development workflows, AI‑culture checklists for managers, and an AI Vault knowledge‑management system.[^10][^11] He open‑sourced his AI knowledge‑base as **Vault for Founders**, and authored a “Lobster Manual” for non‑engineers to get started with OpenClaw; according to his public data, these AI documents have been accessed by thousands of users.[^9] In 2026 he was invited to speak at the Stripe Sessions conference (representing Asia‑Pacific founders); after returning, his first act was to write a reflection urging Taiwanese startups to consider where an AI agent fits in their business model.[^11][^9]

This habit runs through both of his ventures: the crowdfunding and museum‑licensing experience of Shepherd Kit, and the PLG and AI integration methods of Portaly. He packages each “conversion” into reusable assets that others can immediately apply. From physical board games to creator pages to AI operating procedures, the product shape has changed three times, but the underlying line remains: identify a bottleneck for a person, build a machine that moves the stuck element elsewhere, and share the method with the next person who gets stuck.
