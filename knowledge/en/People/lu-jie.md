# Lü Jie: From a Question of 228 That Was Avoided to Twenty-Eight Classes of Taiwanese History

> **30-Second Overview:** In 2026, Lü Jie launched a Taiwanese history course spanning 10 hours, structured around 28 historical sites; the curriculum not only covers regime changes but also maps freedom, democracy, culture, identity, and international standing onto one chart. [^8]
>
> During high school, he once asked about the 228 Incident in class, which his teacher initially avoided. Years later, this tutoring instructor—who originally entered the history department due to a mistake on his application form—repeatedly emphasizes that history is not about memorizing a single answer, but about discerning historical materials, facts, and perspectives. [^1] [^2]
>
> Therefore, Lü Jie's story is more than just "how a famous teacher became popular." What is more worth pursuing is: after teaching history in an engaging way, can one also guide the reader back to the sources, evidence, and their own judgment?

In a high school classroom, a question concerning the 228 Incident was temporarily set aside by the teacher. More than two decades later, Lü Jie, who had posed that question, is breaking down Taiwanese history into 28 accessible historical sites. [^1] [^8]

The distance between these two events is not merely from the classroom to the camera, or from a cram school to an online course. It is more like a fault line in historical education: some questions are overlooked because they are difficult to discuss. Some questions, conversely, demand extra inquiry because they are too easily turned into stories—where does the story's basis lie?

What makes Lü Jie special is that he did not package himself as someone who loved academia from the start. He even responded to others' assumption that he "had aspired to history since childhood" with a very blunt retort:

> "Which ear of yours heard me say 'I am interested in history'?" [^2]

This was not feigned contrast. Behind this counter-question lay an incorrect application form, and it was also one of the things he frequently mentioned later: sometimes life does not follow the script you wrote; the question is whether, after walking through the wrong door, you can re-understand which room you have entered.

## The Question That Was Avoided by the Teacher

In a July 2026 podcast episode, Lü Jie recalled asking about the 228 Incident in high school class, and his teacher "was startled" and did not answer directly. What was left from the program was not the full transcript of the classroom discussion, but a small scene: presidential elections were direct, yet there remained hesitation over what topics could be discussed in class and how far they could go. [^1]

The weight of this scene is that it offered no dramatic resolution. The introduction did not detail why the teacher avoided the topic or how other students reacted. We do not need to fill in a more picturesque story for the blanks. What is certain is that, for young Lü Jie, it was an experience where "history is not something already written in textbooks."

What he asked later was not whether Taiwanese history should discuss tragedy, but whether it could be taught as a body of knowledge worthy of understanding and pride. This "pride" does not have to mean grinding complex history into a national anthem. It is more likely about enabling students to know that they have the right to question how a land was named, how it was governed, and what unfinished memories remain. [^1]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Questions that are skipped do not disappear; they merely appear elsewhere. They might return in cram school settings, short video comment sections, or at the family dinner table; the difference is whether we are prepared to let the question linger longer than a slogan.

## An Incorrect Application Form That Did Not Send Him to His Predetermined Life

Lü Jie studied history at National Chung Cheng University and later completed his graduate studies in History at the same institution. This academic background appears as a flat line in a biographical introduction, but the individual's version is not so neat: he originally applied for law school before ending up in history because of an application error. [^2] [^3]

Before that application form, his education was not smooth sailing. _Weekly_ recorded that after graduating high school, he paused his higher education due to unsatisfactory university entrance exam results. During that time, he worked as a convenience store clerk, cement worker, and in funeral-related jobs, and admitted to having been a "small hoodlum," involved in street fights and disturbances. These are not embellishments added to the turning points of his life, but rather the living conditions he had to acknowledge when recounting "facing his true self." [^10]

It is often written as fate that one application error determines someone's path to becoming a famous teacher. This writing style is too convenient. A more factual account is that an entrance without prior planning later became his field of work. He began tutoring in college, and history ceased being just a required course; it started becoming something he had to explain clearly to a group of students. [^10]

A cram school is an honest arena. If the lecture lacks rhythm, students will leave first. Relying only on test techniques, students might remember one question but not necessarily why they are studying. Lü Jie's approach, therefore, was not to completely expel exams from the classroom, but to place them in a smaller role: history can help children face not just an entrance exam, but also recurring judgments in life. [^2]

## Interviews at Seafood Shops and the First Class He Wanted to Flee From

Lü Jie later described the beginning of his tutoring career as a grand scene: while he was drinking at a roadside seafood shop during college, a cram school van pulled up. He greeted the teacher who got out; upon learning he studied history, she asked him if he wanted to teach a comprehensive review of history. A phone call the next day turned into an interview, which became his first time entering a cram school. [^11]

He recalled that in the first class with about thirty or forty students, he felt like leaving the stage after five minutes. It took ten minutes before he slowly adapted to the pace of the cram school; half an hour later, he felt, "this is my stage." Far from having a heroic entrance, it was closer to professional reality: if you couldn't stand up, the students left; only if you could stay did you have the right to continue speaking. [^11]

He developed a very concrete class configuration for this event: seven tenths of the time was spent on core material, while stories, jokes, and "reasoning" each took one tenth. Stories must be connected to the main course; jokes keep students engaged; and reasoning is what he hoped students would take out of the classroom. This is not a formula replicable in every classroom, but it shows how he treated attention as part of historical education. [^11]

In 2014, a video of him teaching at a cram school in Kaohsiung spread unexpectedly online. The English activity page of National Quemoy University described this event as his career turning point: someone who was previously down began moving toward a new public role because he was seen through the video. [^5]

The video's popularity is not an accreditation of historical quality. It only proved one thing: someone was willing to stop and listen to him. The truly difficult work is maintaining the ability to question, even after "someone is willing to listen."

## Historical Materials, Facts, Perspectives: Three Layers That Stories Cannot Omit

Lü Jie's view of history actually provided a yardstick for checking his own narrative. He stated: "To constitute a piece of history, there must be three elements: historical materials, facts, and perspectives." [^4]

Historical materials are the documents, objects, records, and testimonies that remain; facts are the more reliable reconstructions made about the past after identification, comparison, and discussion by people; and perspectives are how people understand those facts, choosing which relationships to prioritize. The three are connected but cannot be interchanged.

This distinction is precisely the limitation all historical content creators must face. Turning a piece of history into a five-minute memorable story is a skill. But a five-minute story cannot treat perspective as the sole fact, nor can it treat smoothly narrated cause and effect as sufficient evidence.

Lü Jie said when discussing historical education that education often requires students to "learn," but rarely addresses how to think or how to use what they learn. As a result, dates, names, and standard answers remain, while critical consciousness is pushed to the margins. [^4]

This assertion was not limited to interviews or books. In 2018, when discussing the new high school history curriculum framework, he publicly criticized historical education for constantly requiring students to memorize historical materials and facts without adequately training them to form their own perspectives. He shifted the focus to testing methods, arguing that multiple-choice questions and essay questions should each account for half; the former confirms data and facts, while the latter requires students to organize context and argue. [^12]

This does not mean that simply changing to essays will automatically make students think. It is more like a reminder: whatever the test demands, students will spend their time on. If education wants students to provide reasons, compare materials, and take stances, assessment cannot only reward the person who quickly circles the correct option.

This also gave his widely circulated saying a more concrete meaning:

> "History does not teach you to know history; it teaches you to know humanity." [^6]

If this is understood as "human nature never changes, so the past can be directly applied to today," that is too quick. The systems, media, power structures, and social conditions of different eras all change; people cannot treat the stories of ancient figures as ready-made verdicts. What is more worth taking away is another, slower understanding: humans make choices between interests, fears, hopes, identities, and power. To study history is to learn to look at those choices within their context.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The most easily misunderstood aspect of popular education is not that there are too many stories, but that the stories are mistaken for the final conclusion because they sound good. Separating "historical materials, facts, and perspectives" leaves an avenue for scrutiny in every good story.

## A Viral Video Is Not a Simple Answer

The internet allowed the classrooms of famous tutors to leave fixed seats. After the video's spread in 2014, Lü Jie’s way of speaking was received by more unseen audiences. Publications and media exposed him not only to students preparing for exams but also to young readers searching for life answers. [^5] [^9]

The most talked-about segment from that year was when he discussed the June Fourth Incident in class, designing a water truck as a fictional suppression plan, listing five "benefits," and simulating it on stage. Reports documented the laughter of students in the classroom and the viral spread of the video online. [^13]

But reach is not an exemption clause. _Liberty Times_ published a critique by Chen Wei-ting, arguing that the sorrow and struggle of the June Fourth Incident should not be treated with ridicule. The report did not include Lü Jie's official response to this controversy, so it cannot fill in his intentions or defenses. What could be written is that a joke originally meant to capture classroom attention was immediately subjected to scrutiny regarding historical trauma and expressive ethics once it entered the public internet. [^13]

This is an aspect of understanding Lü Jie that cannot be skipped. He excels at making students remember events through laughter, but the standard of historical education is not just about what students remembered; it is also about how the injured in those events were discussed. A good story must have tension. Stories involving state violence and death require restraint.

_The Education Is Not History, But Humanity_ (published in 2015) stated this position very directly. The book title used "Lü She" (魯蛇), a term familiar to the online generation, but the core of the book was not labeling failure; it brought the dilemmas of figures from ancient and modern times before the reader: disappointment, choices, consequences, and starting anew do not only happen in textbooks. [^6] [^9]

_Society is Moving, History Must Understand: Lü Jie Speaks_ (published in 2021) extended this language to myths, the Pre-Qin era, and the Hundred Schools of Thought. The book's preface once again placed the question into consideration: systems often require students to learn, but rarely address how to use what is learned. If studying history only leaves dates and figures, it is easy to treat the past as a series of answers waiting to be memorized. [^14]

This is exactly where Lü Jie's narrative power lies. He brings characters down from icons, allowing emperors, powerful ministers, or failures to become human beings who fear, calculate, and make mistakes. For readers new to history, this is easier to engage with than remembering several dates.

But power also brings responsibility. The more one can narrate complex events like a real-life story, the clearer they must mark the boundaries of sources, inferences, and rhetoric. Listeners should not let go of verification just because the speaker is humorous. Speakers should not tell stories so much that they lose warmth out of fear of criticism.

If historical education only remains memorization, students will think the past is a string of dead answers. If it only remains entertaining stories, students might think the past is merely a plot device serving today's agenda. The three-layered structure proposed by Lü Jie reminds both sides to avoid shortcuts. [^4]

## From the Cram School Stage to the News Desk

Lü Jie’s media path did not stop at teaching videos. In 2023, CTS News Network launched _Lü's Warring States BATTLE_, which he hosted. The program placed international affairs, cross-strait relations, livelihood issues, economy, and technology into discussions spanning generations and disciplines. [^7]

When CTS released it, they quoted his statement: the "BATTLE" in the program title does not just mean conflict, but goes through three stages of "conflict, compromise, and progress." This is a self-setting for a talk show, and can also be seen as the stance he repeatedly adopted when discussing history: putting opposition on the table first, then forcing the question out of a single answer. [^7]

Of course, a talk show is not a historical research lab. It has rhythm, guests, topical issues, and clashes of viewpoints; it cannot demand that every segment sound like an academic paper. However, having a historical educator enter a news studio makes one question clearer: if perspectives require debate, how should the basis of those perspectives be seen?

A better answer might not be to demand that every viewer read the sources first, but to cultivate several basic actions: what does this statement cite? Which people are included in the story, and which are left off-screen? How long is its alleged "always like this"?

These questions may sound unenthusiastic, but they are the starting point for public discussion that doesn't just consist of shouting matches. And because they are sufficiently simple, they are suitable to begin with a history class.

## Twenty-Eight Sites, None Can Replace Reader Judgment

In 2026, Lü Jie’s online course unfolded across 28 key historical sites, spanning prehistory, the Age of Exploration, Qing rule, Japanese colonial rule, and modern times. The course page listed freedom, democracy, culture, identity, and international standing as threads for understanding how Taiwan reached today. [^8]

In an interview with StoryStudio, he positioned this course as "under a banyan tree," not in a classroom; people come to chat and drink tea, not to choose the single answer from four options. To illustrate his point about changing perspectives, he used the Yu Qingfang incident, comparing the practice of issuing sales certificates and markups to vocabulary familiar to contemporary readers in direct sales. This is a narrative strategy, not reducing an anti-Japanese event into a commercial joke; its function is to introduce unfamiliar figures into the language of today's audience before returning to discuss the organization, beliefs, and risks of that era. [^11]

This design resembles a shift. Rather than cutting Taiwanese history into required chronological grids, it starts from a site, the fate of a person, or an era, allowing readers to see that "today" did not grow naturally.

But historical sites are not immersive stage sets. Behind every site are insufficient materials, conflicting memories, and interpretations that researchers may not agree on. A good story opens a door but does not walk the reader through the room. Twenty-eight classes provide a path, but they cannot replace twenty-eight inquiries.

Lü Jie once asked if he could teach a Taiwanese history worthy of pride. If this sentence must stand the test of time, the answer is probably not in making every piece of history sound like a victory, but in enabling people to see the wounds, choices, and creations left by different groups on this island, and knowing that they can continue to ask questions. [^1]

Initially, Lü Jie entered the history department merely because he made an application error. What kept him was not that coincidence, but a requirement for history: you must have materials to discuss facts. Only after knowing the facts can one begin to form perspectives. [^2] [^4]

The question avoided in that high school classroom did not receive a complete answer here. It left behind more like a reminder: when a story is particularly smooth, do not rush to applaud. First, ask where the materials for this piece of history are? What are its facts? And what perspective will you use to turn the next page?
