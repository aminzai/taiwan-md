> **30-second overview:** Evelyn Chen rose to fame with a biomedical engineering background and life-science videos, translating complex knowledge into everyday language. Her public journey has involved rapid fame, medical device advertising penalties, food advertising controversies, and a shift from paid courses to free podcast. This article does not portray her as a perfect science communication authority, but tracks how professional image creates trust, and how that trust is tested through commercial collaborations and error correction.

Evelyn Chen's public image began with a simple translation act: turning everyday problems people encounter into questions science can answer. She uses daily life scenes as entry points, starting from leftover dishes in the fridge, confusion in relationships, and physical reactions. In 2018, she operated her channel under her real name, Chen Ying-tong, and media reported she had a medical engineering background, with early content focused on life sciences and knowledge clarification. [^1]

This starting point is worth noting. Taiwan's YouTuber industry is no longer sustained by comedy, unboxing, or daily vlogs alone. Evelyn Chen turned "I have an explainable principle" into channel recognition. Audiences often judge content credibility based on her professional background before entering the video's argument. The 2018 report by Marie Claire described her as a creator who moved from medical engineering and entrepreneurship to knowledge-based YouTube, noting she brought a scientific perspective to topics like relationships, cooking, child-rearing, and physical health. [^2]

### Professional Appeal as Entry Point, Responsibility Follows

Her early attraction came from lowering the threshold of understanding. Each video did not need to complete a university lesson; audiences could first grasp a concept that could be applied to daily life. The Central News Agency later reviewed her channel, noting she combined scientific theory with plain text and life experience, uploading her first video in June 2018, reaching one million subscribers in 258 days. These numbers can illustrate the speed of her rise to fame, but cannot alone prove the scientific quality of each video. [^3]

This distinction is a basic skill often encountered by knowledge-based internet celebrities. **High academic credentials can make people willing to click on a video, but cannot verify every sentence in the video.** Evelyn Chen later said in an interview with a manager: "Traffic is not something I can control; what I can control is only the video quality. I just hope that in 10 years, when I look back, I won't feel I didn't try hard." [^4] The value of this statement lies in shifting attention from uncontrollable numbers back to verifiable work.

She also said that knowledge-based creators, even with just one follower, have a responsibility to do things right. [^4] This statement separates internet celebrity responsibility from "how many fans." For content platforms, influence is not measured only by subscription numbers. As long as someone changes their diet, purchases products, or believes certain medical claims because of a video, influence has already occurred.

### Simplifying Knowledge Does Not Mean Making It Casual

Evelyn Chen's content translation also faces another challenge: simplification itself creates new risks. When a piece of research is compressed into a few minutes of spoken language, premises, limitations, samples, and uncertainties are easily cut out. What the audience gets is a memorable conclusion, but not necessarily knowing where that conclusion applies.

A comment article published by the Cross-Strait Exchange Foundation cited her organic food video as an example, reporting that the Council of Agriculture had previously clarified the video's statements about pesticide residues in organic agricultural products. This article also discussed the 2019 cervical cancer screening product video and subsequent penalties, arguing that the professional image of knowledge-based creators increases the audience's trust in their information, so incorrect or inadequately explained content will have greater public consequences. This is the commentator's analysis, not proof that all disputes have been independently verified by that article, but it points to the core boundary of science communication content: **the ability to make complex things simple is skill. Knowing what cannot be simplified is responsibility.** [^5]

Evelyn Chen herself once said in an interview that videos occasionally make mistakes, and corrections are made when errors are found. She compared science to stacked bricks; papers may also have errors, what matters is to continue forward after correction. [^4] This "correct when wrong" attitude is worth preserving, but it cannot replace pre-shooting verification. A safer approach is to clearly distinguish confirmed research, reasonable inference, personal experience, and unverified claims before the content enters public circulation.

### When Science Communication Enters Advertising, Trust Has a Price

In March 2019, Evelyn Chen and Hui-Zhi Gene were fined by Taipei City Health Department for a cervical cancer screening medical device video. The Central News Agency reported that the video introduced the product and demonstrated its use, and the health department determined it had advertising nature, fining Evelyn Chen and the manufacturer NT$200,000 each under the Pharmaceutical Affairs Law. The report also pointed out that the collaborative content included a YouTube video and a Facebook post, which the health department determined constituted endorsement behavior. [^6]

The English report of the same incident also pointed out that the video had been requested to be removed, and the health department confirmed that there was a marketing agreement between her and the product manufacturer, treating the case as unapproved medical device advertising. [^7] Although the language and publication time of the two reports differ, they both point to the same fact: when a creator's professional image is placed in medical device promotion, the content is no longer just personal sharing, but enters the field of advertising law and consumer protection.

This incident does not mean "internet celebrities cannot do product endorsements." What is more worth questioning is that it is difficult for audiences to distinguish three roles in the video: is she doing science communication, sharing usage experience, or endorsing for the manufacturer? When a person simultaneously has a biomedical background, a friendly explanation style, and high subscription numbers, these three roles amplify each other. If the advertising label is not clear, the audience receives product information weighted by professional appeal.

In 2021, the Central News Agency again reported that Evelyn Chen used claims like "1 bottle equals 12 bottles" when selling vitamins, raising over NT$30 million in a short time on the crowdfunding platform. The TFDA pointed out that the product was labeled as a general food on the crowdfunding page, and food advertising must not be exaggerated, misleading, or claim medical efficacy. Taipei City Health Department also stated that terms like "improving circulation" might be exaggerated, and further investigation was needed. [^8]

This incident and the 2019 case involve different regulatory issues. The former is an investigation into food advertising wording and function claims, while the latter is about medical device advertising and qualifications. The regulatory issues of the two cases are different, and the core is clearer: **the law focuses on whether public information exceeds the boundaries of product advertising and consumer protection, not on determining the "true scientist" identity of the creator.**

### From Paid Content Back to Free, Not Just a Business Decision

Evelyn Chen also brought the same trust issue into online courses. Digital Times reported that she originally launched the "Consultation Notes" online course, and later agreed with the cooperative platform to release the content for free in podcast format, and assisted in handling refunds. [^9] RTI's English report placed this in the context of Taiwan's YouTube culture and the lack of mental health resources, describing external concerns about whether such "like psychological consultation" products crossed the boundaries of professional training and licensing, and recording her argument that the course was not online therapy. [^10]

The charging and release method of the course can be confirmed by reports, but the controversy involves professional boundaries. The media's use of "therapeutic" description cannot be directly used as a legal conclusion. What is more worth noting is another fact: when creators organize their own experience into a course, and the audience purchases the content, they also receive the credibility of "she has walked this path." Free release can reduce transaction costs, but does not automatically eliminate professional responsibility.

In an interview with a manager, she said that the current KPI of the video is whether the audience can take away one or two key messages after watching. [^4] This statement can become the minimum standard of knowledge-based content, but cannot become the highest standard. Good science communication not only leaves conclusions, but also leaves the conditions of the conclusions. Good new media monetization not only explains what the product can bring, but also lets the audience know which effects have not been proven.

### Traffic Turns Personal Expertise into an Institutional Issue

Evelyn Chen's case cannot only be placed in the framework of "personal choice." Her videos have been repeatedly edited and renamed in different fields through YouTube recommendations, social media sharing, brand cooperation, and media re-reporting. What was originally just an explanation may become health advice in search results, or become a trust endorsement on a crowdfunding page. The scene where the creator speaks changes, and the audience's expectations of the sentences also change.

This is also why "whether there is a commercial label" is only the starting point. Transparent labeling can let the audience know that there is a business relationship in the content, but it does not automatically answer whether the product claims have evidence. Conversely, a video with clear cooperation labels may still cross the line in medical device or food function claims. Verification work needs another layer: what category is the product, how does the supervisory authority define it, and are the sentences in the video experience sharing, advertising promises, or statements with medical effect implications.

The 2019 medical device case provides a systemic perspective. The Central News Agency reported that the health department not only examined whether the video had product images, but also examined the cooperation relationship, the video and Facebook posts, and the legal effect of the endorsement behavior. [^6] The English report of Taipei Times also placed it in the context of unapproved medical device advertising. [^7] This shows that platform content cannot be judged only by "the creator thinks they are sharing." The audience receives public content, and the supervisory authority must also deal with the function generated by public content in the market.

The 2021 vitamin incident pushed the focus to language itself. "1 bottle equals 12 bottles" is an easy-to-remember comparative sentence, but it also makes the audience question the basis, ingredients, and effects of the comparison. The Central News Agency reported the different levels of explanation from the TFDA and Taipei City Health Department, the former dealing with the norm that general food advertising must not be exaggerated or claim efficacy, and the latter investigating whether specific words involve exaggeration. [^8] This layer-by-layer confirmation is more precise than a sentence of "being questioned," and is closer to the way knowledge-based content should work.

### How Audiences Can Read a Science Communication Video

Evelyn Chen's experience can also be transformed into a reading method for the audience. When reading a video, first divide the sentences into several categories: facts that can be directly verified by research or official data, the creator's own experience, product function descriptions, and advice extended from facts. Different types require different strengths of evidence, and they should not be treated as the same type of content just because they are placed in the same editing rhythm.

Then look at what the video is talking about. If the content is about refrigerators, cleaning, or living habits, the cost of errors may be wasting time or money. If the content is about cancer screening, vitamins, sleep, or mental health, the cost of errors may involve delayed medical treatment, wrong purchases, or mistaking personal experience for universal treatment. The closer the topic is to medicine, the more the audience needs to distinguish between "sounds reasonable" and "proven by reliable sources."

Finally, investigate the video's transaction relationships. Whether the creator charges, whether the manufacturer provides the product, whether there is a crowdfunding goal, whether the service is packaged as a course, these conditions themselves do not directly determine the right or wrong of the content. But they will change the risk profile of the information. The audience does not need to treat all commercial cooperation as suspicious, nor should they treat all professional images as exempt from verification. A more mature way of reading is to look at content, evidence, interests, and the public explanation of the supervisory authority at the same time.

Digital Times reported that when "Consultation Notes" shifted from paid content to podcast, the focus was on how to re-arrange profit and original intention. [^9] This shift is worth noting, because free does not mean no value, and charging does not mean untrustworthy. What really matters is whether the product promises are clear, whether the professional boundaries are explained, and whether the audience has enough information to make their own choices. RTI's English report linked the course controversy to the accessibility of Taiwan's mental health services, reminding us that the content of individual creators cannot be used to fill the gap in institutional services. [^10]

### What She Leaves Behind Is Not "Internet Celebrity Success Studies"

Mirror Weekly once recorded her words under the pressure of going viral: "First accept that I can have emotions, first accept that I am not perfect, first accept that I can have pressure, and then deal with this thing." [^11] This statement belongs to her description of her own experience, and should not be packaged as a psychological therapy suggestion. Returning to the context of this article, it reminds us: creators are also learning how to bear attention, but the creator's self-adjustment cannot replace the professional review of the content itself.

Evelyn Chen's importance lies in the fact that her public experience includes fame, transformation, commercial cooperation, and controversy at the same time. She deserves to be included because she made a turning point of Taiwan's new media very clear: professional background can become the entry point of traffic, traffic can be transformed into brands, advertising, and courses, and each transformation redefines "why the audience believes you."

Her story also makes the word "science communication internet celebrity" more concrete. It is a public relationship jointly borne by creators and audiences, requiring continuous verification and explanation. Creators must check data, mark cooperation, explain uncertainties, and leave correction traces after finding errors. Audiences need to know that professional appearance does not necessarily lead to correct conclusions, and subscription numbers cannot replace medical qualifications.

When science communication becomes traffic, expertise does not lose its value. On the contrary, it needs more complete systems, transparency, and self-restraint to protect it. Creators should put the source in the script and description column, platforms should make the boundary between advertising and content easier to identify, and audiences should keep the habit of looking for original data. These tasks may seem slow, but they can reduce the cost of a single error being widely copied. Her experience leaves a more worth long-term questioning problem for Taiwan: Can knowledge creators and audiences work together to turn trust from a feeling into a set of verifiable habits?

### Leaving Corrections Behind, Only Then Can There Be Long-term Trust

The credibility of knowledge-based videos is often misunderstood as "looking complete when shooting." In fact, research will be updated, product specifications will change, and the supervisory authority may also add explanations after investigation. If creators only keep the initial conclusion and delete the previously erroneous segments, the audience will not know how a statement was corrected. Corrections should be regarded as part of the content life cycle, and they can also allow brands and audiences to see how the creator faces new evidence.

Evelyn Chen mentioned in an interview with a manager that science will move forward after errors are corrected. [^4] For new media, this statement can be turned into a very practical editorial process: keep the verification links in the script, mark the evidence strength of high-risk sentences, add update dates in the video description column, and save contracts and advertising labels for product cooperation. These tasks may not appear in the audience's view, but they determine whether the content can be traced after a dispute occurs. For Taiwan's new media, being able to point out where the data comes from, when it was updated, and who corrected it is itself a form of professional service to the public.

This process also helps avoid mixing the creator's personal opinions with the conclusions of the supervisory authority. This article separates the 2019 medical device penalty, the 2021 food advertising investigation, and the 2022 online course dispute into different sections, precisely because they involve different regulations, product types, and source levels. A person's article can have an opinion, but the opinion must be based on facts that can be traced back to the original text.
