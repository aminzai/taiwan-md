On June 17, 1874, Shen Baozhen arrived in Anping. Three months later, construction began on the Erkunshen Battery in Anping, Tainan. In 1876, the four characters "Eternal Golden Castle" were carved on the city gate.[^1] [^2] One man, one battery, and one road into the hinterland connected a stretch of late Qing Taiwan history rarely placed on the same map.

📝 **Curator's note:** What is most worth remembering about Shen Baozhen is not the few historic sites he left for Taiwan, but that he translated a diplomatic crisis into surveying, fortification, county establishment, and military roads. And every such translation changed the people on the land.

## How a Metropolitan Graduate Entered the Shipyard

Shen Baozhen was from Houguan, Fujian, and was Lin Zexu's nephew and son-in-law. This family connection is often written as a simple label, but the more critical turning point came in 1865: after his mother's death, he returned to Fuzhou for mourning observance, and Zuo Zongtang subsequently recruited him to head the Fuzhou Shipyard.[^9]

The Fuzhou Shipyard confronted Shen Baozhen with work different from traditional local administration. The shipyard needed to fit foreign engineers, warship manufacturing, surveying science, and new-style academies into a single system; the academies turned technical training into state capacity. Fuzhou Municipal Government local history records note that the Shipyard once ran academies and dispatched students to Britain and France to study steamship manufacturing and navigation. Shen Baozhen later brought this network of people and techniques to Taiwan.[^9] Cambridge University Press research places him in a core case of late Qing modernization: he turned what could have been a smooth official career toward building a modern naval shipyard and academies, but this path simultaneously contained the achievements of institutional innovation and the failures of insufficient resources.[^10]

This background changes how we view Shen Baozhen. What he brought to Taiwan was not just an imperial commissioner's order, but a whole method of thinking that broke coastal defense down into ships, guns, schools, maps, and talent.

## Why the Fuzhou Shipyard Minister Landed at Anping

Shen Baozhen was born in 1820 in Houguan, Fujian, passed the palace examination in 1847, and later served as Superintendent of the Shipyard, Imperial Commissioner for Taiwan Coastal Defense, and Governor-General of Liangjiang concurrently Minister of Nanyang Commerce.[^1] He was not a local official temporarily dispatched to Taiwan, but someone already familiar with shipyards, warships, academies, and surveying within the Qing's Self-Strengthening and coastal defense system.

The starting point goes back to 1871. The Ryukyu vessel _Shanyuan_ drifted to Taiwan's southeast; 66 crew members came ashore, 54 were killed, 12 were rescued and repatriated to Ryukyu by the Qing government. Three years later, Japan sent troops to the Hengchun Peninsula on the pretext of punishing the perpetrators.[^4] For the Qing court, this was not merely a local conflict. Japan turned "who has the right to govern this land" into an international issue; the Qing court's vague characterization of the mountains as "beyond the pale of civilization" suddenly no longer sufficed.

After Japanese troops landed at Hengchun in May 1874, Shen Baozhen proceeded to Taiwan as "Imperial Commissioner for Taiwan Coastal Defense and Minister for Various Nations' Affairs," arriving in Anping on June 17.[^4] His entourage also included the French instructor Yi Ge (日意格) from the Mawei Shipyard Academy and shipyard students. The subsequent maps, batteries, and coastal surveys were not the separate initiatives of a few officials, but the Shipyard's new technical system unfolding on the ground in Taiwan.[^4]

## From Local Conflict to International Law and Sovereignty Issue

The difficulty of the Mudan Incident lay not only in Japanese troops having already landed. Japan regarded the Ryukyuans killed in 1871 as Japanese subjects; the Qing court had to answer: did those lands in Taiwan's southeast not directly administered by Qing county yamens ultimately belong to territory the Qing could be responsible for? Cambridge research notes that the May–December 1874 Taiwan crisis erupted at the intersection of Japan's incorporation of Ryukyu, the Qing's vague territorial claims, and the Euro-American international law concept of "effective occupation."[^14]

Therefore, Shen Baozhen simultaneously played military commander and diplomatic negotiator. He had to secure Japanese troop withdrawal, and also enable the Qing court afterward to explain how it governed Taiwan. The subsequent county establishment, fortification, surveying, and roads were all administrative answers to this question. They gradually pulled places once called "beyond the pale" into the range the Qing could describe and claim.[^4] [^14]

## A Battery Stands on the Shore First

The first concrete problem Shen Baozhen faced in Anping was that the Taiwan Prefecture capital's sea entrance lacked sufficient modern defense. Tainan Municipal Government records state that the Erkunshen Battery used bricks from Fort Zeelandia, built with Western-style red brick, surrounded by a moat, completed in 1876, and armed with five British-made Armstrong 18-ton muzzle-loading guns.[^2]

The battery's significance lay not only in gun weight. It elevated "the Taiwan Prefecture capital must be held" from a local garrison matter to a coastal defense project the Qing court had to invest in. At the same time, it compressed French engineering knowledge, British artillery, and Fuzhou Shipyard talent into a visible structure at Anping.[^4]

📝 **Curator's note:** Eternal Golden Castle is like a late Qing receipt: the court finally acknowledged Taiwan could not be guarded by the old yingxun system alone, but this receipt also reminds us that the state often only begins paying the cost for a piece of land when crisis presses close.

## The Qing Did Not Rely Only on Local Arms

When crisis pressed, Taiwan's existing weapons and troops did not match the imagination of "modernized coastal defense." Late Qing research notes that among the Taiwan garrison some used matchlocks, and Taiwan militia even fought with bows, arrows, and spears. Li Hongzhang recommended transferring Huai Army commander Tang Dingkui's unit; the elite troops that ultimately reached Taiwan numbered about 6,500.[^14]

Shen Baozhen also requested in 1874 to purchase 15,000 Remington breech-loading rifles, and planned to procure ironclads, rifles, artillery, and ammunition through foreign loans. This added a layer of hard-to-see background to Eternal Golden Castle's red brick walls: it was not a local project completed behind closed doors, but the result of the global arms market, foreign banks, the Fuzhou Shipyard, and Qing finances all connecting simultaneously.[^14]

The Qing court thus learned a very practical lesson in the Taiwan crisis: shipyards could be built domestically, weapons could be attempted for replication, but when war truly broke out, speed, quality, and cash often still had to be sought from the world. Shen Baozhen's coastal defense thinking acquired its global dimension from here.[^14]

## A Map Turns Coastline into National Border

After Shen Baozhen arrived in Taiwan, Mawei Shipyard Academy personnel began Western-style surveying. Indigenous historical documents record that Yi Ge participated in Anping Battery recommendations and guided shipyard students Wei Han and others to produce the _Map of Taiwan Prefecture City and Anping Sea Entrance_ and _Complete Map of Taiwan Prefecture City Streets_. Yan Fu also accompanied the _Yangwu_ to survey Taiwan's southeast sea entrances.[^4]

What these people did looked like technical work at first glance, but actually rewrote governance methods. Maps placed harbors, roads, garrison posts, government offices, and settlements into a single coordinate system, enabling the Qing court to explain where the county was, where the sub-prefecture was, where troops should be stationed, and turning "Taiwan" from an island needing defense into an administrative space that could be measured, zoned, and managed.[^4]

![1875 Qing-era Taiwan administrative map](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/1875%20Taiwan.svg)

_Figure: 1875 Qing-era Taiwan administrative division map. Author: Liaon98; source: [Wikimedia Commons file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:1875_Taiwan.svg), licensed [CC BY-SA 3.0 Taiwan](https://creativecommons.org/licenses/by-sa/3.0/tw/deed.en)[^8]._

After 1875, new administrative units such as Hengchun County, Beinan Sub-prefecture, and Puli Sub-prefecture appeared successively in the Qing's governance plans. Related survey results later left over 2,000 place names, becoming important historical materials for studying late Qing front-mountain, back-mountain, and administrative expansion.[^4]

English-language scholarship places this turn in a larger East Asian context: Taiwan's position in Qing eyes was both a maritime frontier and a potential base for hostile forces. Therefore, coastal defense and administration were not two parallel lines, but two faces of the same national security imagination.[^5]

## The Hinterland Was Not a Mineral List Awaiting Development

In the _Memorial on Comprehensive Planning for the Overall Taiwan Situation_, Shen Baozhen and colleagues wrote the rationale for "opening the mountains" quite directly:

> "For the sake of preventing trouble, not for the sake of profit."[^11]

This sentence deserves to be read back in context. The memorial acknowledged that rumors of gold sand, silver mines, and timber in the hinterland might not yield sufficient revenue; the real reason they could not stop work was that Taiwan was seen as Fujian's left screen and the gateway to seven provinces. The north road had already reached Xiuguluan, the south road had already passed through Beinan, and the middle road was prepared to cross Shuangshan eastward.[^11] The three roads were therefore not merely transportation projects, but military deployments pushing the coastal defense line into the mountains.

This statement also corrects the later common "roads built for development" narrative. Shen Baozhen himself put prevention before profit. Roads, settlers, and administrative establishment had to first let the Qing court enter, before discussing products and tax revenue. This security logic turned the hinterland into space the state had to control, and also placed Indigenous peoples' living domains into military and administrative coordinates.[^4] [^11]

## Hengchun City and the Other Side of "Opening the Mountains and Pacifying the Aborigines"

After Shen Baozhen inspected Langqiao, he memorialized to approve city construction and county establishment, renaming Langqiao as Hengchun, and planning Hengchun City. National Taiwan Museum of Fine Arts collection records note that Hengchun City began construction in 1875 and was completed in 1879.[^1] For the Qing court, this was a method of fixing the southern coast onto the administrative map. For the communities living there, walls, roads, and government offices meant new armies, tax systems, and orders had arrived.

The Qing then promoted "Opening the Mountains and Pacifying the Aborigines": north, middle, and south roads simultaneously extended into the hinterland, and Indigenous communities were handled through pacification, household registration, administrative establishment, and military action. Indigenous studies research notes that post-Mudan Incident maps and administrative establishments indeed let the state system penetrate deeper into the Hengchun Peninsula and eastern Taiwan; but the same process accompanied the weakening of Indigenous autonomy, and surviving records are almost all texts and maps left by external regimes.[^4]

Here we need to invert the common saying. People often understand "Opening the Mountains and Pacifying the Aborigines" as the starting point of road opening, local development, and modern construction, but roads could often be traversed precisely because armies entered first, administration landed first, and land originally grasped by different communities was renamed and rearranged. Shen Baozhen's governance projects therefore simultaneously possessed two faces: they let the Qing court begin to take responsibility for Taiwan, and also made Indigenous peoples bear the pressure of state expansion.[^4]

## For Whom Was the Ban Lifted

"Opening the mountains" without settlers would see roads re-blocked by wild grass; but to recruit settlers, the Qing first had to handle the old bans on people crossing to Taiwan and entering aboriginal territory. In his 1875 memorial, Shen Baozhen listed multiple restrictions, including prohibiting mainlanders from sneaking across, restricting Taiwan civilians from entering aboriginal territory, and penalties for rattan gathering, deer hunting, logging, and palm harvesting. The memorial's tone was pragmatic: "If you want to open the mountains but do not first recruit settlers, then though the road is open it remains blocked; if you want to recruit settlers but do not first lift the bans, then the people will bind their feet and not advance."[^12]

He simultaneously requested relaxing the bans on iron and bamboo, on the grounds that these restrictions easily became tools for corvée levies to extort the people.[^12] This is a side of Shen Baozhen's policy often overlooked: he did indeed try to weaken certain old systems'束縛 on merchants and settlers, but this "lifting of bans" primarily served state recruitment, transport, and defense goals, and did not equal recognition of Indigenous autonomy over land.

## Using a Zheng Chenggong Shrine to Place Taiwan in Loyalty History

Shen Baozhen also handled a quieter but politically weighty project in Taiwan: arranging who could represent Taiwan's history. On January 12, 1875, he memorialized to request a posthumous title for Zheng Chenggong and establishment of a dedicated shrine in the Taiwan Prefecture, writing in the memorial: "Humbly request bestowal of posthumous title and shrine construction, to follow popular sentiment and clarify the great righteousness."[^13]

This request moved Zheng Chenggong from local private worship into the Qing's official sacrificial canon, and re-translated Taiwan's Ming-Zheng memory into a loyalty story the Qing could accept. Shen Baozhen was not only building batteries on the coast; he was simultaneously seeking in temples, posthumous titles, and sacrificial systems a language that could connect Taiwan's popular sentiment to the Qing political order. This cultural policy operated together with adding prefectures and counties and building roads, making governance rely not only on armies but also on who was allowed to become a hero in collective memory.[^13]

## The Blank Left by the Beijing Convention

On October 31, 1874, China and Japan signed the _Beijing Convention_. The Qing agreed to compensate the victims' families and reimburse Japan for road-building, house-building, and other expenses in Taiwan; Kenting National Park Headquarters materials estimate the relevant amount at about 500,000 taels of silver.[^4] Japanese troops withdrew, the Qing temporarily avoided war, and Shen Baozhen's military-political mission obtained a result he could report to the court.

But the agreement's text also left problems. Indigenous documents cite the Qing's then-statement to the British envoy: "Although these people are not governed by Chinese law, their land ultimately does not fall outside Chinese soil."[^4] This sentence stated sovereignty clearly, but did not ask how Indigenous peoples understood their own land, nor did it place them at the negotiating table. The subsequent county establishment, surveying, and roads continued forward in this blank.

📝 **Curator's note:** The hardest part of Shen Baozhen's story lies in that we cannot only write him as a "modernization pioneer," nor only reduce him to an executor of expansion; the true historical weight hides in the same city simultaneously protecting a government, redrawing a land, and changing the options of the land's original inhabitants.

## He Did Not Live to See Taiwan Become a Province, But Wrote the Reasons for Provincial Administration First

Shen Baozhen died in 1879; Taiwan became a province in 1885.[^1] [^16] He did not personally witness the formal establishment of the Taiwan Governorship system, but in the _Memorial on Comprehensive Planning for the Overall Taiwan Situation_ he had already discussed "in the future there will certainly be discussion of dividing the province to specialize responsibility," while reminding of Taiwan and Fujian's mutual dependence in military revenue, talent, rice, and shipyard supply.[^11]

This is not a simple straight line of "Shen Baozhen planned, Liu Mingchuan completed." Cambridge research notes that Shen Baozhen and Zuo Zongtang in the 1870s both used Chinese historical frontier governance experience to argue that Taiwan and Xinjiang needed to change governance methods; they placed reclamation, administration, and defense in the same political-economic problem.[^15] Later Taiwan provincial establishment continued this state imagination that bound land, tax revenue, population, and military responsibility together.

## One Memorial Does Not Equal All of Taiwan

The memorials Shen Baozhen left are very important because they let us hear how the Qing court explained "opening the mountains," "lifting bans," and "protecting Taiwan." But memorials remain the government's voice; Indigenous documents remind us that the maps and administrative archives left by the Mudan Incident and subsequent governance were mostly drawn by external regimes, and Indigenous peoples' own records are extremely scarce.[^4]

So reading Shen Baozhen cannot only ask how much construction he did, but must also ask: in whose language were these constructions used to name the land, by whose laws were residents handled, by whose maps was sovereignty proven? The "development" and "frontier governance" spoken of in late Qing studies, when placed back on the ground at Hengchun, Beinan, and the hinterland, simultaneously contain defense, migration, taxation, and community conflict.[^15]

## After the Battery Remained

Standing today before Eternal Golden Castle's gate, what is easiest to see are the red bricks, the moat, and the four characters "Eternal Golden Castle." Harder to see is how that new order operated together after 1874: the Fuzhou Shipyard sent technology to Anping, the Qing turned coastal defense into national responsibility, Hengchun City brought the southern tip into county administration, and roads and maps turned the hinterland into space describable by administrative organs.[^2] [^3] [^4]

Shen Baozhen's legacy is therefore not just a battery. It is a question: when the state finally became willing to see Taiwan, did it see coasts, harbors, roads, and manageable boundaries, or did it also see the people who had already been living in these places?

The 1874 crisis has long ended[^4]; Eternal Golden Castle remains in Anping's tree shadows, Hengchun City still has walls to follow. What has truly not ended is how we read those remaining bricks, roads, and maps: they prove Shen Baozhen changed Taiwan, and also force us to acknowledge that change never happened only inside government offices.
