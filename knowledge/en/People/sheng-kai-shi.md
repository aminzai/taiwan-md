# Chef Cai: From Steamed Egg Shorts to Frozen Lunar-New-Year Meals — The Trust Cost of a Cooking Influencer

> **30-second abstract:** Huang Shang-kai (Chef Cai) dropped out of school at sixteen and entered the catering trade; at twenty-four he scraped together savings and credit to open his first Mijia Japanese restaurant in Yonghe, New Taipei. Later, a roughly one-hundred-second steamed-egg tutorial video turned him from restaurant owner into a viral cooking influencer, and eventually stitched his home-cooking videos, restaurant, frozen foods and e-commerce into a single brand. What is truly worth remembering is not the feel-good headline of "child chef becomes CEO," but that when kitchen craft is amplified into a commodity, trust is amplified along with it.[^1] [^2] [^3]

## Learn to Wash Dishes First, Then Learn to Be Believed

Chef Cai's story does not begin on the internet. A feature interview in Weekly Cordley records that while he was at Songshan Industrial and Commercial High School he disliked studying, and at sixteen he left school to work in a Chinese banquet hall washing dishes. When business was busy he could work sixteen hours a day, first trading time for experience, then following a master into Japanese cuisine. This experience is not merely a "grit" backdrop; it also explains why, when he later made cooking content, he was not simply demonstrating dishes on camera but drawing on an understanding of ingredients, processes, plating and customer expectations from the catering floor.[^2]

At twenty-four he used savings and credit to raise about two million yuan and open the first Mijia Japanese restaurant in Yonghe, New Taipei. Reports record that the storefront had only sixteen seats, and in the early days monthly revenue was about four hundred thousand yuan with a profit margin of roughly one-tenth. To decide on a menu style he also observed the surrounding community demographics, choosing the more creative omakase sushi format rather than copying the traditional Taiwanese-Japanese cuisine of neighboring shops.[^2]

One easily overlooked detail of this entrepreneurial history: Chef Cai did not open the restaurant after gaining followers; he first accumulated a feel for cost, customer base and product in a physical catering business. The traffic the videos later brought was not starting from zero but landing on an already existing food brand.[^1] [^2]

📝 **Curator's note:** "Dish-washing kid becomes billion-yuan CEO" is a handy news headline but not a complete portrait of the person. Attributing success to hardship obscures more practical abilities: identifying a customer base, testing products, controlling processes, and knowing when to translate restaurant experience for the home kitchen.

## One Steamed-Egg Video Changed How We Watch

Chef Cai began making videos not because of a polished media plan. A friend asked him why, if he could cook, he only made Japanese cuisine; this prompted him to turn what he did in the restaurant into demonstrations ordinary families could understand. An interview with the global food-trend outlet points out that he deliberately used pots and heat sources common in home kitchens, simplifying the process so viewers could actually try it themselves.[^1]

What really made the channel explode in views was the roughly one-hundred-second "Transform Your Ugly Steamed Egg." Both Weekly Cordley and Mirror Weekly treated this video as the turning point. After the video went viral, Chef Cai discovered from fan comments that techniques professional chefs take for granted are not necessarily familiar to many families, and he subsequently launched the "Mijia Home Cooking Classroom."[^2] [^3]

The format of this channel is close to a miniature version of a family kitchen. Ingredients can be sourced from markets or convenience stores, cookware need not be restaurant-grade, video length is usually short, and demonstrations never pretend the viewer owns professional equipment. The value of cooking content thus shifted from "looks impressive when you watch" to "you dare to try after watching."[^1] [^2]

Chef Cai also treats authenticity as a signature. In an interview he said that rice grains flying out while stir-frying and ingredients dropping while plating are things that can happen in any home kitchen, and there is no need to delete them all for the sake of a perfect shot. This choice makes him less like a chef who only shows finished dishes and more like someone translating restaurant techniques back to home conditions.[^1]

## From Cooking Videos to Food Brands

When "Mijia Home Cooking Classroom" accumulated a large following, Chef Cai did not treat it as merely an advertising billboard; he extended the cooking techniques shown in the content into products. Weekly Cordley reports that he incorporated frozen and shelf-stable foods, cookware and e-commerce channels into his business portfolio, and at one point planned to take the food products to the Macau casino market.[^2]

An interview with the global food-trend outlet makes this transformation even clearer: a restaurant is limited by seats, location and on-site staff, whereas a food brand can use freezing, shelf-stable formats, retail and home delivery to bring the cooking experience that only happens inside a shop to the consumer's home. This is a shift from "cooking one dish" to "designing a product that can be mass-produced."[^1]

This is also where cooking influencers differ from ordinary catering celebrities. A master chef's influence can stop at restaurant reservations and media exposure, but a cooking influencer can stitch together demonstrations, comments, products and home delivery into a single consumption path. Viewers first learn a dish, then buy a pot or a meal kit, and finally treat "Chef Cai says this dish works" as a preliminary quality guarantee. Content thus ceases to be merely content and becomes the front door of product trust.[^1] [^2]

📝 **Curator's note:** Traffic is not proof of product quality. It can make a new product visible faster, but it cannot replace recipes, labeling, preservation, logistics and quality control. Cooking videos build expectations; food brands must deliver on them with actual products.

## The Lunar-New-Year Meal Incident: When Video Demonstrations Meet the Delivered Box

The 2020 lunar-new-year meal controversy is the most important body of material for understanding Chef Cai, because it makes the gap between content branding and food responsibility concrete. Mirror Weekly reports that consumers complained that the delivered meals differed from the promotions and video demonstrations, and some demanded refunds. Mijia apologized afterward, and Chef Cai sued the OEM manufacturer for compensation.[^5]

Another Mirror Weekly report records that Mijia claimed consumer refund amounts exceeded ten million yuan, while the OEM manufacturer argued that the recipe and cost of the meals should be understood jointly through the collaborative content, and counter-accused the promotional packaging of creating consumer expectations. Court rulings were reported as determining that part of the meal defects were the factory's responsibility, with the factory ultimately paying Mijia six hundred sixty thousand yuan in consumer refunds. Here the different levels must be separated: consumer complaints, the parties' claims in litigation, and the scope of responsibility that a court ruling can confirm are not the same thing.[^6]

In a response carried by Ettoday, Chef Cai apologized again for the year's meal shortcomings, and also pointed out that part of the costs listed in the judgment were not the full production costs, adding that the case would still be handled by lawyers. NOWnews interviewed the meal evaluators and suppliers, discussing the dishes, portions, logistics, frozen storage and marketing costs, and pointed out that price reasonableness cannot be judged by a single cost figure.[^4] [^7]

What is most worth preserving from this incident is not the easily circulated label of "influencer selling sky-high-margin lunar-new-year meals," but the way brand responsibility is broken down into concrete issues: whether the dishes in the promotion match the delivered product, how OEM and brand parties conduct acceptance, how consumers are refunded, who monitors logistics and preservation, and whether the creator's face and voice generate a higher level of trust than ordinary products.[^5] [^6] [^4] [^7]

## The Reality of a Cooking Influencer Is Not Just a Camera Style

Chef Cai's content emphasizes minimal retouching, and this "authenticity" is useful in cooking tutorials. It lets viewers see the constraints of a home kitchen and lowers beginners' fear of failure. But when the same person begins selling frozen foods, authenticity cannot stay limited to rice grains and wok-scraper sounds in the video; it must enter the product's specifications, preservation and after-sales procedures.

This is the most easily overlooked turning point in cooking content. Viewers can tolerate a bloopers in a tutorial video, but they do not necessarily tolerate quality problems in a paid product. The former is a sense of closeness while watching; the latter is a responsibility in a transaction. The former makes people feel the creator is like themselves; the latter demands the brand prove it is more capable than an ordinary household at controlling food safety.[^1] [^5] [^6]

Mirror Weekly also reports that after the lunar-new-year meal controversy, Chef Cai continued to launch frozen and shelf-stable foods, cookware and catering brands. This does not mean the controversy did not happen, nor that the brand has been forgiven by everyone. It only shows that the recovery of an influencer brand is not completed by a single apology, but must be continuously tested in the next product, the next explanation and the next consumer experience.[^3]

📝 **Curator's note:** Chef Cai's case should not be written as a simple success or failure. More worth watching is how the same set of "down-to-earth" language simultaneously builds trust and raises the degree to which a brand is expected, questioned and held accountable.

## He Turned the Kitchen into Media, and Brought Media Back to the Dining Table

Chef Cai's place in Taiwan's influencer history is not determined only by follower count or catering business scale. He made a very concrete shift visible: the expertise of a restaurant, after being translated through short videos, can enter a family kitchen, and then, through commoditization, return to the consumer's dining table.

This path looks natural, but every step changes the burden of responsibility. A restaurant chef mainly faces on-site diners; a cooking-tutorial creator faces viewers; a food brand faces consumers in payment, preservation, delivery and returns. Chef Cai walked from the kitchen onto a platform, and then from the platform toward a food brand, effectively placing himself inside three different evaluation systems.[^1] [^2] [^6]

Therefore, writing about Chef Cai cannot be limited to "he knows how to eat" or "he knows how to sell." What is truly interesting is that he put the craftsmanship of the catering industry, the intimacy of social-media videos and the scalability of food products on the same table. That table can be very lively, and it can also fall suddenly silent when quality problems arise. The more successful the brand, the less it can rely on the creator's personal charisma alone; it must let viewers see how the product is made, accepted and corrected.

## Conclusion: After One Steamed-Egg Video, There Is an Entire Chain of Responsibility

Chef Cai's story begins with one steamed-egg video, but it should not stop at "overnight fame." He first accumulated craftsmanship on the catering floor, then lowered the threshold with home-cooking videos, and finally turned viewing into restaurant, food and digital-commerce brands. This is a distinctly Taiwan-style influencer path, because it puts street-side catering, family kitchens, social-media platforms and festival consumption into the same business cycle.

But when content becomes a product, the creator's voice is no longer just a recommendation. It participates in the consumer's imagination of portion, quality and fair price. The lunar-new-year meal incident reminds us that what is hardest for an influencer brand to maintain is not exposure, but the distance between promise and reality. Chef Cai is worth recording not because he offers a universally copyable get-rich template, but because his case asks the question clearly: **When the audience believes in a person's craftsmanship, what proof must the brand offer that this belief has not been wasted?**[^5] [^6] [^4]
