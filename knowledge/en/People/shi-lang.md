> **30-Second Overview:** In July 1683, Shi Lang led Qing forces to defeat the Zheng naval army in Penghu. In August of that year, Zheng Ke-wei surrendered, and it was not until 1684 that the Qing court incorporated Taiwan into an administrative order of one prefecture and three counties.[^2] [^7] Shi Lang's role is difficult to conclude as merely a "hero" or a "traitor": he was a former general of the Zheng clan, a commander in the Qing army, and the author of memorials advocating for Taiwan's retention, but the Qing court's decision to keep Taiwan was not made by him alone. [^5]

Shi Lang was born in 1621 and died in 1696. Between these two years, he served as a subordinate to Zheng Zhilong, later joining Zheng Chenggong. Subsequently, he sided with the Qing court, becoming the commander of the Fujian naval forces tasked with attacking Taiwan. The data from the National Taiwan Museum writes this turning point briefly, yet it is precisely what makes it striking: a person's military capability did not change; what changed was the regime to which he pledged allegiance and his reasons for viewing the Zheng clan.[^1]

### He Operated Within an Overseas Regime

What Shi Lang joined in his youth was not a force sustained solely by land fortifications. The Zheng forces were structured around shipping routes between the Fujian coast, Kinmen, Xiamen, and Taiwan; fleets, ports, trade, and armies were interconnected. Zheng Chenggong established the Zheng regime based in Taiwan in 1661 during his invasion of Taiwan, which was under Dutch rule. Shi Lang fought within this maritime network, knowing how the fleets moved and understanding why Penghu was more than just a small dot on the map.[^1] [^2]

After Zheng Chenggong's death in 1662, the Zheng regime still controlled Taiwan, but its footholds along the Fujian coast gradually diminished, increasing military and financial pressure. Research from the Ministry of National Defense points out that the Qing court simultaneously maintained its navy while absorbing surrendered Zheng generals and sailors through appeasement. Internally, the Zheng forces faced power struggles, difficulties in maintaining arms, and declining morale. Shi Lang's later victories cannot be attributed solely to his personal decisive actions; they must also be placed within the context of long-term exhaustion by both regimes.[^2]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The story of Shi Lang is not one of a person suddenly becoming powerful, but rather an overseas regime gradually losing its most specialized sea.

### Carrying Family Grudges Across the Strait After Zeng De Was Killed

Shi Lang’s rupture with Zheng Chenggong is often understood as a military disciplinary conflict. Zheng Chenggong believed that Shi Lang violated orders when he dealt with his subordinate, Zeng De. As the conflict escalated, Shi Lang's father, Shi Daxuan, and his brother, Shi Xian, were killed, forcing Shi Lang to escape and eventually join the Qing army. This past cannot be merely treated as background for a "famous general changing sides"; it turned the later Penghu Sea Battle into a national war carrying personal wounds.[^1] [^2]

Here is a detail often obscured by heroic narratives: Shi Lang did not switch allegiance after the natural end of the Zheng regime, but while Zheng Chenggong was still alive and the Zheng forces still controlled Taiwan. To the Qing court, he brought not just military strength, but also intelligence and experience from within the enemy's ranks. For the Zheng clan, his surrender was like handing over someone who knew their secrets to the opposing army.[^1] [^2]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** What history is hardest to process is not complex allegiance, but when personal grievances are taken up by the state and wear the uniform of justice.

What the Qing court valued was precisely this surrendered general who was familiar with the Zheng navy and the Taiwan seas. Shi Lang was promoted to Commander of the Fujian naval forces in 1681 and advocated for an attack on Taiwan. However, the Qing army could not cross the sea simply by receiving orders. Military research from the Ministry of National Defense indicates that the Qing court first went through appeasement, navy preparation, shipbuilding, and logistical planning before gradually transforming its initial disadvantage in naval warfare into a condition capable of launching an expedition.[^1] [^2]

### July 8th: The Qing Army First Targeted Penghu as a Gate

Shi Lang's true first target was not Tainan, but Penghu. Penghu is located between Fujian and the main island of Taiwan; it serves as a transit point for crossing the sea and as an outpost for the Zheng regime. Controlling it meant removing the lock on the western gate of Taiwan first. The Qing court adopted the strategy of taking Penghu first not because the strait suddenly became easy, but because Shi Lang placed geography, logistics, and Zheng army morale onto the same operational map.[^2]

On July 8, 1683, the Qing forces departed from Tongshan Island. After arriving in the waters of Penghu on July 10, Shi Lang led his troops to attack the Mazu area. The first engagement was not successful; tides and winds trapped the Qing vanguard, and the Zheng army had an opportunity to surround the Qing ships, during which Shi Lang was wounded. The Penghu Sea Battle was not a straight line of "Qing forces crushing all," but a war where the sea conditions and terrain spoke first.[^2]

The map places Taiwan and Penghu in the same field of view, which helps explain why Shi Lang viewed Penghu as the initial move. The coastline of the main island of Taiwan is long; if the Zheng army dispersed its main forces evenly, any location could have become a landing point for Qing troops. If they concentrated their main force in Penghu, it meant staking a war on the batteries, harbors, and logistics of an outlying island. The Zheng army's choice to fight decisively in Penghu was a rational arrangement given limited forces, but it also made the consequences of losing it more concentrated.[^2] [^7]

On July 11, Shi Lang reorganized at Bazaodao Island. Subsequently, Qing forces captured Huju Island and Tongpan Island, gradually shortening the distance to Penghu port. On the morning of July 16, the Qing army launched a general assault, advancing in three directions and breaking the Zheng formations with the "Five Plum Blossom Formation," which concentrated firepower on a single target.[^2] The Zheng forces originally had artillery batteries and coastal fortifications for support, and their ship numbers were not necessarily overwhelmingly inferior. What truly changed the situation was Shi Lang's tactical adjustment after the initial setbacks, allowing the Qing army to turn scattered fire into sharp points capable of crushing the opponent.[^2]

### What Was Left After Penghu Fell?

After the defeat at Penghu, Liu Guoxuan retreated with the remaining forces back to Taiwan, and the Zheng regime did not have another decisive naval battle capable of turning the tide. Zheng Ke-wei surrendered in August 1683, and Shi Lang entered Taiwan; this moment marks the end of Zheng rule, but it does not mean the Qing court had already decided how to manage Taiwan next.[^1] [^2]

This difference is crucial. Capturing Penghu was a military issue; accepting Zheng Ke-wei's surrender was a transfer of regime; deciding whether to keep Taiwan, establish prefectures and counties, and station troops was an imperial governance issue. Reading this as the single phrase "Shi Lang recaptured Taiwan" obscures the debate that truly changed Taiwan's system.[^5] [^7]

### A Memorial That No Single Person Could Wield

After Zheng Ke-wei’s surrender, Shi Lang did not immediately receive a universally agreed-upon answer. The Qing court internally debated whether to keep Taiwan or evacuate the residents and troops, returning it to the sea winds. On December 22, 1682 (in the 22nd year of Kangxi), Shi Lang submitted the _Memorial on Abandoning or Retaining Taiwan_ (_Gongchen Taiwan Qi Liu Shu_), arguing that Taiwan could not be lightly abandoned based on geography, population, resources, and coastal defense.[^3] [^4]

In his memorial, he wrote: "A fertile region, a rugged territory." This was not a slogan condensed by later generations, but an original statement from when he personally visited Taiwan and argued the land and coastal defense together. He further wrote: "To defend Taiwan is to secure Penghu; Taiwan and Penghu are secured together in one defense." In Shi Lang's strategic imagination, Taiwan was not an isolated overseas territory, but a depth that could be defended by Penghu.[^3]

The memorial contained another layer worth noting. Shi Lang discussed not only military matters but also the people, land, and livelihoods remaining in Taiwan: if all people were evacuated, they would face limited ships, large populations, and unemployment; if Taiwan was abandoned, maritime powers might find a foothold again. This shows a general writing a governance proposal for the court, not just seeking personal glory.[^3]

This memorial is powerful, but it cannot be reduced to the single myth of "Shi Lang convincing Kangxi with one letter." Research by Huang Youwei, comparing records like the _Qiju Zhu_ (Daily Records), points out that the Qing court may have already decided to govern Taiwan before Shi Lang's first submission advocating for retention; Kangxi controlled the pace of governance, and the political operations of officials like Manzhu and Mingzhu also contributed to the final consensus. Shi Lang's memorial was important, but "proposing reasons" and "completing the decision" are two different things.[^5]

This correction changes how we view Shi Lang. He is not a hero who single-handedly moved Taiwan from "abandoned" to "retained," but a general who provided firsthand experience of conquering Taiwan, strategic language, and governance proposals within the established direction of the court. In this light, his merits were not erased; they were placed within the political system rather than being above it.

### After One Prefecture and Three Counties Took Up Arms

The victory in Penghu in 1683 ended the Zheng regime; the establishment of one prefecture and three counties in 1684 marked when the Qing court began treating Taiwan as a spatial administrative unit. Administrative evolution data from the Academia Sinica records that in the 23rd year of Kangxi, Taiwan Prefecture was established, belonging to the Provincial Inspection Office of Fujian Province (Bimbai Dao), and governed the three counties of Taiwan, Fengshan, and Zhulu, while also including the Thirty-Six Penghu Islands.[^7]

One prefecture and three counties is not a neutral administrative chart. It incorporated the prefectural city of Taiwan, the southern plains, and the lands north of Zhulu, along with Penghu, into a single bureaucratic language; it reconfigured the space that was originally operated by Zheng military administration, local communities, and maritime merchants into an administrative unit that the Qing court could tax, garrison, and mobilize. Behind this administrative construction is how power reached the villages and ports again.[^6] [^7]

But mapping out administrative divisions does not mean the Qing court understood the island. Research from the Palace Museum indicates that after defeating Zheng Ke-wei and going through the debate over abandonment or retention, the Qing court adopted a relatively passive governance of Taiwan; it only gradually corrected its policy towards Taiwan when faced with internal and external threats. A fleet can change a regime in ten days, but it takes the bureaucracy longer to realize what it has taken control of.[^6]

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The speed of conquest often leads people to think governance is complete; in reality, only after the cannons fall silent does the system begin to reveal how unfamiliar it is.

### The Conquering General Also Became a Land and Sea Merchant

Shi Lang's influence in Taiwan was not limited to memorials or official posts. A summary of a lecture by Lin Yuru published in _Tainan 400_ describes him as an important landlord and maritime merchant in late 17th-century Taiwan: after the conquest, he acquired large amounts of land, intervened in sugar exports, and controlled trade between Taiwan, Penghu, and Xiamen under the guise of raising military pay. This aspect reminds us that victory in war reallocates not just official seals, but also land, commodities, and shipping routes.[^8]

This material comes from a lecture summary published by a local government, not original ledgers from Shi Lang's era, so it is suitable for posing questions rather than treating all land figures as absolute truth. It at least adds a necessary follow-up question to the narrative: after Shi Lang secured Taiwan for the Qing court, who did the sugar, land, and maritime traffic of Taiwan flow to?[^8]

Therefore, Shi Lang cannot be confined solely within the frame of a naval hero. To the Qing court, he was someone familiar with the seas and capable of completing the conquest; to the local society of Taiwan, he was a center of power in post-war resource redistribution. Military merit and governance disputes can coexist without needing to erase one another.

### Hero or Traitor: Who Needs Whom?

The reason Shi Lang's image has changed repeatedly is not because historical records suddenly disappeared, but because different eras select different versions of Shi Lang from the same body of records. English research by Ronald C. Po points out that official and public narratives in China often place Shi Lang within the heroic framework of pacifying Taiwan and completing national unification; while Taiwan has long viewed him as a former Zheng general who defected to the Qing court, or even an authoritarian ruler, with more re-evaluations appearing only after the 1990s.[^9]

Both "hero" and "traitor" have grasped part of the truth. Shi Lang did indeed defeat the Zheng naval forces in Penghu with Qing troops, and he did participate in Taiwan's governance as a Qing official; simultaneously, his defection and conquest are linked to the personal trauma of his family being killed, and the post-war land and trade power cast a shadow that local society could not ignore. Only by wiping away one side can we obtain a seemingly clean but actually unreliable Shi Lang.[^1] [^8] [^9]

Perhaps the most worthy aspect of Shi Lang is where he stood at intersections: the boundary between Ming and Qing, the strait between Fujian and Taiwan, the line between general and administrator, and the boundary between personal revenge and national war. He could end a regime in 1683, but he could not unilaterally decide whether an island should remain; he could change the ruler of Taiwan with a naval battle, but he could not dictate how future generations should remember him.
