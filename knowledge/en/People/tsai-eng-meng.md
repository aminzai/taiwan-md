On March 18, 2024, within the case files of the Taipei District Court in Taiwan, a question emerged that resembled an online argument but required a legal answer: Who can rewrite the Wikipedia entry for Tsai Ying-ming? The entry at the time listed controversial narratives regarding his media acquisitions, cross-Strait relations, and the Chinese government. Tsai Ying-ming argued that this had damaged his reputation and personality rights. [^1]

Tsai Ying-ming filed a civil lawsuit against the Taiwan Wikimedians Association and its former and current secretaries-general, requesting that the defendants tolerate his free editing of the "Tsai Ying-ming" entry in Chinese Wikipedia. The phrase "sued Wikipedia-related organizations" in this article precisely refers to this lawsuit against the Taiwan Wikimedians Association and other defendants; it does not treat the Taiwan Association, the Wikimedia Foundation, and the Chinese Wikipedia community as the same legal entity. [^1] [^2] [^3]

> **30-Second Overview:** Tsai Ying-ming is not the founder of the 1962 Yilan Food company; he entered his father's Yilan Food in the 1970s, later served as general manager, and shifted the food business, originally focused on processing and export, toward brand management. In 1979, "Wang Zai" expanded into the Taiwan domestic market; the Wang Wang official history records the brand's founding in 1983 and cooperation with Japan's Iwatsuka Seika to develop rice crackers. This path from family business succession and product transformation to a cross-border food group later extended to media acquisitions and reputation lawsuits; understanding Tsai Ying-ming cannot start from just one label of "pro-China" or "entrepreneur." [^10] [^11]

## The Daodaochang Family and Cinematic Childhood

Regarding Tsai Ying-ming's childhood, currently directly citable materials mainly come from the biographical pages and excerpts of financial writer Chang Dian-wen, rather than household registration, school files, or a complete verbatim interview with Tsai Ying-ming himself. Lianjing Publishing House, when introducing _The Heart in the Mouth: Tsai Ying-ming's Rise in Cross-Strait Wang Wang_, described him as the youngest son of the Tsai family, born in 1957 and raised in Daodaochang. The excerpts link his father, Tsai A-shih, with the Central Cinema and the film screening business. [^11]

These materials provide a specific childhood scene: in an era before television was widespread, family business was connected to Japanese films, Taiwanese Hokkien films, and cinema crowds. The excerpts also describe mother Tsai Chen-chao handling internal affairs in the family business, while the father was more responsible for external affairs. However, these contents belong to the narrative of biographical excerpts; this article does not treat all personality descriptions or family anecdotes among them as facts verified by independent archives. [^11]

Tsai Ying-ming's childhood is therefore unsuitable to be written as a simple "rich kid legend." A more prudent statement is: the publisher's excerpts place him in a family environment where he simultaneously engaged with family commerce, cinema culture, and Japanese popular imagery. This background can help readers understand his later emphasis on brands, entertainment, and consumer experience, but it cannot directly deduce his political stance or all decisions in adulthood. [^11]

## From Yilan Food to the Wang Wang Brand

Yilan Food was not an enterprise founded by Tsai Ying-ming. The Wang Wang official company history lists Yilan Food's founding year as 1962 and records Tsai Ying-ming becoming general manager in 1977. Lianjing Publishing House's pages, however, use 1976, when the 19-year-old Tsai Ying-ming went to Yilan and took over the Yilan Food factory, as the starting point of the character's entrepreneurial story. [^10] [^11]

These two statements can coexist but cannot be mixed. The former explains the company's history and the starting point of the legal entity's business; the latter explains when Tsai Ying-ming entered and began to assume operational responsibilities. For a character article, the most accurate sentence is not "Tsai Ying-ming founded Yilan Food," but "he entered and took control of Yilan Food in the 1970s, later transforming the family-inherited food business into the Wang Wang brand."

The Wang Wang official history records that in 1979, "Wang Zai" was born, beginning to expand into the Taiwan domestic market. In 1983, the Wang Wang brand was founded, cooperating with Japan's Iwatsuka Seika to develop the Taiwan rice cracker market. This was a key step from a factory oriented toward processing and export to one with its own brand, product identity, and consumer memory. [^10]

Therefore, if "founding an enterprise" is to be used for Tsai Ying-ming, it should precisely point to the Wang Wang brand and its corporate system that he later established and expanded, rather than rewriting Yilan Food, established in 1962, as his personal entrepreneurial achievement. This difference seems merely a matter of years and verbs, but it actually separates the father's inherited company, Tsai Ying-ming's operational succession, and his personal credit in establishing the brand.

On August 29 of that year, the court did not order anyone to fix the article for him. The main text of the judgment contained only one sentence: "The plaintiff's claim is dismissed." [^1]

This result is easily read as "the entrepreneur lost the lawsuit against Wikipedia." But what is truly worth leaving behind is the court's first step of separating four roles often confused together: the person writing, the administrators managing the entries, the Taiwan association promoting the Wiki projects, and the Wikimedia Foundation providing the website infrastructure.

📝 Curator's Note: This lawsuit superficially disputes a paragraph of text, but underneath, it disputes whether a public figure can bring their autobiography into an encyclopedia co-written by strangers.

## The Defendants Are Not the Owners of the Pen

Tsai Ying-ming's request was not simply asking a media outlet to publish a correction. He argued that after the Taiwan Wikimedians Association was established in 2003, it was responsible for promoting and managing multiple Wiki projects, and thus should tolerate his free editing of the controversial content in the Chinese Wikipedia "Tsai Ying-ming" entry. [^3]

The first-instance court first looked not at whether those sentences sounded good, but whether the Taiwan Wikimedians Association actually had substantive management rights. The judgment cited the association's articles of association, believing that the tasks listed in the articles, including promoting the Wikimedia Foundation's projects, promoting the concept of open collaboration, holding educational activities, and maintaining member rights, cannot lead to the inference that the association actually operates Chinese Wikipedia. [^1]

The court then examined Wikipedia's administrator system. Protecting or reverting pages, deleting or restoring pages, reverting edits, and handling disruptive behavior belong to administrator permissions. Administrators are generated by the Chinese Wikipedia community, not designated by the Taiwan Association. [^1]

This difference is subtle but determines the direction of the lawsuit. The association can promote Wiki projects in Taiwan and can assist controversial parties in finding editors to communicate. This does not mean it can command the global Chinese community to retain a specific version. The court even cited existing explanations indicating that what the association can do is closer to assisting communication or asking administrators to handle malicious destruction, rather than directly issuing editing commands for the entry. [^1]

Liberty Times's summary of the first-instance judgment also pointed out that the court believed it was difficult to determine that Chinese Wikipedia was operated and managed by the Taiwan Wikimedians Association; Tsai Ying-ming had edited the entry, but it was later reverted by website administrators, not restricted by the association. [^2]

## Tsai Ying-ming Could Actually Enter, but the Changes Did Not Stay

The most vivid detail in the judgment is a record of editing and reverting. Tsai Ying-ming was not completely unable to log in or write. He had modified the "Tsai Ying-ming" entry, and the modified content was later reverted by administrators. The court recorded that the reversion might involve reasons such as "destructive editing, content having advertising or promotional nature." [^1]

The legal issue here thus shifts from "who deleted my article" to "who has the right to decide if a version complies with encyclopedia rules." The court believed that this reversion showed that Tsai Ying-ming's editing rights were not turned off by the Taiwan Wikimedians Association. It also cannot guarantee that any version written by an editor will be permanently retained. [^1]

A September 2024 Wikinews report organized the four sets of controversial content claimed by the plaintiff as: acquiring Taiwan media to curry favor with the Chinese Communist Party; meeting with officials from the Taiwan Affairs Office after the acquisition; criticism by political figures regarding related transactions; and allegations of receiving Chinese funds and being penalized. The report simultaneously recorded the association's defense: actual content management is held by administrators generated by community voting, and Tsai Ying-ming's modifications were reverted by other editors according to rules. [^3]

These four sets of content did not need to have their truth defined sentence by sentence in the same lawsuit. The first instance first handled the attribution of responsibility: Tsai Ying-ming could not prove that the defendant association had substantive editing management rights, nor could he prove that the association had ever restricted his free editing; therefore, the request had no legal basis. [^1]

📝 Curator's Note: In an open-collaboration encyclopedia, "can I write" and "will my version be left" are two different things. The judgment in the Tsai Ying-ming case precisely separates these two things.

## The Second Instance Pushes the Issue to "Subject to Public Comment"

Tsai Ying-ming appealed the first-instance judgment. On July 22, 2026, the High Court of Taiwan dismissed the appeal, maintaining the result that Tsai Ying-ming lost; the case can still be appealed, so it should not be written as finally determined. [^4] [^5]

The reasoning in the second instance went a step further than the first. Liberty Times reported that text related to "currying favor with the Chinese Communist Party" had been deleted before the lawsuit or during the trial. The remaining content regarding the acquisition of the China Times Media Group, cross-Strait relations, and national security belongs to matters acceptable for public comment. [^4]

The High Court also noted that the entry attached source materials and notes. According to United Daily News's summary of the judgment, the court believed that this content did not violate Chinese Wikipedia's neutral point of view, verifiability, and defamation policies, nor did it exceed the scope of reasonable comment. [^5]

This is not the court declaring all Wikipedia content to be correct, nor is it the court endorsing those controversial political judgments. A more precise statement is: in this litigation request, Tsai Ying-ming did not prove that the defendant association was the subject with the right to delete and modify content. The second instance also believed that the remaining related narratives involved public figures, media acquisitions, and national security, possessing public commentability, and the article provided verifiable sources. [^4] [^5]

The World Journal's summary of the second instance also listed the content Tsai Ying-ming wanted to add to the entry, including Wang Wang Group sales spanning 59 countries and regions, donations to the Bao Diao (Protect Diaoyu Islands) group, corporate history, product flavors, and text criticizing the first-instance judgment. The court believed that these requests were mostly supplementing corporate and public welfare deeds, or even commenting on the court, rather than filling in reputation damage; therefore, they did not belong to appropriate remedies for reputation restoration. [^6]

This judgment creates a boundary between "correction" and "self-promotion." Public figures can certainly provide data and can request media or encyclopedias to examine errors. But putting a large amount of self-favoring content into the same request, the court will ask: Is this correcting errors, or is it striving for an image layout written by himself?

## The Platform, Association, and Foundation Are Not the Same Person

The case has another layer of network structure easily ignored. The Taiwan Wikimedians Association is a Taiwanese promotional and community organization. The website infrastructure of Wikipedia is maintained by the Wikimedia Foundation located in the United States. In its self-introduction, the Wikimedia Foundation states that it is responsible for the host and protecting projects like Wikipedia, but does not serve in an editing role on Wikipedia. Entries are jointly established by volunteers worldwide based on reliable sources and community policies. [^8]

This official statement and the logic of the first-instance judgment illuminate each other. The association is not an administrator, and the foundation is not the author of every line of text. The daily addition and deletion of entries fall within a community governance system composed of volunteers, administrators, policies, and talk pages. The court cannot automatically attribute all editing behavior to an organization just because it is related to Wikipedia. [^1]

In a 2025 legal analysis, the Open Culture Foundation placed this case within the larger issue of platform governance: Wikipedia is a non-profit knowledge project without advertisements, relying on volunteers and source collaboration, and cannot simply apply the liability model of social media giants. The analysis also reminds that if the law only looks at "content is on the website" without looking at how content is produced and who has control, public-interest open-source platforms may thereby bear incorrect liability. [^7]

This is commentary, not an extension of the conclusion from this case's judgment. The court handled Tsai Ying-ming's request against specific defendants. The Open Culture Foundation handles platform legal systems, safe harbors, and the safety of anonymous collaborators. Separating the two actually allows us to see clearly the public issues left by the case.

## Three Issues Left by the Tsai Ying-ming Case

The first issue is: Can public figures edit their own entries? Technically, Wikipedia allows people to participate in editing. In terms of governance, editing involving self-interest easily creates conflicts of interest. The party concerned knows their own experience best and has the most motivation to delete unfavorable information. A reasonable system should not prohibit him from providing data, but should not automatically elevate "the person's own statement" into the "encyclopedia version."

The second issue is: Does the existence of sources mean the content is not controversial? No. Reliable sources can support the verifiability of a text, but cannot exempt all tone, proportion, and inference from liability. This is why the second instance, in its judgment, simultaneously mentioned public commentability, data notes, encyclopedia policies, and the scope of reasonable comment, rather than just looking at whether the entry had links attached. [^4] [^5]

The third issue is: Who should the party concerned look for to handle it? The first-instance judgment points to a counter-intuitive but more system-compliant path than filing a lawsuit: return to edit history, talk pages, and community policies, finding the editors or administrators actually handling the entry. The Taiwan Association can assist in communication, but cannot guarantee that all global Chinese editors will accept a certain version of the text. [^1]

📝 Curator's Note: Power in the internet age is not only in "who can delete articles," but also in "who is mistakenly believed to be able to delete articles." A lawsuit lays the cost of this misconception bare on the court's judgment.

## Conclusion: A Judgment Does Not Write Tsai Ying-ming's Autobiography

Tsai Ying-ming moved from the Wang Wang food business into media ownership controversies; this story has already been handled by articles on Taiwan.md. This case occurred in another field: a public figure attempting to handle narratives about himself online, but encountering platform governance hierarchy issues in court. [^9]

Neither the first nor the second instance decided how Tsai Ying-ming's life should be recorded. The court only confirmed a few things: The Taiwan Wikimedians Association is not the substantive editing manager of Chinese Wikipedia. Tsai Ying-ming could edit, but his version was reverted by administrators. The second instance believed that the remaining content involved matters subject to public comment, and had sources and notes. The text he requested to add was not all about reputation restoration. [^1] [^4] [^5]

This ending will not satisfy everyone. Tsai Ying-ming may still believe his position was not presented fairly; the Wiki community must also face the tension between public figures, conflicts of interest, and the right to reputation. What is truly worth preserving is not the simple answer of "who won," but that next time someone wants to modify public knowledge, we can first ask clearly: Who holds the pen now?

## This Is Not Issuing a Liability Waiver for Either Side

Writing this case as Tsai Ying-ming lost and Wikipedia won is still too hasty. The court did not rule that all entry content is un-criticizable, nor did it say public figures can only accept unfavorable narratives. The court handled whether specific defendants had authority and whether specific requests were established. [^1] [^4]

Similarly, Wikipedia's "verifiability" is not a magic spell. The foundation's official statement indicates that entries should be presented based on reliable sources, fair proportion, and neutral point of view; volunteers will form consensus through discussion and divergence. This system requires sources and also requires continuous editorial judgment. [^8]

For Tsai Ying-ming, the viable path left by the litigation is not to turn the encyclopedia into an autobiography, but to propose sources for verifiable errors, allowing the community to discuss under open rules. For the Wiki community, it cannot treat his objections as noise just because the party concerned is wealthy, famous, or controversial. Every reversion should be able to return to policies, sources, and edit history. [^1] [^7]

This is also the reason this case is worth keeping on Taiwan.md: it is not only about Tsai Ying-ming, but also about how Taiwan handles a person, a legal request, and a knowledge space maintained jointly by strangers. When the three collide, the most important first step is not to take sides, but to first draw out the boundaries of power.
