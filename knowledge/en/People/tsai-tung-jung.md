> **30 Second Overview:** Tsai Tung-rong was born in Xinwen, Buidai Township, Chiayi County, in 1935. After moving to the United States in the 1960s, he became an independent movement figure abroad and served as the first chairman of FAPA in 1982. In 1990, upon returning to Taiwan, he brought "self-determination" from overseas advocacy onto the streets, into political parties, and before the Legislative Yuan; in 1997, he was involved in co-founding Formosa TV. After the referendum law was enacted in 2003, Taiwan finally had a legal entry point for referendums, but thresholds and review mechanisms also narrowed that door. Understanding Tsai Tung-rong requires seeing both what he opened and why he remained dissatisfied with the way it was opened.

# From Xinwen to a Return Ticket

On June 13, 1935, Tsai Tung-rong was born in Xinwen Township, Buidai County, Chiayi. The official biography preserved by the Legislative Yuan lists that he studied law at National Taiwan University (NTU), earned an M.A. in Political Science from the University of Tennessee, and a Ph.D. in Political Science from the University of Southern California, later teaching at City College of New York.[^1]

This educational trajectory easily paints him as just a resume, but it omits a more specific turning point: during his time at NTU, he was elected chairman of the Student Union; subsequently, due to political activities, he came under scrutiny from the Kuomintang government and left Taiwan. The New Taiwan Peace Foundation records that in June 1960, he participated in the Guanji Ling Conference to discuss overthrowing the KMT regime and Taiwanese independence.[^2] Later, he moved to the United States and could not return to Taiwan for over two decades.

"Not being able to go home" was not rhetoric for Tsai Tung-rong; it was a concrete political condition. The United States provided him space to teach, organize, and lobby, while also fixing him overseas. This distance later became his tool: he learned to translate the Taiwanese issue into language that the U.S. Congress understood, but he also clearly knew that the voice from abroad could never substitute for the vote of those inside Taiwan.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** Tsai Tung-rong’s starting point was not "returning to participate in politics," but first losing the right to return; his repeated questioning of whether the people could decide their future was also an inquiry into how a person excluded from the family home could send decision-making power back to Taiwan.

# A Timeline That Belongs to More Than Just a Biography

Tsai Tung-rong's political life spanned overseas organizations, mass movements, media creation, and legislative defense. By placing several years side-by-side, one can see that his work was not four disconnected résumés, but the same issue using different tools in different arenas: who could speak for Taiwan, and who could decide Taiwan's future?

The significance of this table is not in slicing the person into years, but in seeing how "overseas" and "domestic" translated each other. Lobbying during the FAPA period relied on organizations and Congress. The referendum movement after returning required streets, political parties, legislation, and voting. The tools changed, but the core issue did not.

# Learning to Speak Taiwan to Congress Overseas

In 1970, Tsai Tung-rong became the first chairman of the World Alliance for Taiwan Independence. In 1982, he co-founded the Formosan Association for Public Affairs (FAPA) and served as its first chairman. These two organizations had different functions: the former dealt directly with the organizational network of the Taiwanese independence movement, while the latter brought the interests of overseas Taiwanese and Taiwanese democracy into the public policy space of the United States.[^2] [^3]

The official commemorative text from FAPA states that Tsai Tung-rong, along with Peng Ming-min and Chen Tang-shan, were founding fathers of the organization, confirming his role as first chairman in 1982. This source proves the position and organizational relationship but cannot independently prove all subsequent diplomatic outcomes often attributed to him.[^3]

This distinction is important. The life of a politician is often compressed into a string of "founding, promoting, succeeding," but the achievements of an organization usually come from long-term collective work. What Tsai Tung-rong truly left was the ability to break down Taiwan's democratic predicament into individual issues that American politics could handle: human rights, immigration quotas, congressional resolutions, and international visibility.

After returning to Taiwan, he carried this methodology, but the target of lobbying changed. Members of Congress in Washington became legislators in Taipei. Overseas compatriots’ associations became street crowds. "Taiwan must be seen" became "Taiwanese people must be able to vote."

# 1990: Referendum Becomes an Organized Movement from a Slogan

The New Taiwan Peace Foundation records that Tsai Tung-rong returned to Taiwan in 1990 under the pretext of mourning, subsequently founding the Referendum Promotion Association and serving as its founding chairman. Through methods such as candlelight vigils, marches, and the 410 hunger strike, he transformed the referendum from an overseas claim into a domestic mass movement.[^2]

His biography is closely linked to this movement: official records of the Legislative Yuan list "Founding Chairman of the Referendum Promotion Association" among his experiences, and the verbatim record of the Legislative Yuan also retains his comments on the referendum movement, mentioning in 1990 the proposal to handle Taiwan's accession to the UN via a referendum.[^1] [^4]

At that time, Tsai Tung-rong was not just demanding a law. He needed to create a political common sense: major issues of Taiwan could not forever be decided only by the President, the legislature, or political parties. This is why his movement included marches, hunger strikes, proposals, and international advocacy—because he needed "the people ruling themselves" to become an action visible before the system existed.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The story of the referendum law often starts with articles; Tsai Tung-rong’s story reminds us that there was a body before the text: walking, fasting, standing on the street with signs—all were rehearsals for a system that did not yet exist.

# Formosa TV: Another Way to Put People into Media

Tsai Tung-rong's political practice was not limited to ballots. The official history of Formosa TV records that in 1994, two teams applying to be the fourth radio television station merged to form a preparatory office, and they obtained approval in 1995. When the Board of Directors and Supervisors was established on March 27, 1996, Tsai Tung-rong was nominated as chairman. Formosa TV officially launched on June 11, 1997, becoming Taiwan's first private radio television station.[^5]

![Formosa TV logo](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/f/fe/Formosa_TV_logo.svg)

_Figure: Formosa TV logo. Source: Formosa TV; the file is preserved by Wikimedia Commons, and its copyright status is determined as public domain in the File Page, but trademark and identification uses are still subject to relevant regulations, see [File Page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Formosa_TV_logo.svg).[^11]_

The founding principles stated by Formosa TV include popularization of equity, separation of ownership and operation, and media autonomy. These texts belong to Formosa TV's own historical narrative, but they still show what Tsai Tung-rong viewed media as at the time: not just a company broadcasting programs, but a public project breaking the existing radio structure.[^5]

In his 2003 first-person article, _Formosa TV and I_, Tsai Tung-rong wrote: "For Taiwan, I founded Formosa TV; for Taiwan, I resigned from Formosa TV." He placed the founding of Formosa TV and resigning as chairman in the same political judgment, citing the hope to promote "the withdrawal of parties, government, and military from media." This is his self-account, which cannot substitute for all evidence of Formosa TV's operational history, but it clearly shows his conception of media.[^6]

The "people ruling themselves" he sought thus had two entry points: one was the ballot box, and the other was the narrative people encountered every day. Referendums allowed the people to answer questions; Formosa TV influenced which questions were visible and how they were discussed. Both faced the same difficulty: who had the right to set the agenda?

# 2003: Law Finally Codifies "Referendum"

The English institutional introduction by the Central Election Commission states that the _Referendum Law_ was enacted on December 31, 2003, establishing a legal basis for the people to vote directly on laws, legislative principles, or major policies. The National Referendum is managed by the Central Election Commission; proposals can come from citizen signatures, the Legislative Yuan, the Executive Yuan, or the President.[^7]

For Tsai Tung-rong, this was an outcome of a long journey: direct popular decision-making in public affairs finally had a working legal entry point. But "having an entry point" does not mean "easy passage." A referendum must go through proposal, signature gathering, review, voting, and result determination; every threshold changes whether the people can reach the final step.

In his verbatim speeches before the Legislative Yuan, Tsai Tung-rong recalled that the Referendum Law was passed by the Legislative Yuan on November 27, 2003, and also mentioned that the Executive Yuan is the competent authority for the referendum law. This record preserves his institutional perspective: a referendum is not a single election campaign, but a legal procedure that requires the executive agency, the legislature, and the people to share responsibility.[^4]

From overseas advocacy to domestic legislation, Tsai Tung-rong finally obtained what he sought, but he did not get a law written entirely according to his ideals. This is where the story begins to become uncomfortable and worthy of understanding.

# "Birdcage" Is Not the Handiwork of One Person

The Alliance for Civic Oversight of Parliament compiled legislative review materials from 2003 that pointed out that among the 63 articles passed on a second reading on November 27, 35 were passed based on party negotiation clauses, 22 were passed according to the KMT version, and 5 were passed according to the Executive Yuan and DPP caucus versions, with one article passed by all three parties.[^8]

The same compilation noted that the handling of "vote count not reaching half" in Tsai Tung-rong's version and the Taiwan Alliance (Tai-Lian) version was originally designed to invalidate that referendum. Other versions treated it as a rejection. These two designs were modified during the party negotiations on November 26 and 27.[^8]

Therefore, the popular "Birdcage Referendum" cannot be simplified as Tsai Tung-rong closing the door himself. The voting record from the Alliance for Civic Oversight presents the result of multi-party negotiation and seat politics jointly shaping it. Tsai Tung-rong pushed the referendum into law but could not control every bar of the resulting fence.[^8]

A common narrative that only blames "Tsai's Referendum" reverses half the causality. A closer reading of the historical materials is: Tsai Tung-rong promoted the issue of direct popular decision-making to the Legislative Yuan, but the final thresholds were a choice made by party versions, caucus negotiations, and voting majorities together.

> 📝 **Curator's Note:** The most memorable thing about the "Birdcage" is not who it can be blamed on, but that it makes two stages of democracy appear simultaneously: first someone builds the door, and then someone decides how narrow it should be.

# Legal Issues Remain in Law

A study published in the English academic journal _China Perspectives_ in 2006 pointed out that the 2003 Referendum Law and the 2004 defensive referendum raised multiple legal issues, including the constitutionality of certain articles, the legal basis, the legal effect of referendum results, and the limitations on the President's power to call a defensive referendum. The researchers ultimately concluded that the responsibility for amending these issues should lie with the Legislative Yuan of Taiwan.[^9]

This study did not portray Tsai Tung-rong as the sole driver, nor did it attribute the limitations of the Referendum Law to individual failure. It provided another scale: once a political movement enters law, it must be tested against the constitution, separation of powers, and legal validity. Tsai Tung-rong's movement gave the people the language of voting; legal research questioned whether that language was precise enough.

This is also the contradictory legacy left by Tsai Tung-rong. He constantly demanded that Taiwan's future be returned to the people, yet he had to negotiate among political parties, international pressure, and state agencies. For someone accustomed to pushing issues forward through action, institutionalization itself is a compromise. But without institutions, action can only stop at the next march.

# From "Overseas Nationals" to Legislative Seats

The official biography of the Legislative Yuan lists Tsai Tung-rong as a legislator in the seventh term under "National Unclassified and Overseas Nationals," noting his long involvement with diplomatic and defense committees. This electoral district position was not merely an administrative classification; it brought his early overseas network into Taiwan's formal representative system: someone who was once excluded from returning to the island later represented voters, including those overseas Taiwanese, in the Legislative Yuan.[^1]

There is a turning point here that is easily overlooked. Tsai Tung-rong did not abandon his overseas status and completely become an domestic politician before discussing referendums. His position in the Legislative Yuan itself tied overseas advocacy with domestic legislation. For him, the Legislative Yuan was not the end of the overseas movement, but another place where he had to learn to negotiate.

This also explains why he repeatedly oscillated between "Taiwan's future," "self-determination," and the "Referendum Law." Overseas organizations needed to speak Taiwan to foreign legislators; the Legislative Yuan required him to break down the same claims into articles, powers, and procedures. Political ideology, once entering an institution, must accept the speed of text. Once text becomes law, it is interpreted in different ways by different factions.

# Three Types of Evidence, Three Tsai Tung-rongs

Writing about Tsai Tung-rong cannot rely on a single source. The official biography of the Legislative Yuan provides his positions and education. FAPA and the New Taiwan Peace Foundation preserve organizational memory and timelines of individuals. His own _Formosa TV and I_ provides the first-person political rationale. English academic research places the Referendum Law within the framework of constitutionality and legal effect. These sources complement each other, while also reminding readers not to treat one narrative as the whole truth.[^1] [^2] [^3] [^6] [^9]

The official biography is best suited to answer "where he served," but it cannot necessarily answer "why he did it." Biographical materials can detail the sequence of actions, but they may carry a commemorative tone. First-person articles allow Tsai Tung-rong to speak for himself, but they represent his political judgment, not the consensus conclusion of all observers. Academic research can deconstruct systemic issues, but it cannot supply an inner monologue without evidence.

Putting these four layers together, Tsai Tung-rong is no longer just a collection of titles like "Father of Referendums" or "Founder of Formosa TV." He is a person who must be read across multiple archives: the biography shows how he entered the system, organizational history shows how he built networks, self-accounts show how he understood his actions, and legal research shows how the system constrained the action.

# Why Legislative Victory Still Feels Like Failure

The contradictions of the 2003 Referendum Law were not just about whether the thresholds were high, but also about "whose version" was left behind. The 63 articles compiled by the Alliance for Civic Oversight showed that 35 came from party negotiation, 22 from the KMT version, and only 5 passed according to the Executive Yuan and DPP caucus versions, with one article passing by all three parties. This distribution shows that the resulting law was not a complete implementation of Tsai Tung-rong's draft, but a text jointly produced by politics and procedural negotiation.[^8]

Therefore, Tsai Tung-rong’s victory has two dimensions. The first is symbolic: referendums transformed from political movement claims into institutional names in national law. The second is procedural: once the law was established, whether the people could use it was constrained by rules regarding proposals, signatures, review, and voting results. The former gave activists historical standing; the latter forced him to face the compromises that activists disliked the most.

The questions raised by English legal research perfectly capture this contradiction: Is the constitutional basis of the Referendum Law sufficient? Is the legal effect of referendum results clear? How are the powers of the President to initiate a defensive referendum limited? These questions show that a referendum does not automatically become legitimate just because it is called "direct popular decision-making." It still requires clear legal boundaries and verifiable procedures.[^9]

What Tsai Tung-rong left was not a perfect law, but an undeniable institutional proposition: do the people have the right to directly answer the nation's most important questions? The thresholds of the Referendum Law can be amended; the design of the system can be debated, but once this proposition enters public life, it is difficult to pretend it never existed.

# A Door or a Reminder He Left Behind

After Tsai Tung-rong passed away in 2014, he was often called "Tsai's Referendum," and frequently remembered as the founder of FAPA, co-founder of Formosa TV, or legislator. These titles each capture one aspect of his life, but none are sufficient to explain why he repeatedly performed the same action in different arenas: pushing decision-making power from the hands of a few to the hands of more people.[^1] [^2] [^3] [^5]

His path had clear costs. Putting political ideals into media easily causes tension between media autonomy and political mobilization. Placing sovereignty issues into referendums often encounters international relations, constitutional boundaries, and security risks. Codifying referendums into law requires accepting that the thresholds might be so high as to disappoint the people. These are not merely footnotes in a biography; they are the weight carried by the political tools he chose.

If one only remembers Tsai Tung-rong "promoting referendums," the story ends in 2003. If one only remembers the "Birdcage Referendum," the story starts with him being written as a failure from the beginning. A more accurate reading is: he pushed a door that previously existed only in movement slogans to the face of the law. After the door was installed, he and those who followed had to continue dealing with the frame, the lock, the key, and who could stand on the other side of the door.

This is perhaps what makes Tsai Tung-rong the hardest to simplify. His political legacy is not a completed answer, but an ongoing question: what should the system protect for the people to decide their future, and what should it not block?
