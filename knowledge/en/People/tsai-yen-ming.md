On March 18, 2024, a case file at the Taipei District Court in Taiwan presented an issue resembling an online dispute but requiring a legal answer: who has the right to rewrite Tsai Yan-ming's Wikipedia article? The original article contained controversial narratives concerning his media acquisitions, cross-Strait relations, and the Chinese government, which Tsai Yan-ming claimed damaged his reputation and personal rights.[^1]

Tsai Yan-ming filed civil lawsuits against Wikimedia Taiwan and its former and current secretaries, requesting that the defendants tolerate his free editing of the "Tsai Yan-ming" article on Chinese Wikipedia. The "lawsuit against related Wikipedia organizations" mentioned in this text specifically refers to the lawsuit brought against defendants such as Wikimedia Taiwan, not treating the Taiwanese chapter, the Wikimedia Foundation, and the Chinese Wikipedia community as a single legal entity.[^1] [^2] [^3]

> **30-Second Overview:** Tsai Yan-ming is not the founder of Yilan Foods in 1962; he joined the family business at Yilan Foods in the 1970s, later serving as general manager and shifting the food business—originally focused on processing and export—toward brand management. In 1979, "Wangzai" developed the domestic market in Taiwan, and the official history of Wangwang records the brand's establishment in 1983, collaborating with Japan's Iwazuka confectionery to develop rice crackers. This path, from inheriting a family enterprise, product transformation, to becoming a cross-border food group, later extended to media acquisitions and defamation lawsuits; understanding Tsai Yan-ming cannot begin solely with the labels "pro-China" or "entrepreneur."[^10] [^11]

## Childhood in Taodaihang

The available materials directly citing Tsai Yan-ming's childhood primarily come from biographical books by financial writers, rather than household registers, school records, or complete verbatim interviews with Tsai Yan-ming himself. When the Federation of Commercial Associations published _Heart in Mouth—The Rise of Wangwang in Cross-Strait Relations_, he was depicted as the youngest child of the Tsai family, born in 1957 and raised in Taodaihang. The book excerpts also link his father, Tsai Ah-shi, to Central Theater and the film exhibition industry.[^11]

These materials provide a specific childhood setting: an era before television was widespread, where the family business intersected with Japanese cinema, Taiwanese films, and theater crowds. The excerpts also describe his mother, Tsai Chen-chao, managing domestic affairs in the family business, while his father handled external matters more frequently. However, these contents are narrative from biographical excerpts; this article does not treat all personality descriptions or family anecdotes within them as facts verified by independent records.[^11]

Therefore, Tsai Yan-ming's childhood should not be written as a simple "rich kid legend." A more cautious description is that the publisher’s excerpts place him in a family environment simultaneously exposed to family business, theater culture, and Japanese popular imagery. This background helps readers understand his later emphasis on branding, entertainment, and consumer experience, but it cannot directly deduce his political stance or all his adult decisions.[^11]

## From Yilan Foods to the Wangwang Brand

Yilan Foods was not a company founded by Tsai Yan-ming. The official history of Wangwang lists the establishment year of Yilan Foods as 1962 and records that Tsai Yan-ming served as general manager in 1977. The pages from the Federation of Commercial Associations describe Tsai Yan-ming, aged 19 in 1976, arriving in Yilan to take over the Yilan Foods factory as the starting point of his entrepreneurial story.[^10] [^11]

These two accounts can coexist but must not be mixed. The former explains the company's corporate history and legal entity start; the latter explains when Tsai Yan-ming entered and began assuming management responsibilities. For a biographical article, the most accurate sentence is not "Tsai Yan-ming founded Yilan Foods," but rather, "He joined and took over Yilan Foods in the 1970s, later transforming the family-inherited food business into the Wangwang brand."

The official history of Wangwang records that Wangzai was born in 1979, beginning to develop the domestic market in Taiwan. In 1983, the Wangwang brand was established, and it collaborated with Japan's Iwazuka confectionery to develop the Taiwanese rice cracker market.[^10] This was a crucial step from a factory oriented toward processing and export to one focused on proprietary branding, product identity, and consumer memory.

Thus, if "founding an enterprise" is to be used for Tsai Yan-ming, it should precisely refer to the Wangwang brand and its corporate system that he later established and expanded, rather than rewriting Yilan Foods, which was founded in 1962, as his personal founding story. This difference seems merely a matter of year and verb, but it separates the company inherited by the previous generation, Tsai Yan-ming's management succession, and his individual contribution to building the brand.

On August 29 of that year, the court did not order anyone to correct the article for him. The operative part of the judgment stated only: "The plaintiff's claim is dismissed."[^1]

This outcome is easily read as "Entrepreneur loses against Wikipedia." However, what is truly noteworthy is that the court first separated four roles often confused: the writer, the administrator managing the entry, Wikimedia Taiwan promoting the project, and the Wikimedia Foundation providing the website infrastructure.

## The Defendants Are Not the Owners of the Pen

Tsai Yan-ming’s request was not merely asking a specific media outlet to issue a correction. He argued that since Wikimedia Taiwan was responsible for promoting and managing various Wikipedia projects after its establishment in 2003, it should tolerate his free editing of controversial content in the "Tsai Yan-ming" article on Chinese Wikipedia.[^3]

The court at the first instance did not initially look at whether the sentences sounded good; rather, it examined whether Wikimedia Taiwan actually held substantive management authority. The judgment cited the association's articles of incorporation, concluding that its stated tasks—which include promoting the Wikimedia Foundation’s projects, promoting open collaboration concepts, holding educational activities, and maintaining member rights—could not be extrapolated to mean the association operated Chinese Wikipedia.[^1]

The court then examined the administrator system of Wikipedia. Protecting or removing pages, deleting or restoring pages, reverting edits, and handling vandalism are all administrative powers. Administrators are generated by the Chinese Wikipedia community, not designated by Wikimedia Taiwan.[^1]

This distinction is subtle but determined the direction of the lawsuit. The association could promote Wikipedia projects in Taiwan and assist disputed parties in finding editors for communication. This does not mean it can command the global Chinese community to retain a specific version. The court even cited existing explanations stating that what the association could do was closer to assisting communication or asking administrators to handle malicious vandalism, rather than directly commanding edits under an article.[^1]

A summary of the first-instance judgment by Liberty Times also pointed out that the court found it difficult to determine that Wikimedia Taiwan was responsible for operating and managing Chinese Wikipedia; Tsai Yan-ming had edited the entry, which was subsequently reverted by site administrators, not restricted by the association.[^2]

## Tsai Yan-ming Could Actually Edit, But the Changes Were Not Kept

The most vivid detail in the judgment is a record of editing and reversion. Tsai Yan-ming was not completely unable to log in or write. He had modified the "Tsai Yan-ming" article, but his modifications were later reverted by administrators. The court recorded that the reversion might have been due to reasons such as "vandalistic editing, content with advertising, or promotional language."[^1]

The legal question here thus shifted from "Who deleted my article?" to "Who has the authority to decide if a version complies with encyclopedia rules." The court concluded that this reversion showed that Tsai Yan-ming's editorial authority was not shut down by Wikimedia Taiwan. It also could not guarantee that any version written by an editor would be permanently retained.[^1]

Wikipedia news reports in September 2024 compiled the four sets of controversial content claimed by the plaintiff: acquiring Taiwanese media to curry favor with the Chinese Communist Party, meeting with officials from the State Council for Taiwan Affairs after acquisition, criticism of related transactions by political figures, and allegations of receiving Chinese funds and being penalized. The report also noted the association's defense: that actual content management was controlled by administrators generated through community voting, and Tsai Yan-ming's modifications were reverted by other editors according to rules.[^3]

These four sets of content did not need to be defined as true or false in the same lawsuit. The first instance first addressed accountability: Tsai Yan-ming could not prove that the defendant association had substantive editorial management power, nor could he prove that the association had restricted his free editing, so the claim lacked legal basis.[^1]

📝 Curator's Note: In open collaboration, "can I write" and "will my version be kept" are two different matters. The judgment in the Tsai Yan-ming case separated these two issues.

## The Second Instance Pushed the Issue to "Public Commentary"

Tsai Yan-ming appealed the first-instance ruling. On July 22, 2026, the Taiwan High Court dismissed the appeal, maintaining the result that Tsai Yan-ming lost; the case is still appealable and cannot be written as having reached a final conclusion.[^4] [^5]

The reasoning of the second instance went one step further than the first. Liberty Times reported that the text related to "currying favor with the Chinese Communist Party" had already been deleted before or during the trial. The remaining content regarding the acquisition of Zhongshi Group, cross-Strait relations, and national security was considered matters open to public commentary.[^4]

The High Court also noted that the article included sources and footnotes. According to United Daily News's summary of the judgment, the court found that this content did not violate Chinese Wikipedia’s policies on neutrality, verifiability, or defamation, nor did it exceed the scope of reasonable commentary.[^5]

This is not a declaration by the court that all Wikipedia content is correct, nor is it an endorsement by the court for controversial political judgments. A more precise statement is: in this lawsuit, Tsai Yan-ming failed to prove that the defendant association was the entity with the authority to delete or modify content. The second instance also considered the remaining narratives related to public figures, media acquisitions, and national security as having a public commentary aspect, and noted that the article provided verifiable sources.[^4] [^5]

A summary of the second instance published by World Daily listed content Tsai Yan-ming wanted added to the entry, including Wangwang Group's sales in 59 countries and regions, donations to peace advocacy groups, corporate history, product flavors, and criticism of the first-instance judgment. The court found that these requests were mostly supplementary accounts of corporate and charitable activities, or even commentary on the court, rather than attempts at reputation repair, and therefore did not constitute appropriate reputational redress.[^6]

This determination drew a line between "correction" and "self-promotion." A public figure can certainly submit data and demand that media or Wikipedia review errors. But when a large amount of self-serving content is included in the same claim, the court asks: is this correcting an error, or is it fighting for a personal image space written by oneself?

## The Platform, Chapter, and Foundation Are Not One Person

There is another layer of network structure that is easily overlooked. Wikimedia Taiwan is a promotional and community organization in Taiwan. The website infrastructure of Wikipedia is maintained by the Wikimedia Foundation, located in the United States. The Wikimedia Foundation states in its self-introduction that it is responsible for hosting and protecting projects like Wikipedia but does not act as an editor on Wikipedia. Entries are created collaboratively by volunteers from around the world based on reliable sources and community policies.[^8]

This official statement illuminates the logic of the first-instance judgment. The association is not an administrator, nor is the Foundation the sole author of every word. Daily additions and deletions to entries fall within a community governance system composed of volunteers, administrators, policies, and discussion pages. A court cannot automatically attribute all editing actions to an organization simply because it is related to Wikipedia.[^1]

In its 2025 legal analysis, the Open Culture Foundation placed this case into the larger issue of platform governance: Wikipedia is a non-profit knowledge project relying on volunteer and source collaboration without advertising, and it cannot be simply subjected to the responsibility model of social media giants. The analysis also warned that if the law only looks at "content existing on the website" without looking at how the content was generated or who has control, public benefit open-source platforms may bear incorrect liability.[^7]

This is commentary, not an extension of the case's judgment. The court addressed Tsai Yan-ming’s claims against specific defendants. The Open Culture Foundation addresses platform legality, safe harbors, and the security of anonymous collaborators. Separating the two allows us to see the true public issues left by the case.

## Three Issues Left by the Tsai Yan-ming Case

The first issue is: Can a public figure edit their own article? Technically, Wikipedia allows people to participate in editing. In terms of governance, edits involving personal interests are prone to conflicts of interest. The party knows their experiences best and has the strongest motivation to remove unfavorable information. A reasonable system should not prohibit them from providing data, but it also should not automatically elevate "personal narrative" to "encyclopedic version."

The second issue is: Does the existence of a source mean the content is controversy-free? No. Reliable sources can support the verifiability of a passage, but they cannot absolve all tone, proportion, and inference. This is why the second instance mentioned public commentary, source footnotes, Wikipedia policies, and the scope of reasonable commentary when making judgments, not just checking if an entry has links.[^4] [^5]

The third issue is: Whom should the party approach? The first-instance judgment points to a less intuitive but more system-aligned path than filing a lawsuit: returning to edit histories, discussion pages, and community policies to find the actual editors or administrators managing the entry. Wikimedia Taiwan can assist in communication, but it cannot guarantee that all Chinese editors globally will accept a certain version.[^1]

## Conclusion: One Judgment Does Not Write Tsai Yan-ming’s Autobiography

Tsai Yan-ming's journey from Wangwang Foods into media ownership disputes has been covered by articles on Taiwan.md. This case, however, occurred in another arena: a public figure attempting to manage narratives about themselves online but encountering issues of platform governance in court.[^9]

Neither the first nor the second instance determined how Tsai Yan-ming's life should be recorded. The courts only confirmed a few things: Wikimedia Taiwan is not the substantive editor of Chinese Wikipedia. Tsai Yan-ming was able to edit, but his versions were reverted by administrators. The second instance found that some remaining content involved matters open to public commentary and had sources and footnotes. The text he requested to add was not entirely for reputational repair.[^1] [^4] [^5]

This outcome will not satisfy everyone. Tsai Yan-ming may still feel his position has not been fairly presented, and the Wikipedia community must confront the tension between public figures, conflicts of interest, and reputation rights. What is truly worth keeping is not the simple answer of "who won," but what we can ask next time someone wants to modify public knowledge: who holds this pen now?

## This Is Not a Certificate of Exemption for Any Party

To frame this case as Tsai Yan-ming losing and Wikipedia winning is still too quick. The court did not rule that all entry content was uncritical, nor did it say that public figures can only accept unfavorable narratives. The court addressed whether specific defendants had the authority and whether specific claims were valid.[^1] [^4]

Similarly, "verifiable" in Wikipedia is not a magic spell. The Foundation's official statement indicates that entries should be presented according to reliable sources, fair proportion, and neutral viewpoint, with consensus formed by volunteers through discussion and disagreement. This system requires sources and continuous editorial judgment.[^8]

For Tsai Yan-ming, the viable path left by the lawsuit is not turning Wikipedia into an autobiography, but submitting verifiable errors with sources so that the community can discuss under public rules. For the Wikipedia community, it cannot be treated as noise simply because the party is wealthy, famous, or controversial. Every reversion should lead back to policies, sources, and edit histories.[^1] [^7]

This is also why this case is worth keeping on Taiwan.md: it is not just about Tsai Yan-ming; it is about how Taiwan handles a person, a legal claim, and a knowledge space maintained by strangers. When these three collide, the most important first step is not to take sides, but to draw the power boundaries first.
