> **30-Second Overview:**
> Wang Da-hong (1917–2018) is hailed as the "Father of Modern Taiwanese Architecture" and the "Architect Poet" [^1] [^2]. As the son of Wang Chong-hui, Minister of Foreign Affairs of the Republic of China [^3], he studied engineering at the University of Cambridge and architecture at Harvard University, where he was mentored by Bauhaus founder Walter Gropius. Returning to Taiwan with the most cutting-edge Western modernist training, he chose to spend his life battling the "conflict between tradition and modernity." From the controversial and modified Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall to the reconstructed Jianguo South Road Residence, Wang Da-hong used minimalist, calm geometric lines to embed a literati-style Eastern solitude between concrete and glass.

In September 1965, Taipei’s architectural community welcomed an unprecedented design competition: the government publicly solicited memorial hall designs from twelve renowned architects to commemorate the founding father, Sun Yat-sen [^4] [^5]. In an era desperate to prove legitimacy and grandeur to the world and internally, most submissions presented massive palace-style roofs, dense flying eaves, and dazzling yellow glazed tiles. However, the winning work was unexpectedly quiet—it was a low, horizontally extending modernist building with a roof featuring a subtle, delicate upturn, designed by the then 48-year-old Wang Da-hong [^4] [^6].

This victory did not bring the expected applause and smooth sailing; instead, it opened a decades-long prelude of solitude, as his aesthetic stood out of step with the times.

## The Bauhaus Heir Emerging from Cambridge and Harvard

Wang Da-hong was born on July 6, 1917, in Beijing. His father, Wang Chong-hui, had served as Minister of Foreign Affairs and President of the Judicial Yuan of the Republic of China [^3] [^7]. His unique family background allowed him to travel through Europe during his youth, witnessing the rise of Western modern art and architectural thought. He first entered the University of Cambridge in the UK to major in mechanical engineering, then crossed the ocean to the United States to study architecture at the Harvard Graduate School of Design (GSD), becoming a fellow student of I.M. Pei [^7].

At Harvard, their mentor was Walter Gropius, founder of the Bauhaus, the cradle of the modernist design movement. Gropius taught students that architecture was not a display of power or a collage of historical styles, but an honest response to contemporary materials, structures, and the true needs of human life. This training was branded into Wang Da-hong’s bones: his drawings always featured minimalist lines, precise proportions, pure white walls, and large areas of glass.

However, when he returned to post-war Taiwan怀抱ing pure modernist ideals, he found himself facing a society eager to find national symbols and political legitimacy in visual forms. The "Less is More" philosophy of modernism and its unadorned rationality appeared too avant-garde on the streets of Taipei at the time, with some decision-makers even viewing it as "not Chinese enough."

## Compromise and Persistence in the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall: When Modernism Meets Traditional Palaces

In Wang Da-hong’s professional career, the design of the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall was undoubtedly the largest and most tormenting creative test [^8].

"The Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall was my most difficult design... The Founding Father was a revolutionary, so the architecture commemorating him must be innovative. His character and lifestyle were characterized by simplicity and plainness, so this building should not fall into luxury and vulgarity." Wang Da-hong stated in an interview in his later years [^5]. Initially, he submitted a purely modernist scheme that completely shed traditional symbols and possessed a contemporary sculptural sense, but it was rejected by higher authorities.

To ensure the building could be completed, he added upturned eaves symbolizing tradition onto the framework of modern structure [^6] [^8]. But this was not a craftsman-style copy; it was a strictly geometrically calculated modern abstract form: the flying eaves rose slightly, carrying the imagery of tradition without the heaviness and complexity of palace architecture. Although the final completion sparked much controversy over this eclectic solution, with some criticizing the upturned eaves as inconsistent with tradition, architectural scholars looking back years later realized that this was a rare model among highly scarce public buildings that successfully integrated personal will with modern structure [^6] [^8] [^9].

### Dialogue Between Modernism and Local Tradition

In Wang Da-hong’s design philosophy, "tradition" was never a set of symbols to be copied or replicated, but a soul and sense of proportion in space.

Pure geometric lines, white walls, large floor-to-ceiling glass | Grand palace-style roofs, glazed tiles, and dragon-phoenix decorations
Architecture as a container reflecting contemporary real life | Architecture as a monument displaying regime legitimacy and historical symbols

📝 **Curator’s Note:**
Wang Da-hong’s tragedy and greatness lie in his persistent attempt to uphold "the honesty and dignity of space itself" in an era that demanded political correctness and symbolic representation. He used the knife of modernism to cut the traditional Chinese robe; pain was inevitable, but this left an irreplaceable slice of the era.

## Jianguo South Road Residence: An Architect’s Ultimate Honesty to Himself

Compared to the compromises in public architecture, Wang Da-hong displayed the most pure, unadulterated architectural aesthetics in his private residential creations. In 1953, he personally designed and built his own residence on Jianguo South Road in Taipei [^10].

This single-story house occupied a small area but became a myth in the history of modern residential architecture in Taiwan. White exterior walls, black tiles, circular moon gates, a small courtyard with profound Eastern garden意境 (artistic conception), and minimalist furniture tailored entirely to internal living functions [^10] [^11]. Wang Da-hong did not treat it as a mansion to flaunt status, but as a modern peach blossom spring to house the soul of a literati.

However, with the rapid urban renewal of Taipei, this Jianguo South Road residence faced demolition in the 2010s. Fortunately, through the efforts of the Wang Da-hong Architecture Research and Preservation Society and experts from various fields, the Taipei Fine Arts Museum reconstructed this building in 2017 in the Fine Arts Park next to the museum [^10] [^11].

When people walk into the reconstructed Jianguo South Road residence, they are stunned by that extreme quietness. Sunlight spills through the moon gate onto the terrazzo floor; the proportion of every corner and every piece of furniture inside reveals the owner’s strict discipline in life. This is not just a house, but a sonnet written in concrete and light and shadow.

## Unfulfilled Ambitions and the Literary World: From the Moon Landing Monument to _Phantasmagoria_

Beyond architectural design, Wang Da-hong’s talents were not limited to reinforced concrete. He participated in an international design competition following the 1969 American Apollo Moon Landing program, designing a highly forward-looking and cosmic "Moon Landing Monument," showcasing his poetic imagination of human technology and the macrocosm of the universe.

In his later years, he also wrote the English science fiction novel _Phantasmagoria_. With delicate and fluent English prose, he constructed a utopian world blending Eastern philosophy and future technology. This book not only reflected his profound erudition but also revealed his innermost childlike innocence and curiosity toward the unknown world.

## The Solitude of the Architect Poet and the Echoes of the Era

On May 28, 2018, Wang Da-hong passed away peacefully at the age of 101 [^2]. In his long life, he designed countless influential works, including the Ministry of Foreign Affairs Office Building, NTU Dormitories, Honglu, and the Taiwan Cement Building [^12] [^13]. He always wore crisp suits, spoke gently yet firmly, like the last gentleman emerging from the early Republic of China era.

For a long time, Taiwanese society did not fully understand him. The public chased larger commercial buildings and more ornate decorations, while he simply sat quietly on the chair he designed, smoking, watching the light and shadow move on the white walls.

It was not until his later years and after his death, when Taiwan’s urban landscape appeared chaotic due to over-decoration, that people suddenly looked back and realized this architect poet had already provided the answer half a century ago: architecture does not need to shout loudly; it only needs to be honest, quiet, and leave blank space.
