> **30-second overview:**
> Wang Da-hong (1917–2018) was hailed as the "father of Taiwan's modern architecture" and an "architectural poet" [^1] [^2]. As the son of Wang Chung-hui, the Republic of China's foreign minister and president of the Judicial Yuan [^3], he studied engineering at Cambridge University and architecture at Harvard University, learning from Bauhaus founder Walter Gropius. Bringing back the most advanced Western modernist training, he chose to spend his life wrestling with the "conflict between tradition and modernity." From the controversial and modified Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall to the vanished-and-reappeared Jianguo South Road residence, Wang Da-hong used extremely minimalist and calm geometric lines, hiding a literati-style Eastern solitude between cement and glass.

In September 1965, Taipei's architectural circle witnessed a competition of unprecedented scale: to commemorate the late Mr. Sun Yat-sen, the government invited twelve renowned architects to submit designs for the memorial hall [^4] [^5]. In an era desperately needing to prove legitimacy and grandeur to both the world and internally, most submissions presented grand palace-style roofs, dense flying eaves, and dazzling yellow glazed tiles. Yet the winning work was surprisingly quiet — a low-rise, horizontally extended modernist building with a subtly refined upturned roof, designed by the then-48-year-old Wang Da-hong [^4] [^6].

This competition victory did not bring the expected applause and smooth sailing; instead, it opened the long, solitary preface to decades of being out of sync with the times' aesthetic tastes.

## The Bauhaus Disciple Who Emerged from Cambridge and Harvard

On July 6, 1917, Wang Da-hong was born in Beijing. His father, Wang Chung-hui, once served as the Republic of China's foreign minister and president of the Judicial Yuan [^3] [^7]. This unique family background allowed him to travel through Europe in his youth, witnessing the rise of Western modern art and architectural trends. He first studied mechanical engineering at the University of Cambridge in the UK, then crossed the ocean to the United States, entering Harvard University's Graduate School of Design (GSD) to study architecture, becoming a fellow student of I. M. Pei [^7].

At Harvard, their master was the cradle of the modernist design movement — Bauhaus founder Walter Gropius. Gropius taught students that architecture is not about the ostentation of power or the patchwork of historical styles, but an honest response to contemporary materials, structure, and the real needs of human life. This training was etched into Wang Da-hong's bones: his drawings were always minimalist lines, precise proportions, pure white walls, and large glass surfaces.

Yet when he returned to post-war Taiwan with pure modernist ideals, he found a society eager to seek national symbols and political legitimacy through visual means. The modernist principle of "Less is More" and its rational, undecorated style seemed too avant-garde on Taipei's streets, even being viewed by some decision-makers as "not Chinese enough."

## The Compromises and Persistence of the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall: When Modernism Meets Traditional Palaces

In Wang Da-hong's career, the design of the Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall was undoubtedly the greatest and most tormenting creative test [^8].

"The Sun Yat-sen Memorial Hall was my most difficult design... Sun Yat-sen was a revolutionary, so the building commemorating him must be innovative; his character and lifestyle are marked by simplicity and plainness, so this building should not be flowery or vulgar." Wang Da-hong said in an interview in his later years [^5]. Initially, he submitted a completely modernist plan that stripped away all traditional symbols, with a strong contemporary sculptural feel, which was rejected by superiors.

In order to make the building constructible, he added symbolic traditional upturned eaves to the modern structural framework [^6] [^8]. But this was not a craftsman's copy; it was a modern abstract form calculated through strict geometry: the eaves are slightly lifted, carrying traditional imagery without the heaviness and complexity of palace architecture. Despite the many controversies surrounding the final building, even criticisms of the upturned eaves not matching tradition, architectural scholars later reviewing it were amazed to discover it was one of the rare public buildings that successfully integrated personal will with modern structure [^6] [^8] [^9].

### The Dialogue Between Modernism and Local Tradition

In Wang Da-hong's design philosophy, "tradition" was never a set of symbols to be copied or replicated, but a soul and sense of proportion in space.

```tw-versus
Wang Da-hong's modernist practice | What society expected at the time
Pure geometric lines, white walls, large floor-to-ceiling glass | Grand palace-style roofs, glazed tiles, and dragon-phoenix decorations
Architecture is a container to reflect contemporary real life | Architecture is a monument to display political legitimacy and historical symbols
Internal spatial flow, direct dialogue with natural light and shadow | Closed, symmetrical, emphasizing authority and class axes
```

📝 **Curator's note:**
Wang Da-hong's tragedy and greatness lay in his constant attempt, in an era demanding political correctness and symbolic representation, to insist on "the honesty and dignity of space itself." He used the knife of modernism to carve the long robes of Chinese tradition, a pain that was inevitable, but thus left behind an irreplaceable slice of the times.

## Jianguo South Road Residence: An Architect's Ultimate Honesty to Himself

Compared to the constant compromises in public architecture, Wang Da-hong showed the purest and most unadulterated architectural aesthetics in his private residences. In 1953, he personally designed and built his residence on Jianguo South Road in Taipei [^10].

This modest house, though not large in area, became a myth in the history of Taiwan's modern residential architecture. White exterior walls, black tiles, round moon gates, a small central courtyard with strong oriental garden atmosphere, and minimalist furniture designed specifically for internal living functions [^10] [^11]. Wang Da-hong did not treat it as a luxurious mansion to show off his status, but as a modern Peach Blossom Spring where a literati's soul could reside.

However, with the rapid urban renewal of Taipei, this Jianguo South Road residence faced demolition in the 2010s. Fortunately, under the joint efforts of the Wang Da-hong Architecture Research and Preservation Society and experts from all walks of life, the Taipei Fine Arts Museum recreated this building in the art park next to the museum in 2017 [^10] [^11].

When people enter the reconstructed Jianguo South Road residence, they are stunned by its extreme quietness. Sunlight streams through the moon gate onto the gravel floor; every corner of the room, every table and chair's proportion, reveals the owner's strict discipline toward life. This is not just a house, but a sonnet written in concrete and light and shadow.

## Unfinished Ambitions and the Literary World: From the Moon Landing Monument to "Phantasmagoria"

Wang Da-hong's talents were not limited to steel and concrete. He participated in the international design competition following the 1969 American Apollo moon landing plan, designing a highly futuristic and cosmic "Moon Landing Monument," showcasing his poetic imagination for human technology and the cosmic macrocosm.

In his later years, he also wrote the English science fiction novel "Phantasmagoria," using delicate and fluent English writing to construct a utopian world that merged Eastern philosophy with future technology. This book not only reflects his deep erudition, but also reveals the childlike innocence and curiosity deep within his heart toward the unknown world.

## The Solitude of the Architectural Poet and the Echoes of the Times

On May 28, 2018, Wang Da-hong passed away peacefully at the age of 101 [^2]. Throughout his long life, he designed numerous influential works such as the Ministry of Foreign Affairs office building, National Taiwan University dormitories, Honglou, and Taiwan Cement Building [^12] [^13]. He always wore a well-tailored suit, spoke gently and firmly, like the last gentleman from the early Republican period.

For a long time, Taiwanese society had not fully understood him. The public pursued even larger commercial buildings and more luxurious decorations, while he quietly sat in the chair he designed, smoking, watching the light and shadow move across the white walls.

Until his later years and after his death, when the city's landscape became cluttered due to excessive decoration, people suddenly turned back and realized that this architectural poet had already given the answer half a century ago: architecture does not need to shout loudly; it only needs to be honest, quiet, and leave blank space.
