# Wei Zong-cheng: Drawing Yokai from Sanxia into Ximending, Allowing Mazu to Step Out of Comic Panels

> **30-Second Summary:** In 2009, Wei Zong-cheng became a professional cartoonist with the political satire comic _The Ma Huang Arrives_. _Record of Nether War_, serialized starting in 2010, placed post-1992 earthquake Taiwan, Ximending street scenes, Taoist sects, and Mazu Lin Mo-niang into a young adventure story. While this is often viewed as a success of "local elements," Wei Zong-cheng's own explanation is closer to the mundane: he drew markets, buses, and worries about going to school; Taiwan was not a label slapped on, but something that grew from life itself.

On February 2, 2017, the Lin Mo-niang lantern at the Taipei Lantern Festival debuted in Taipei. This was not the middle-aged Mazu common in temples, but the girl character from Wei Zong-cheng's _Record of Nether War_. The lantern stood 8 meters high and 5 meters wide, with a total length of 10 meters, preparing to appear in the Xicheng Carnival parade. [^1]

A few years prior, this character existed only on the comic page. On New Year's Day 2021, Minghua Garden adapted _Record of Nether War_ into an electronic rock _gezaixi_ opera, and Wei Zong-cheng was invited to perform as "Seventh Uncle." He later recalled that he initially thought it was just a minor appearance until the press conference revealed the role was substantial. [^2]

The starting point for this journey was not a major cultural policy, but a political parody comic born in the fan fiction community that publishers were unsure whether or not to pick up. What is truly special about Wei Zong-cheng is not just his willingness to place politicians, the adult industry, and folk deities in the same creative universe, but his constant question: how can a joke, a deity, or a street look like a place where Taiwanese people actually live?

## He Didn't Draw "Taiwanese Flavor" First; He Drew What He Saw

Wei Zong-cheng grew up in Sanxia, New Taipei. Although _Record of Nether War_ begins in Ximending, the story extends to familiar local landscapes like Sanxia and Da Bao Creek. To readers, these places are backgrounds. To the author, they are part of the characters: the yokai and Taoists do not fight in an unnamed city; they fight at recognizable intersections, commercial districts, and mountain streams in Taiwan. [^3]

He said that the concept for _Record of Nether War_ formed back in middle school. Looking at gods and demons in Japanese comics, he thought, "If Japan can draw its legends like that, then the yokai and local folklore I know around me should be able to too." [^3]

This statement is easily rephrased as "he has a local consciousness." But that description misses a key layer: Wei Zong-cheng is not describing a plan where he first decides to represent Taiwan and then collects symbols. He said what he drew were mostly things he had experienced, including what he saw at the wet market, what he saw on the bus, and what he worried about while going to school. [^3]

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> The common narrative is that "local comics place Taiwanese culture into their works." However, Wei Zong-cheng’s creative sequence reverses cause and effect: he did not first treat temples, yokai, and place names as cultural markers to color the story; instead, he drew his own life into it. What might be called "Taiwanese flavor" is perhaps not a list of materials, but an unavoidable way of seeing by the author.

Therefore, the locality in _Record of Nether War_ appears not just in character names. It appears in the direction of the streetscapes, the posture of the characters walking, and the absurdity when deities and police are placed side-by-side. It also appears in details like gods drinking beverages or eating meat buns, which pull divinity back into the mundane. A 2011 report by _Taipei Times_ noted that Wei Zong-cheng made Mazu a central character in _Record of Nether War_, portraying her as a cute girl and having her eat meat buns and drink lemon honey. [^4]

## Starting with _The Ma Huang Arrives_: Humor is Actually a Method of Observation

_The Ma Huang Arrives_ in 2009 brought Wei Zong-cheng from fan creation to professional comics. An interview with Openbook described it as political satire, and also listed his later works: _AV End Finger_, _Jump! Datong Moe Convention_, _My Chairman Is So Cute_, and _Bahi Huang Ying_. [^5]

The success of _The Ma Huang Arrives_ has a concrete number: 4,000 copies sold per day at fan conventions, with final sales reaching 40,000. This result established Wei Zong-cheng as a full-time cartoonist and led Future Digital, which originally distributed Japanese adult games, to start operating original Taiwanese comics. [^6]

However, he does not view political comics merely as protest posters. When discussing how to handle political satire, he said the key is to maintain observation, allowing both sides of the argument to appear; otherwise, the work becomes too extreme. He also stated that politicians are just used to enhance entertainment value, and true success is achieved only if the satire remains valid even after the characters are removed years later. [^5]

This method makes his comics look ridiculous, but their structure is precise. The humor does not flatten reality; rather, it first establishes a recognizable scene for the reader, and then places something that shouldn't be there—but coincidentally illuminates power relations—within that scene. Wei Zong-cheng described his work in interviews as "a tragedy of power struggle wrapped in comedy." [^5]

## From Fan Fics to Sustainable Commercial Comics

Wei Zong-cheng’s resume can be understood through several turning points. These are not merely a timeline of fame, but a microcosm of how Taiwanese comics moved between readership communities, publishers, and public cultural activities.

| Year      | Turning Point                                                                  | Significance for Creation                                                                                                         |
| :-------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 2009      | _The Ma Huang Arrives_ sparks discussion at fan conventions                    | Political satire becomes an entry point for professional comics, proving that fan readers can form a publishing demand. [^5] [^6] |
| From 2010 | Serialization of _Record of Nether War_                                        | Establishes a long-form worldview using Taiwanese landscapes, folk religion, and young adventure. [^5]                            |
| 2017      | Lin Mo-niang becomes the main lantern character at the Taipei Lantern Festival | The character moves from print comics to urban festivals and public viewing. [^1] [^7]                                            |
| 2021      | Minghua Garden releases _gezaixi_ opera adaptation                             | The comic's worldview is retranslated into the body, music, and stage language of _gezaixi_ opera. [^2] [^7]                      |

This timeline also shows that Wei Zong-cheng’s importance is not just about sales. When a work allows publishers to see a market for original comics, when characters are adopted by public events, and when traditional troupes adapt them into stage works, the author is not just delivering a book; they are testing whether a Taiwanese narrative can withstand the weight of different media.

His collaboration with Future Digital was particularly crucial. Openbook's report recorded that Future Digital, originally mainly dealing in distributing Japanese adult games, began operating original Taiwanese comics due to the market response to _The Ma Huang Arrives_. [^6] This detail reminds readers that the so-called Taiwanese comics industry is not a straight line planned by large institutions. It often starts with one publisher willing to try again and an author willing to rearrange political figures familiar to the readers.

_Record of Nether War_ turned this accident into a long-term project. Long-form requires consistent output, editorial judgment, printing, and promotion; it also requires readers to come back after the first installment. While Lin Mo-niang's recognition is important, what truly sustains the worldview are the landscapes, the faith, and the character relationships that continuously generate new stories. This is why Wei Zong-cheng always returns to life observation when discussing creation, rather than just character design.

## _Record of Nether War_: Putting Deities into a Livable Network of Relationships

_Record of Nether War_ began serialization in 2010. The story is set after the 1992 earthquake, as supernatural phenomena increase across Taiwan, leading police to form the "Black Sun Project" to handle incidents with Taoists. The work does not treat folk religion as a mysterious filter; instead, it makes humans, gods, ghosts, and yokai mutually related. [^2]

Wei Zong-cheng initially imagined a group of passionate young adventurers, but editor Han Jingyue suggested adding Lin Mo-niang, which made Mazu an important character in the story. A dinner conversation recorded by Openbook noted that Han Jingyue believed the origin point of the comic was the character, and Lin Mo-niang first attracted readers, gaining recognition through creation. [^6]

Thus, Mazu no longer appears only in shrines, processions, or legends. In the comics, she is 16-year-old Lin Mo-niang, possessing a tension between cuteness, youthfulness, and divinity. A 2023 study published in _Asian Ethnology_ placed this image within the changes of Taiwanese folk religion and popular culture, noting that the first volume, published in 2010, introduced Mazu into the comic world as a young girl, later appearing in larger public spaces at the 2017 Taipei Lantern Festival and in 2021 _gezaixi_ opera. [^7]

What is most noteworthy here is not the familiar slogan "tradition meets modernity," but how the relationship between deities and readers changes after media transformation. Researchers point out that Lin Mo-niang, moving from a character in male subcultures to lanterns, theater, and public events, has simultaneously sparked different interpretations of generational, religious, and political identity. [^7]

> 📝 **Curator's Note**
>
> The most daring aspect of _Record of Nether War_ is not making Mazu cute, but making her someone readers can stick with long-term. A deity transforms from the center of a single festival into a character in the story who gets hurt, makes choices, and forms relationships with other characters. This is not lightening faith; it is placing faith into a narrative grammar that young readers genuinely use.

## "No Absolute" Actually Provides Creative Space

When discussing Taoist material, Wei Zong-cheng does not present Taiwanese folk religion as a neat system of knowledge. He says the Taoist sects in Taiwan are vast and diverse; when asked about different sects, each one might say something different, sometimes even disowning others. [^3]

This inconsistency did not stop him from writing; instead, it became creative space. When there is no single recognized version, he can first deal with the combat rules and story structure in the comics, then let different legends collide. But this freedom does not mean arbitrariness. Regarding the depiction of Lin Mo-niang, he and his editor maintain an unspoken boundary, knowing which adaptations would push the character into inappropriate positions. [^3]

He also maintains a small but significant habit: after every publication, he takes the book to worship at Mazu temples first. He says this is not fear, but a custom and courtesy. [^3]

This action places "creative freedom" and "cultural responsibility" on the same table. Creators can rearrange mythology, but they cannot pretend they do not care who originally believed in the myth or how it was used. Creation is not borrowing a deity as decoration and then tearing up the IOU after the work is finished.

## When Comics Leave Paper, Authors Are Drawn onto the Stage

In December 2020, Central Broadcasting Corporation reported that Minghua Garden adapted _Record of Nether War_ into an electronic rock _gezaixi_ opera, scheduled for January 2, and 3, 2021, at the Taiwan Opera House. Wei Zong-cheng was surprised when he first heard about the invitation from Minghua Garden; he originally thought he would only be on stage for a minute, but later learned he was playing Seventh Uncle. [^2]

In an OPENTIX interview, he described this performance bluntly: "I was forced." He initially thought it was just a minor cameo, but upon the press conference, he realized the role was heavy, and he could only "force himself" onto the stage. [^3]

Print comics use panels to control the reader's gaze, but the stage requires actors to project the character's aura to the audience simultaneously. Wei Zong-cheng observed that the delicate and steady demeanor of Lin Mo-niang in the comics might not be easily conveyed on stage; instead, the large movements of the male lead Chen Bo-rong were easier to bring to life. [^3]

This is the real difficulty of transmedia adaptation: it's not finished just by making a costume; it requires finding a new way for the character to breathe in the new medium. Wei Zong-cheng’s principle for adapters is: "The adapter can create freely, as long as they are within the same worldview. As long as each creator can tell the story well on their own, I won't have many objections." [^3]

## A Long Work Sustained Not by Inspiration, But by Waiting

_Record of Nether War_ managed to become a long-form series not just because Lin Mo-niang was recognizable. Openbook’s report recorded that after serialization began in 2010, the first issue sold 14,000 copies, and the Lin Mo-niang main lantern at the 2017 Taipei Lantern Festival helped this character break out of the Taiwanese comics echo chamber. [^6]

Wei Zong-cheng also pointed out the fragility of long-form comics: many people want to draw a massive series, but the work might be canceled due to subsequent marketing, exposure planning, or publishing conditions. Not everyone can finish it. [^3]

After having children, his previous routine of working 12 hours almost every day was disrupted; he could only work while the children slept. When asked when the work would conclude, he did not give a date, but just laughed and said, "I've been drawing for so long, I should hurry up and finish it." [^3]

This sentence sounds like a joke, but it speaks to another reality of long-form comics. The story must keep moving forward, but the author’s life does not stop at the beginning of creation. Family, publishing, marketing, reader expectations—everything pulls on the same serialization line.

## Why This Character Deserves to Be Remembered

Wei Zong-cheng cannot be summarized in Taiwanese comics history using only the terms "local" or "adult." Both words are true, but they too quickly end the discussion. Political parody brought him his first readers; adult humor gave him an unrestrained tone; what truly supports the long-form work is his placing different layers of Taiwanese experience into one story: conversations in markets, observations on buses, rituals at local temples, politicians constantly changing in the news, and the reader's feeling of both familiarity and strangeness toward deities.

This style has a clear risk. When gods are drawn as girls, readers might only see the character's cuteness and forget the religious context behind her. When politicians are depicted as jokes, readers might only retain a stance while ignoring how the humor reorders power relations. Wei Zong-cheng’s work does not provide standard answers to these questions. It is more like an open laboratory where readers, after laughing, decide what they accept, what they question, and which cultural symbols cannot merely be treated as decoration.

Academic studies tracking the Lin Mo-niang image fill in gaps that comic criticism often overlooks. When the character enters lanterns and theater, she no longer belongs only to the author and comic readers. Local governments, troupes, religious believers, anime fans, and general audiences may all reinterpret her through their own experiences. [^7] These different interpretations should not be erased because a character's ability to live on often depends on whether she continues to generate questions in new contexts of use.

For engineers or knowledge base readers, Wei Zong-cheng’s case also offers a practical perspective: cultural content is not randomly splicing elements from a database; it requires establishing a set of rules that readers can repeatedly recognize. Place names, characters, faith, humor, and media adaptation must have traceable relationships with each other. Only when the work transforms from comics to lanterns, _gezaixi_ opera, or an object of study does the worldview not completely lose its shape just by changing its medium. This is the real reason why a character's story is worth remembering.

## From "Looking Real" to "Making Sense"

Wei Zong-cheng repeatedly talks about visual details in interviews. How should thugs under the arcade stand? Why are the ribs getting smaller when the characters buy lunchboxes? What kind of Taiwanese radio do factory workers like to listen to? These are not background materials used merely for decoration. They determine whether the reader can believe that someone actually lived in that world before a joke appears. [^5]

This observation also explains why he can connect seemingly unrelated frameworks. Politicians are placed into Hong Kong-style martial arts; ROC history is put into talent shows; deities and police are placed into the same case procedure. The more familiar the framework, the easier it is to spot the deviation. The power of humor comes not from unfamiliarity, but from the reader knowing how things were supposed to work, only to suddenly see them pushed aside by Wei Zong-cheng. [^5]

The official feature in _CCC Creation Collection_ placed _Record of Nether War_ in the discussion of "transliterating other cultures," reminding creators that when adding fantasy elements to real locations, they must still understand cultural materials, taboos, and representation responsibility. This position is suitable for viewing Wei Zong-cheng: he truly rearranges legends, but he does not treat Taiwanese customs as a set of interchangeable icons. When adapters use Lin Mo-niang or other religious characters, the consistency of the work's worldview and respect for cultural context must be addressed together. [^8]

Therefore, Wei Zong-cheng's "perversion" or "parody" is not arbitrary indulgence. It is closer to a working method: first observing daily life in sufficient detail, and then pushing the order of politics, history, or faith to the absurd boundary. The reader laughs first, and only then do they have the chance to discover that what was pushed aside has always existed within their own lives.

## What He Left Behind Is Not a Moe Character, But a Path That Can Continue

Wei Zong-cheng's work is easily reduced to several labels: political parody, adult humor, moe Mazu, Taiwanese comics IP. But grouping these labels together misses his core work. He repeatedly shrinks abstract grand themes down to specific scenes: power returns to the actions of politicians, faith returns to the entrance of Mazu temples, locality returns to Sanxia and Ximending, and cultural conflict returns to how a character should speak.

_Record of Nether War_ is not packaging "Taiwan" as an easily exportable commodity. It is more like a city built by the author from life: deities can walk into it, yokai have their own kinship ties, police and Taoists solve cases together, and the cartoonist himself might be invited to perform on stage as Seventh Uncle. This city is not clean or neat; precisely because of this, it does not look like a tourist poster.

From _The Ma Huang Arrives_ in 2009 to _Record of Nether War_'s serialization starting in 2010, Wei Zong-cheng used "making people laugh" as the entry point and hid politics, faith, local legends, and industry difficulties behind that entrance. When Lin Mo-niang moves from the comic page to an 8-meter lantern, and then onto the _gezaixi_ stage, what is truly moved out is not a character, but the confidence of Taiwanese creators to build a worldview from their own lives.

Wei Zong-cheng once said: "I am just a cartoonist." [^5] But a person who is "just a cartoonist" allowed yokai from Sanxia to walk into Ximending, allowed Mazu to cross pages, lanterns, and stages, and made readers re-imagine: do the streets we pass every day perhaps already house a mythology that hasn't been drawn yet?
