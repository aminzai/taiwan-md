> **30-Second Overview:** Yeh Hung-ch'a mixed the fear following the White Terror, the loyalty of episodic novels, the mechanisms of Japanese mystery, and the Taiwanese publishing scene into a youth named Zhuge Liang. The work was once wildly popular across Taiwan but later disappeared due to censorship and market changes. Looking at him today is not just about recovering a character; it is about seeing how Taiwanese comics once developed its own grammar.

In 1958, Yeh Hung-ch'a began serializing _Zhuge Liang Battles the Devil Party_ in the Taipei magazine _Comic King_. At that time, he was 34 years old and had been imprisoned for over a decade due to political cartoons. Readers sent in more than 10,000 guesses about the true identity of the devil party's leader. Zhuge Liang was originally planned as a series of only ten installments but survived thanks to the letters and the market.[^1] [^2]

This origin has an counterintuitive aspect: although Zhuge Liang appears to be a hero from ancient China, he is a character forged in the post-war Taiwanese publishing environment, incorporating Japanese comics, films, rental bookstores, and local printing. He is not merely a "traditional hero," but a Taiwanese product born from mixed cultures.

📝 **Curator's Note:** What makes Zhuge Liang worth re-examining is not how skilled he was in fighting, but how he allows us to see an island deconstruct external forms and reassemble its own hero.

## Yeh Hung-ch'a Didn't Start with a Hero

Yeh Hung-ch'a was born in Nanmen, Hsinchu, in 1924. As a youth, he participated in paper puppet competitions, creating promotional works about hygiene and air raid defense. In 1942, he formed the "Xin Gao Comic Group" with fellow comic enthusiasts. After the war, this group helped establish the magazine _Xin Xin_, using two-page comics to satirize corruption, inflation, and social issues.[^3]

_Xin Xin_ ceased publication after the 228 Incident. When Yeh Hung-ch'a was 26, he was imprisoned during the White Terror, and his family had to liquidate their assets to secure his release. Years later, Yeh Hung-ch'a’s eldest son, Yeh Chia-lung, recalled: "My grandparents sold off their possessions just to get him out. This was the greatest suffering of my father's life, but it was also the inspiration for his creation of the hero Zhuge Liang. He hoped there could be a hero who helps the weak and fights against evil forces."[^2]

This statement cannot be taken as the sole answer to the work. Reducing Zhuge Liang simply to an allegory of the White Terror would flatten a long-running, continuously rewritten comic into a single symbol. A more accurate description is that his imprisonment gave Yeh Hung-ch'a an emotional problem, and the publishing market, genre culture, and reader correspondence transformed this problem into a character capable of continuing adventures.

After _Xue You_ magazine was founded in 1953, children's comics gradually became an attraction in the publishing market. In 1958, _Comic King_ became one of Taiwan's first publications primarily focused on comics, and it was through this window that Yeh Hung-ch'a promoted himself, leading to Zhuge Liang's true debut.[^4]

## Ancient Names, Taiwanese Methods

The name Zhuge Liang itself carries traces of patchwork. English research indicates that the character mixes the names and virtues of Zhuge Liang and Yang Silang, while the story borrows from ancient Chinese imagination but incorporates mysteries, masks, mechanisms, and transformation elements common in Japanese mystery comics and special effects films.[^4] [^5]

The biographical data on Yeh Hung-ch'a from the Hsinchu City Cultural Bureau lists a broader range of influences: episodic novels, _Romance of the Three Kingdoms_, _Water Margin_, Eastern ninjas, monster movies, and Western detective thrillers all fed his creation. Therefore, Zhuge Liang and Zhenping do not advance solely through martial prowess, but through tracking identities, cracking traps, and discerning truth from falsehood.[^3]

This also explains why Zhuge Liang's enemies often wear masks. The mask gives the story a mystery, and it prevents the character's good or evil nature from being explained in the first panel. For contemporary child readers, what truly made them turn pages might not have been loyalty or filial piety, but "who this person really is."

## Popularity, Then Forced Compliance

In the 1960s, Zhuge Liang became a familiar hero to children. Data from the Hsinchu City Cultural Bureau indicates that the Zhuge Liang series eventually formed into nine volumes and 55 books. Yeh Hung-ch'a and his wife Chen Jinlian established Hongjia Publishing in 1964 to continue publishing through single volumes.[^3] Exhibition materials from the National Comic Museum also point out that the work was popular across Taiwan from 1958 to 1970 and was later adapted into television dramas, stage plays, and films.[^1]

Success brought pressure. In 1962, the Executive Yuan passed the _Regulations for Compiling Illustrated Guides_, leading comic publishing toward a stricter regulatory environment. In 1967, censorship restrictions on supernatural abilities, character behavior, and story content caused martial arts comics to lose the exaggerated imagination that originally attracted readers.[^3] [^4]

Yeh Hung-ch'a did not stop writing immediately. He established a publishing house, took on apprentices, and taught many young people from poor families how to draw, maintaining production through reprints and new works. However, regulatory restrictions coincided with the import of Japanese comics, changing the market. A Taipei Times report noted that in 1968, Zhuge Liang was forced to lose his supernatural abilities, leading to a decline in reader interest, and Hongjia Publishing finally ceased operations in 1973.[^4]

This history cannot be written merely as "censorship killed comics." Censorship certainly constrained creation, but the market also faced issues of plagiarism, declining quality, and cheap reprints. Academic studies place Zhuge Liang within the political, economic, and livelihood predicaments of the 1950s and 1960s, arguing that the hero comic is both a child's imagination and a text reflecting social projection.[^5]

Thus, the disappearance of Zhuge Liang was not due to a character suddenly becoming unpopular, but the result of changes involving the author, readers, publishing regulations, and the cultural market.

## A Sticker, Preserving the Reader's Hand

The National Taiwan Museum of History houses a sticker of Zhuge Liang produced after 1965. It is only 4.8 cm in diameter; the front features Zhuge Liang in ancient red attire, while the back bears the traffic sign "Caution Bicycle."[^6]

This small sticker reminds us that a character's life is not confined to comic books. It entered games of trading, slapping, and card-playing, and was printed on children's toys alongside popular film characters, opera performers, and comic figures. The work was taken out of the bookstore by readers, becoming something tangible that could be held, won or lost at, and kept in a pocket.

Equally important are the manuscripts. A 2024 exhibition featured original drafts, personal belongings, and one hundred collected items. The exhibition also included oral guides from Yeh Chia-lung, allowing visitors to approach the work again through the artist's workspace, classical music, and family memory.[^1] [^2]

Yeh Chia-lung said: "Ordinary people can do extraordinary things."[^1] If this sentence is taken only as a motivational slogan, it is too light. When placed back into the exhibition context, it refers to how an artist suppressed by the era was preserved by family, collected by museums, organized by researchers, and reinterpreted for new generations, allowing the work to enter the public view again.

## From Memory Back to Public Culture

Zhuge Liang has been adapted into 1970s films, 1980s television dramas, and a 2022 3D animated film, _Zhuge Liang: Hero of Heroes_.[^1] In 2024, the National Comic Museum presented three series—_Zhuge Liang 100_, _Becoming Zhuge Liang_, and _Hero's Soul_—placing original drafts, animations, reader memories, and comic history in one park.[^1] [^7]

These adaptations do not mean the original work was completely preserved. Every recoloring, adaptation, exhibition, or commercialization reselects which characters, tones, and values to retain. What academic research calls "loyalty, filial piety, righteousness, wit, and courage" is an important ethical framework of the work, but it is not everything. Zhuge Liang also encompasses masks, misidentification, cross-cultural borrowing, publishing labor, and gaps left by censorship.[^5]

The photograph of the National Comic Museum on Wikimedia Commons depicts a preserved Japanese colonial-era building. It is not an original draft of Zhuge Liang, nor can it be used as historical evidence for the work. In this article, it only serves to point out one thing: comic preservation requires space, and space also reconfigures how the work is viewed.[^8]

## Not Just Preserving Childhood

Zhuge Liang’s modern reappearance is easily written as "Our childhood has returned." But childhood does not return unchanged. The re-published versions, the museum displays, and the animated film frames all allow readers to see things they hadn't seen before: that Yeh Hung-ch'a was imprisoned, that comics were censored, that publishers supported apprentices, that manuscripts might have been lost, and that a small sticker preserved the character's outline for a generation of children.

Therefore, Zhuge Liang is more than just a youth hero in red clothes. He is more like a measuring stick for Taiwanese cultural memory: when society only remembers the sword and the Devil Party, we see entertainment. Only when we bring back Yeh Hung-ch'a's imprisonment, his wife's support, the apprentices' labor, and the systemic constraints do we see a comic that preserved imaginative freedom for its readers in an era of constraint.

The 2024 exhibition allowed people to re-engage with the manuscripts; the 2022 animation brought the character into a new visual grammar; and the museum turned an old building into a public space for preserving Taiwanese comics. Zhuge Liang does not need to be frozen in the titles of "earliest" or "most classic." He is more like a mask that continues to ask questions: when the next Taiwanese creator faces limitations, will we give them the chance to finish their hero?
