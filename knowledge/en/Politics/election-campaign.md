Every pre-election period sees Taiwan's streets undergo a marvelous transformation. The deafening loudspeakers, the colorful campaign flags, and the "stage trucks" that can transform from ordinary vans into neon-flashing stages in minutes collectively construct Taiwan's unique "election campaigning culture" [^1]. This is not merely a political struggle for votes; it is a "democratic carnival" integrating popular aesthetics, social psychology, and legal norms.

From a sociological perspective, election campaigning in Taiwan has long transcended mere policy dissemination, evolving into a highly ritualized process of collective empathy [^7]. In these events, voters often seek not cold policy data, but emotional resonance and a sense of group belonging [^15].

## Stage Trucks and Propaganda Vehicles: The "Metamorphosis Vajra" of Popular Culture

If one were to select the most representative visual symbols of Taiwanese campaigning venues, the "Stage Truck" and modified propaganda vehicles are undoubtedly on the list. These vans, which appear ordinary in daily life, can be rapidly deployed into luxurious stages equipped with top-tier sound systems, LED screens, and dazzling lights merely through hydraulic systems and simple operation upon reaching their destination [^3].

Photographer Shen Chao-liang (沈昭良) profoundly documented this unique mobile stage culture of Taiwan in his famous _STAGE_ series. He pointed out that the development of stage trucks originates from the frequent needs for deity worship during local temple fairs and wedding/funeral celebrations in Taiwan [^2]. Early performance stages were often temporary structures, which were time-consuming and labor-intensive. As times evolved, operators modified vans into electronic music vehicles to improve mobility and visual effect, eventually developing the highly automated hydraulic stage trucks seen today.

![Image 1: Political campaign vehicles and supporter groups traversing urban streets during early street campaigning activities. This image records the dynamic landscape of Taiwanese street political mobilization, with large installations and sound equipment mounted on the vehicles, demonstrating a high degree of popular participation and mobility.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b6/908_Taiwan_Republic_Campaign_people_and_Fuso_FM_truck_20060318.jpg)

As shown in the image above, early political mobilization in Taiwan was often accompanied by large modified trucks and long processions. This practice of temporarily converting transportation into propaganda platforms broke down the spatial limitations of traditional political rallies, allowing political messages to penetrate directly into every alleyway. The design of these stage trucks and propaganda vehicles is rich with strong local Taiwanese characteristics; the vibrant color combinations and exaggerated lighting effects not only create a powerful visual impact but also reflect the grassroots society's yearning for "excitement" and "spectacle" [^3].

## Democratic Snacks: Political Mobilization on the Palate

In Taiwan's campaigning scenes, besides the political speeches on stage, the "democratic snacks" below are equally indispensable to the soul of the event. Among these, none is more famous than "Democratic Sausage" [^18]. This street vendor culture, originating from 1980s street movements, involved vendors moving with protesting crowds, providing physical sustenance for participants and becoming an informal symbol of the democratic movement [^18].

![Image 2: A "Democratic Sausage" stall photographed at a protest or campaigning event. White smoke rises from the charcoal grill, and the vendor provides popular food to the crowd amidst the noisy political gathering; this "charcoal aroma from the protest line" has become a unique olfactory and visual memory in Taiwan's democratic movement history.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c5/318_-_%E6%B0%91%E4%B8%BB%E9%A6%99%E8%85%B8.jpg)

As depicted in the image above, Democratic Sausage stalls are often located close to protesters or campaign crowds. The sizzling sausages and drifting smoke are not just sources of physical nourishment but also carry the shared life experience of Taiwanese civil society in street struggles and election mobilization [^18]. Another food with high political symbolism is "Election Fried Rice Noodles" [^17]. In central and southern Taiwan, candidates often prepare large batches of fried rice noodles and meatball soup to treat local residents during small policy forums. This "noodle-party" culture has been fragrant in Taiwan for over 30 years; it serves not only to fill voters' stomachs but also as an expression of human connection and a tool for mobilization [^17].

## Election Propaganda Vehicles: Mobile Broadcast Towers and Noise Anxiety

The "election propaganda vehicles" (colloquially called war vehicles) weaving through alleys are another political landscape. These modified vehicles usually carry large loudspeakers, repeatedly broadcasting candidates' policies, campaign songs, or appeals [^9]. Although this is seen as a necessary means to reach the grassroots, the noise they generate often sparks social controversy.

According to Article 54 of the _Public Officials Election and Recall Act_, political parties and candidates are prohibited from creating noise with loudspeakers; otherwise, environmental protection authorities may issue fines under the _Noise Control Act_ [^10]. However, because propaganda vehicles have high mobility, enforcement agencies often face technical difficulties in actual measurement and ticketing [^11]. This tension between "the voice of democracy" and "life tranquility" reflects the continuous refinement of civil rights boundaries during Taiwan's democratic transition.

![Image 3: An aerial view of a large campaigning evening event in Fengshan District, Kaohsiung. Tens of thousands of supporters gather in the brightly lit square, waving flags, embodying the irreplaceable "theatrical effect" and collective empathy of physical Taiwanese campaigning events.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3d/The_campaign_rally_of_Han_Kuo-yu_in_Fengshan%2C_Kaohsiung.jpg)

As the magnificent scene of the large-scale campaign event in the image above demonstrates, tens of thousands of people gather at night, collectively building powerful political energy through uniform slogans and waving flags. This "theatrical effect" generated by physical gatherings is something that no digital social media can fully replace.

## Election Souvenir Economics: From Fans to Wuxia Design

Another characteristic of Taiwanese elections is the colorful array of "election souvenirs." From the practical items like facial tissues, ballpoint pens, and lighters of yesteryear, to plastic fans essential during hot weather, these souvenirs are not just promotional tools; they form a unique "election industry chain" [^12].

As the voter base has become younger, election souvenirs have evolved from mere giveaways into "brand merchandise." Candidates have begun releasing aesthetically designed, trendy goods such as eco-bags, sports towels, and even character toys, attracting supporters to purchase them voluntarily, thereby transforming political identification into a display of lifestyle [^12]. Interestingly, this industry chain was previously outsourced in part to China to reduce costs, but due to geopolitical risks in recent years, many orders have returned to local Taiwanese manufacturers, forming another form of "roots remaining in Taiwan" [^12].

## The Magic of Language and "Dòun Suàn": Emotional Catalysts at Campaign Venues

In campaigning venues, language is not just a tool for communication; it is an emotional catalyst. Among these, the term "Dòun Suàn" (凍蒜) is perhaps the most well-known. This word, derived from the Minnan dialect's homophone for "to be elected," has become an indispensable slogan in Taiwanese elections [^5]. When hosts lead tens of thousands below to shout "Dòun Suàn" with hoarse voices, the overwhelming momentum often instantly pushes the atmosphere to its peak.

In addition to simple and powerful slogans, a politician's "election language" is also a profound art form [^1]. In campaign speeches, candidates often heavily use popular vernacular, metaphors, and comparative techniques to bridge the gap with voters. This linguistic strategy not only effectively stimulates solidarity among supporters but also creates easily transmissible memory points in an era of information fragmentation [^1].

## The Boundary of the 30-Dollar Lunchbox: The Tug-of-War Between Law and Human Sentiment

To maintain fair elections, Taiwan's judicial organs have gradually developed a set of standards for judging vote-buying. The most widely known is the so-called "30-dollar threshold" [^4]. This standard originates from a 2001 advisory letter from the Ministry of Justice, which stated that if candidates send simple promotional items valued at less than NT$30, it is generally considered insufficient to sway voters' voting intentions and therefore does not constitute vote-buying [^13].

However, in actual legal operations, "30 dollars" is not an absolute line that cannot be crossed. When courts judge whether vote-buying has occurred, they still consider the candidate's subjective intent, the nature of the item, and whether it is sufficient to influence voters' willingness to vote [^4]. For example, providing simple fried rice noodles at a campaign evening, even if the cost exceeds 30 dollars, is usually viewed as an act of "hospitality" in Taiwanese society rather than bribery [^4].

Vivid visual symbols and slogans intertwine under the sun, becoming the most representative democratic landscape during Taiwan's major elections, symbolizing the cohesion of collective voter will.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/7/75/%E8%87%BA%E7%81%A3%E9%80%86%E8%BD%89%E5%8B%9D_Flag_of_Come-back_Win_for_TAIWAN.jpg)

As shown in the image above, campaign flags are not just visual tools to mark factions; they also play a role in building inward cohesion during collective activities. In this ocean of flags, voters can temporarily shed the isolation of daily life and integrate into a community with a shared political imagination.

## Social Capital and Political Participation: The Deeper Function of Campaigning

From an academic perspective, campaign evenings function to strengthen "Social Capital" [^16]. Through face-to-face collective participation, trust and norms among voters are reinforced, which in turn influences voter turnout through interpersonal networks [^15]. Research shows that Taiwanese voters' voting participation is influenced not only by rational policy analysis but also by the emotional mobilization of their social networks [^15].

With the advent of the digital age, the battleground of elections has gradually shifted to online communities and meme dissemination [^14], but physical campaign evenings still possess an irreplaceable "theatrical effect"; they are the clearest and most indispensable collective memory in Taiwan's democratic process.
