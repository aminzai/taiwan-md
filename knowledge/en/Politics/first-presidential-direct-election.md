On March 23, 1996, Taiwan elected its president and vice president by direct popular vote for the first time. That day, 10.76 million people cast ballots, a turnout of 76.04%. Lee Teng-hui and Lien Chan won 54% of the vote.[^1] On the same day, the Kinmen garrison remained on heightened alert under Chinese military pressure; ballot boxes and a military crisis appeared simultaneously in Taiwan's reality that morning.[^2]

This election featured four presidential-vice presidential tickets: the KMT's Lee Teng-hui and Lien Chan, the DPP's Peng Ming-min and Hsieh Chang-ting, and two independent tickets — Lin Yang-kang and Hau Pei-tsun, and Chen Li-an and Wang Ching-feng.[^1] On the surface, this was a presidential election; looked at more deeply, it was an institutional relocation of "who has the right to decide the national leader" from the National Assembly chamber into the people's hands.

## Before a Single Ballot, How Was the President Produced

The Central Election Commission's election history records the first presidential election in 1948 as having elected Chiang Kai-shek by National Assembly delegates, with Li Tsung-jen elected vice president. This indirect election and the 1996 direct popular vote form the clearest before-and-after contrast of Taiwan's institutional transformation.[^3]

On May 20, 1949, martial law was imposed in Taiwan. It was not lifted until July 15, 1987. The Central Election Commission also lists the 1991 termination of the Period of National Mobilization for Suppression of the Communist Rebellion, the 1992 full re-election of legislators, and the 1994 first direct election of provincial governors and special municipality mayors by provincial and municipal residents as nodes in the gradual reconstruction of democratic institutions.[^3]

1996 had a clear institutional genealogy. Before it came the lifting of martial law, parliamentary re-election, direct election of local executives, and a power structure originally designed for emergency times that was continuously dismantled. Academia Historica's scholarly compilation notes that the constitutional engineering of the Lee Teng-hui era addressed the mobilization-for-suppression system, the long-unre-elected parliament, and direct popular election of the president, among other issues.[^4]

## After Wild Lily, Direct Election Became an Operable Institution

In March 1990, the Wild Lily student movement demanded abolition of the National Assembly, abolition of the Temporary Provisions, convening of a National Affairs Conference, and a reform timetable. These demands later moved from the square into institutional discussion. In a 2026 retrospective on the history of direct elections, the Presidential Office also placed the Wild Lily student movement, the following year's full parliamentary re-election, and the 1996 direct presidential election on the same democratization path.[^5]

Central Election Commission data shows the Period of National Mobilization for Suppression of the Communist Rebellion terminated on May 1, 1991. A National Assembly election was held in December 1991, and the full re-election of legislators was completed in December 1992.[^3] The significance of these dates lies in their representing a parliament that could no longer permanently retain seats on the grounds of an emergency period beginning to face re-authorization by Taiwan's people.

On July 28, 1994, the National Assembly passed a constitutional amendment establishing direct election of the president and vice president, adopting a joint ticket system, changing the presidential term to four years with a one-term limit, to take effect from 1996.[^1] This constitutional decision converted a political slogan into ballot design: what the people cast was a jointly accountable national leadership team.

The institutional transition also changed the direction of political accountability. Under indirect election, the president's emergence was mainly accomplished by National Assembly delegates; after direct election, candidates had to place their platforms, images, and crisis-management abilities directly before the entire electorate. This did not mean all problems of the presidential system were solved overnight, but it gave the legitimacy of the highest executive authority a ballot entrance that could be periodically tested. The Central Election Commission's electoral system evolution and Academia Historica's constitutional compilation happen to provide two angles: institutional text and historical context.[^3] [^4]

## Four Tickets Lay Out the Ballot

1996 is often condensed to "Lee Teng-hui elected." This result is certainly important, but remembering only the winner misses how this election accommodated different imaginations of Taiwan. CNA-compiled candidate data shows the DPP fielded Peng Ming-min and Hsieh Chang-ting; Lin Yang-kang and Hau Pei-tsun ran as independents; Chen Li-an and Wang Ching-feng formed another independent ticket.[^1]

Peng Ming-min was a major figure in Taiwan's democracy movement; Lin Yang-kang was a heavyweight candidate after a KMT split. The four tickets appearing on the ballot let 1996 exceed the binary of ruling party versus opposition party, and also revealed differing judgments within the KMT on reform pace, national positioning, and cross-Strait risk. CNA's retrospective on the 1996 election notes that the KMT mainstream and non-mainstream held different views on direct presidential election, and the divergence ultimately led some to leave the KMT and form the New Party.[^1]

The election also began to require new political technologies. CNA-preserved photos and text records include the first televised presidential candidate policy presentation on February 25, 1996, as well as candidate debates, platform promotion, and candidacy registration scenes.[^1] When presidential candidates had to explain policies to voters before television cameras, the legitimacy of the national leader was no longer decided solely by intra-party negotiation but also had to undergo open contest.

The "first" here cannot be understood merely as a media event. It was simultaneously a redistribution of electoral accountability: candidates faced nationwide policy comparison, and voters for the first time compared four different political lines on the same presidential ballot. This made 1996 both institutional history and political communication history. Campaign images, policy presentations, and on-site voting together constituted a new public memory.[^1]

This vote was also closely watched by international media. CNA's retrospective notes that at the time, over 500 journalists from more than 260 international media outlets covered the election. For a society rewriting how its president is produced, polling stations simultaneously became windows through which the world watched Taiwan's democratic transition.[^1]

Academia Sinica's database preserves a survey study targeting 1996 presidential voters. The study simultaneously asked voters whom they voted for, party identification, candidate image, unification/independence views, media use, social networks, and attitudes toward PRC military exercises. The final sample was 1,406 cases.[^6] This lets us see that the "people" of direct election are not a neat, uniform group, but individuals each carrying their own memories, identities, and risk assessments.

## How Election Day Was Organized

The first direct presidential election also tested whether election administration could turn the new institution into executable daily procedure. The Central Election Commission's election history places the 1991 National Assembly re-election, 1992 full legislative re-election, 1994 direct election of provincial governors and mayors, and 1996 direct presidential election on the same institutional evolution line. These elections let voters gradually familiarize themselves with voting, vote counting, candidate registration, and election result announcement procedures.[^3]

The political significance of election day therefore lies in "island-wide synchronization": voters in different localities expressed their choices on the same day using the same joint presidential-vice presidential ballot, and results were aggregated by the central election authority. This procedural synchronization shifted presidential legitimacy from negotiation among specific political elites to an outcome jointly witnessed by voters nationwide. This is an institutional detail, yet it is the foundation on which democratic daily life stands.[^3]

## Missiles and Ballots Arrive Simultaneously

One starting point of the 1995 crisis was the U.S. decision in May 1995 to allow Lee Teng-hui to speak at Cornell University. The National Defense University Press's compilation on the 1995–1996 Taiwan Strait Crisis notes that China viewed Taiwan's democratization and "pragmatic diplomacy" as potential moves toward independence, and during the crisis suspended cross-Strait dialogue, conducted missile launches and military exercises.[^7]

The crisis timeline spanned two elections: the 1995 legislative election in Taiwan, and the 1996 direct presidential election. Military signals were therefore directed not only at the government but also entered the environment of voter judgment. The National Defense University study summarizes Beijing's objectives as influencing U.S. policy, forcing Taiwan to abandon efforts to expand its international space, and weakening the political support of Lee Teng-hui and forces supporting Taiwan subjectivity. These are researchers' summaries of policy objectives, not directly equivalent to every individual voter's actual voting motives.[^7]

By the period before the 1996 presidential election, the People's Liberation Army conducted missile launches and large-scale military exercises around Taiwan. The National Defense University study notes that in March 1996 the U.S. dispatched two carrier battle groups near Taiwan waters to convey military signals while avoiding crisis escalation.[^7] A Reuters retrospective also notes that the missile tests before the 1996 direct election were believed intended to deter Taiwan voters from supporting Lee Teng-hui, whom Beijing suspected of pro-independence leanings.[^8]

Caution is needed against a common simplification: not all ballot changes can be directly attributed to missiles. The New York Times reported on March 24, 1996 that Lee Teng-hui won 54% support from nearly 11 million voters, and that the sense of crisis from military exercises may have caused some voters originally supporting the largest opposition party to shift toward Lee Teng-hui.[^9] "May have" is the key word. This is an interpretation offered by a contemporary report, not an erasure of other voter motives.

Scholarly articles also treat the 1996 election as a political event worthy of independent study. Thomas J. Bellows published "The March 1996 Elections in the Republic of China on Taiwan" in the _American Journal of Chinese Studies_, Vol. 3, No. 2, 1996, pp. 235–249. The JSTOR article page confirms the author, journal, and publication information.[^10] This reminds us that this election is both the lived experience of Taiwan's people and an object of analysis in international politics and democratic studies.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** Missiles cannot fill out ballots for voters, but they change the question in each person's heart as they walk into the polling station: what I'm voting for today is a candidate, and also whether I accept someone else deciding for me.

## 54%: From Victory to Institution

Lee Teng-hui and Lien Chan ultimately received 5,813,699 votes, 54%; Peng Ming-min and Hsieh Chang-ting received 2,274,586 votes, 21.11%; Lin Yang-kang and Hau Pei-tsun received 1,603,790 votes, 14.89%; Chen Li-an and Wang Ching-feng received 1,074,044 votes, 9.98%. A total of 10.76 million voters cast ballots, turnout 76.04%.[^1]

These numbers can answer who won, but cannot themselves answer what the election "represents." The New York Times at the time described the 54% as a strong mandate for Lee Teng-hui, and also reported that some voters viewed the military crisis as a political signal requiring a response.[^9] But Academia Sinica's voter study design shows that election results need to be understood from multiple variables: candidate evaluation, party identification, issue positions, media, and social networks.[^6]

Therefore, the more prudent statement is: the 1996 54% simultaneously possesses two natures. It is the winning proportion of Lee Teng-hui and Lien Chan among four tickets, and it is the institutional outcome of Taiwan's first determination of presidential legitimacy by universal suffrage. The latter layer is more durable than the former, because it does not disappear with the victory or defeat of the next election.

The Central Election Commission records the 1996 election as the first direct popular election of a four-year-term president and vice president.[^3] This four-year rhythm later became the temporal sense of Taiwan's political life: candidates must return to the people, parties must reorganize, policies must answer to the ballot's questioning.

## Direct Election Leaves Controversy for the Next Vote

Taiwan has since experienced multiple presidential elections and party alternations. The value of the direct election system therefore lies not only in the first result, but in its establishment of a repeatable political cycle: incumbents must accept a new round of authorization at term's end, and opposition parties can transform dissatisfaction into candidates, platforms, and ballot competition. FPRI's political analysis reviews a series of peaceful and legitimate elections after Taiwan's democratic transition, and notes that elections often link local governance, party competition, and cross-Strait policy.[^3] [^11] The institution established in 1996 did not eliminate controversy. It placed controversy into a procedure in which the people can periodically intervene.

This also explains why direct presidential election and the national identity issue always appear together. The Presidential Office's 2026 retrospective manuscript understands direct election as a milestone of popular sovereignty, democratic legitimacy, and Taiwan subjectivity. This is the current government's political interpretation of history; readers should read it alongside the Central Election Commission's electoral system data, Academia Historica's historical compilation, and academic research, without treating any single official narrative as the complete answer.[^5]

Direct election still has its limits. It lets the people choose the president, but does not guarantee all public policies can be fully discussed; it lets voters replace incumbents, but does not automatically eliminate party polarization, media bias, or power asymmetry between central and local governments. Lee Teng-hui-related scholarly compilation also discusses democratic reform's institutional achievements alongside subsequent bottlenecks such as the representative system and centralization in the same article.[^4]

So what is most worth remembering about 1996 is not the overly smooth conclusion that "Taiwan completed democratization from then on." The more accurate understanding is that Taiwan turned democracy into an institution that must be repeatedly used and repeatedly calibrated. The first vote did not guarantee answers for later generations; it only placed the power to decide in the hands of later generations.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The true legacy of direct election falls in the re-authorization every four years: no one can forever hold that 54% in trust for the people.

On March 23, 1996, Taiwan did not wait for risk to disappear before voting. Voters completed voting while military threats still existed, and the institution gained its first island-scale civic verification that day.[^1] The crisis need not be romanticized, and not every ballot was cast for the same reason; what is certain is that democracy's weight is often declared not in calm but in actual use while uncertainty has not yet exited.

From National Assembly delegates voting, to the people voting directly; from martial law and emergency period, to candidates debating before television cameras; from a frozen national imagination, to re-facing voter scrutiny every four years, 1996 shifted the subject of Taiwan's politics. That subject is imperfect, inconsistent, not always rational, but it must be seen: the people.
