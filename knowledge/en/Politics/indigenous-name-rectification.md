# Indigenous Peoples' Name Rectification Movement: From "Shanbao" to Collective Subject in the Constitution

> **30-second overview:** The 1990s Indigenous name rectification movement centered on a designation on the surface, but its core was whether the state could continue describing a people with a name left by rulers. The movement demanded state recognition that Indigenous peoples themselves hold the right to name, organize, and determine their future. After the 1984 founding of the Indigenous Rights Promotion Organization, name rectification gradually became bound together with land rights, autonomy rights, political participation, and cultural preservation. The 1991 and 1992 constitutional amendment struggles did not succeed immediately. The 1994 Constitutional Amendments changed "Shanbao" to "Indigenous people" (原住民), and 1997 adopted "Indigenous peoples" (原住民族) with collective rights significance. This path ran through streets, churches, communities, the National Assembly, and the Legislative Yuan; institutional outcomes were formed through long-term negotiation across multiple arenas.

## "Shanbao" Was Not a Neutral Old Designation

When a state names a group, it looks like administrative terminology but actually determines who gets registered, who receives resources, who is treated as individuals, and who as a collective. The Council of Indigenous Peoples' English history page compiles the designations for Taiwan's Indigenous peoples under different regimes: "fan" (番, "barbarians") in the Qing era, "Takasago-zoku" (高砂族) during Japanese rule, and "Shanbao" (山胞, "mountain compatriots") used by the postwar Nationalist government. These names were decided by rulers and placed ethnic groups into administrative categories.[^1]

The problem with "Shanbao" lies not only in its discriminatory connotations but in how it turned Indigenous peoples into objects needing management. The "mountain" (山) in the designation binds ethnicity to geography, as if their political identity relates only to remote mountain areas. This classification cannot fully describe Pingpu (plains) groups, urban Indigenous people, or those living outside traditional territories, nor can it express each group's distinct history, language, systems, and collective memory. The name rectification movement demanded state recognition: Indigenous peoples are not merely classified subjects on administrative forms, but have the right to participate in determining their own names and political position.

The Council of Indigenous Peoples records that on December 29, 1984, Indigenous intellectuals founded the Taiwan Indigenous Rights Promotion Association, using "Indigenous people" (原住民)—a designation chosen by the people themselves—and placing anti-stigma, land, culture, and political status within a single rights framework. The 1987 _Declaration of Taiwan Indigenous Rights_ further advanced this self-naming into the language of collective rights.[^1] Academic research published by Academia Sinica's Institute of Linguistics also regards the 1984 organization as a key starting point of the name rectification movement, noting how media and state designations affect how Taiwan's Indigenous peoples are understood.[^2]

## Name Rectification Was Never a Single Demand

Writing the name rectification movement merely as "changing Shanbao to Indigenous people" misses the movement's true political content. When Indigenous peoples raised name rectification demands during the 1991 National Assembly constitutional amendments, a common slogan was "Right to Rectify Names, Land Rights, Autonomy Rights." Taitung County Government's Indigenous Peoples' Day materials record that on June 23, 1994, Indigenous groups held the third "Name Rectification, Land and Autonomy Rights into the Constitution" grand parade before the Presidential Office, with 37 organizations responding.[^3]

These three terms interconnect. The right to rectify names demands state recognition of Indigenous peoples' subject identity. Land rights demand state recognition that traditional territories are not spaces for arbitrary disposal. Autonomy rights demand that Indigenous peoples, as political subjects, participate in decisions in their own social, cultural, and life domains, not merely accept state guarantees. Without land and autonomy, name rectification might remain only on identification documents and statutes. Without name rectification, land and autonomy also struggle to find a recognized rights-bearing subject in state institutions.

| Movement Language            | Political Issue Addressed                                                        | Possible Institutional Landing Points                                                         |
| ---------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Right to Rectify Names       | Who has authority to determine the group's name and identity                     | Constitutional terminology, household registration, administrative agencies, public education |
| Land Rights                  | Whether traditional territories are merely state-manageable land                 | Land policy, consultation and consent, traditional territories and remedies                   |
| Autonomy Rights              | Whether Indigenous peoples possess collective political decision-making capacity | Autonomy systems, ethnic councils, local and central authority                                |
| Cultural and Language Rights | Who decides how history, language, and knowledge are preserved                   | Education, language policy, cultural assets, media                                            |

The Council of Indigenous Peoples' later explanation of Indigenous Peoples' Day also places name rectification alongside self-determination, autonomy, land, and cultural rights in the same historical context. This linkage gradually became the movement's common language in the late 1980s and early 1990s, later entering policy discussions.[^1]

## The National Assembly's Door: Name Rectification Demands Blocked for the First Time

On April 15, 1991, Indigenous peoples raised the first name rectification protest during the National Assembly constitutional amendments. The Council of Indigenous Peoples' English article records that Indigenous peoples then demanded changing "Shanbao" to "Indigenous people" (原住民), viewing this demand as an expression of collective will. However, the first constitutional amendment did not adopt the name rectification demand; the Constitutional Amendments retained the "Shanbao" terminology.[^1]

The significance of this failure lies in making activists clearly see that political sympathy does not equal institutional recognition. Indigenous peoples could be called objects needing education, assistance, and welfare, but as long as statutes continued using the state-given name, Indigenous peoples had not truly become speaking subjects in the Constitution. The amendment process appears to be legal technique but actually decides whose self-description can enter the state's highest norms.

During the 1992 second constitutional amendment, the name rectification movement mobilized protests again, still without full response. This process also shows the movement could not change law with just one large parade. Indigenous groups had to repeatedly appear in the sightlines of the National Assembly, Presidential Office, and public media, and make different ethnic groups, parties, and state agencies understand that name rectification involves the state's long-term power relations in describing and managing Indigenous peoples—it cannot be reduced to a special privilege.

## The Constitution Changed the Designation, but Rights Remained Outside the Door

The Council of Indigenous Peoples' official English article records that on April 10, 1994, then-President Lee Teng-hui, at an Indigenous cultural conference in Pingtung, first addressed this group as "Indigenous people" (原住民). On April 14, the KMT Constitutional Amendment Planning Group placed the "Shanbao" replacement content into the amendment draft. The June 23 grand parade brought name rectification, land, and autonomy rights together to the Presidential Office. On July 1, Lee Teng-hui received Indigenous constitutional movement representatives, pledging support for name rectification.[^1]

On July 28, 1994, the National Assembly passed the Third Constitutional Amendments. On August 1, the President promulgated and enacted the amendment provisions; "Shanbao" was formally changed from constitutional terminology to "Indigenous people" (原住民). The Presidential Office's Third Constitutional Amendment page preserves the amendment provisions and Indigenous political participation clauses from that time.[^4]

This was an important achievement, but not the endpoint of all demands. The Third Amendment provisions mainly addressed Indigenous identity, political participation, education, cultural preservation, social welfare, and enterprise assistance, and did not at once complete traditional territory confirmation, autonomy systems, or complete collective decision-making rights. The Indigenous Rights Handbook also lists the 1994 name rectification, 1996 Council establishment, 1997 Indigenous peoples clause, and 2005 _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_ separately, reminding readers not to mistake a single designation change for resolution of all institutional problems.[^6]

## Kao Cheng-chih and "Revoting": Political Risk in the Chamber

The name rectification movement cannot be written only as a story of street pressure on the government. In the National Assembly chamber on July 28, 1994, Indigenous representatives had to handle a very concrete procedural crisis: how provisions would obtain sufficient votes, how different party versions would be negotiated, how representative quotas and ethnic classifications would be written into the Constitution.

A 2024 PTS in-depth report returns to the Legislative Yuan Congressional Library's preserved July 28, 1994 National Assembly proceedings, noting that Paiwan National Assembly delegate Kao Cheng-chih (高正治), after the relevant provisions failed two votes, proposed a "revote" (重行表決). Lin Cheng-erh (林正二) handled the co-signature, requiring 30 National Assembly delegates' signatures to proceed, ultimately obtaining 31. The meeting record preserves scenes of "commotion from the floor," "surrounding the chair and clamoring," and multiple interrupted speeches, showing name rectification was not quietly bestowed by the state.[^7]

This detail also complicates political responsibility. Kao Cheng-chih's revote gave the name rectification provision a chance to re-enter the agenda, but the final passed version remained the result of concessions among parties. The PTS report notes that DPP National Assembly delegates hoped to use "Taiwan Indigenous Peoples" to break the mountain/plains classification, while the KMT version retained the Mountain Indigenous and Plains Indigenous framework. The final passed provisions cannot be fully equated with the version movement participants originally envisioned.[^7]

Therefore, the 1994 success simultaneously contains victory and incompleteness. The state finally ceased using "Shanbao" as a constitutional designation, but statutes retained issues of classification, representative quotas, and land rights. Name rectification brought Indigenous peoples into the Constitution, but did not automatically give all Indigenous peoples equally full speaking space within it.

## From Individual Identity to Collective Subject: The Weight of Plural Terminology

The 1994 "Indigenous people" (原住民) was an important individual and group identity designation; the 1997 "Indigenous peoples" (原住民族) further carried collective rights significance. The Council of Indigenous Peoples' official article explains that during the fourth constitutional amendment, the plural term "Indigenous peoples" (原住民族) with collective rights implications replaced "Indigenous people" (原住民).[^1] This plural terminology pushed the political subject's position toward collective rights.

"Indigenous people" (原住民) can refer to a citizen with Indigenous identity. "Indigenous peoples" (原住民族) demands state recognition of the ethnic group as a collective possessing rights to history, culture, land, autonomy, and political organization. This collective rights language later interconnected with the _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_. The National Law Database's regulation page preserves the full text of the _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_; the law places Indigenous identity, traditional culture, land, natural resources, autonomy, education, language, and consultation consent into a single statutory framework.[^5]

The Academia Sinica Indigenous Historical and Cultural Knowledge Network's introduction to the _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_ also notes that during the 1994 constitutional amendment, Indigenous representatives proposed amendment suggestions on ethnic recognition, autonomy, central dedicated agency, ethnic council, and traditional naming rights, which were not all incorporated at the time. The 1997 constitutional amendment expanded Indigenous peoples clauses but still cannot be understood as completing all movement demands.[^8]

## From Name Rectification to the Council of Indigenous Peoples: Administrative Agency Is Not the Endpoint

![2008 Taiwan Indigenous Peoples Craft Exhibition Opening, Deputy Minister Watan Kiso of the Council of Indigenous Peoples, photographer Rico Shen, CC BY-SA 4.0.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e3/2008_Taiwan_Indigenous_Peoples_Craft_Exhibition_Opening_Watan_Kiso.jpg)

_Caption: Photographed by Rico Shen in 2008 at the Indigenous Craft Exhibition opening, Wikimedia Commons file page marked CC BY-SA 4.0. This is a post-name-rectification administrative and cultural policy scene, not a photo of the 1990s protest sites. It shows how the dedicated agency subsequently engaged with cultural and rights issues.[^11]_

The 1996 establishment of the Council of Indigenous Peoples, Executive Yuan, gave the post-name-rectification political subject a central-level dedicated agency. The Indigenous Rights Handbook lists the 1996 Council establishment as an important sequel to the name rectification movement, linking it with the 1994 name rectification, 1997 constitutional amendment, and 2005 _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_ into an institutional timeline.[^6]

The dedicated agency's appearance has two faces. One side: administrative responsibility became clearer; Indigenous education, culture, social welfare, and land policy no longer had to be scattered across different ministries. The other side: Indigenous rights might be re-incorporated into a policy classification managed by a central agency. The name rectification movement originally demanded subjectivity and autonomy; if the administrative agency only handles subsidy distribution, cultural activity management, or statistics production, it might turn "Indigenous peoples" once again into objects of governance.

The _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_'s importance lies in writing Indigenous rights as legal obligations the state should follow, not merely welfare policy. But legal passage does not mean rights are implemented. Land delineation, traditional territories, resource development, autonomy systems, and consultation consent all require subsequent regulations, administrative decisions, court rulings, and local negotiation. The name rectification movement's historical significance precisely lies in letting these subsequent disputes no longer be describable only as "mountain administration" or "minority welfare."

## Indigenous Peoples' Day: Memory Fixed, Also Possibly Simplified

![Tree-lined avenue in Wushe Incident Memorial Park, photographer jeffreyjhang, CC BY-SA 2.0.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e9/The_forest_road_in_Wushe_Incident_Memorial_Park.jpg)

_Caption: Photographed by jeffreyjhang in 2013 at Wushe Incident Memorial Park, Wikimedia Commons file page marked CC BY-SA 2.0. This is a later-formed historical memory space, cannot serve as on-site imagery of the 1930 Wushe Incident or 1990s name rectification protests. It illustrates how Indigenous history is preserved in commemoration, parks, and public education.[^12]_

The Executive Yuan passed relevant regulations in 2005, designating August 1 as Indigenous Peoples' Day. This date corresponds to the August 1, 1994 promulgation and enactment date of the Constitutional Amendments. The Council of Indigenous Peoples' official article notes that Indigenous Peoples' Day commemorates the name change while reminding society of the self-determination, land, autonomy, and cultural rights behind name rectification.[^1]

The commemorative day provides a fixed public time for looking back. Schools, government agencies, and media can re-discuss name rectification history every August 1, and the institutional background of the old designation "Shanbao" is less easily forgotten. But the commemorative day may also compress over a decade of struggle into a pretty date. Speaking only of August 1, readers may not see the 1984 organization founding, 1987 Rights Declaration, 1991 and 1992 constitutional amendment failures, 1994 street parade, and the risk Kao Cheng-chih took proposing a revote in the chamber.

The PTS report cites Kao Cheng-chih's lifetime remarks, noting that the three characters "Indigenous people" (原住民) then touched not only legal terminology but also different ethnic groups' anxieties about Taiwan's history, land resources, and identity position.[^7] This uncomfortable part should be preserved. Name rectification was not a consensus naturally formed after everyone understood simultaneously. The process contained conflict, discrimination, negotiation, and political exchange. Only by preserving the conflict can Indigenous Peoples' Day avoid becoming a depoliticized festival slogan.

## What One Name Change Changed, and What It Did Not

The name rectification movement's most direct achievement was the state formally abandoning "Shanbao" as a constitutional designation, adopting the name Indigenous peoples themselves advocated. This change affects identity documents, legal texts, educational content, administrative agencies, and public language. It also forced the state to stipulate in the Constitution Indigenous political participation, education, culture, and social welfare guarantees.

A deeper achievement: name rectification shifted "who are Taiwan's Indigenous peoples" from a ruler's classification problem to a political problem Indigenous peoples have the right to participate in deciding. This turn connected later Indigenous autonomy, traditional territories, ethnic naming, language revitalization, and cultural asset disputes. Name rectification let more issues surface. The state cannot recognize subjectivity only in designation while continuing to treat Indigenous peoples as protected, managed objects in land and decision-making.

But name rectification also did not automatically change reality. Indigenous land rights still need clear legal procedures and administrative execution. Autonomy still needs concrete institutional design; constitutional affirmation itself is insufficient to complete systems. Indigenous languages still face pressures in education, usage domains, and intergenerational transmission. State recognition of multiculturalism also does not equal every group having equal bargaining power in resource allocation and policy design.

| Time        | Completed Institutional Changes                                    | Problems Not Resolved by Name Alone                                                                   |
| ----------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| 1984–1987   | Indigenous rights organization and Rights Declaration formed       | How movement gains sustained political representation                                                 |
| 1991–1992   | Name rectification demands enter constitutional amendment protests | State still refuses "Shanbao" change                                                                  |
| 1994        | "Indigenous People" written into Constitution                      | Land, autonomy, ethnic classification still contested                                                 |
| 1996–1997   | Council established, "Indigenous Peoples" enters Constitution      | How dedicated agency avoids recentralizing governance                                                 |
| 2005 onward | _Indigenous Peoples Basic Act_ provides rights framework           | Traditional territories, autonomy, consultation consent, implementation still need institutional work |

The core political question the name rectification movement leaves: after the state recognizes a name, does it truly change its governance approach toward this group? If the name changes but land decisions still exclude Indigenous participation, autonomy remains only declarative, and language and history remain marginalized in mainstream education, then name rectification has only completed the first step of subjectivity.

## After Writing the Name into the Constitution

The Indigenous name rectification movement added a strand that cannot be omitted to Taiwan's democratization. Democracy includes electoral systems, party competition, and direct presidential elections, but also demands state recognition that different groups have the right to describe themselves, preserve their own systems, and participate in deciding land and public resources. From this angle, the 1994 constitutional amendment belongs to constitutional reform itself, and is an indispensable part of Taiwan's democratization.

Kao Cheng-chih proposing a revote in the 1994 chamber, street groups demanding name rectification, land, and autonomy before the Presidential Office, Indigenous organizations building rights language in the 1980s—these actions together turned a designation originally decided by the state into public controversy. The state's later ability to write "Indigenous peoples" into the Constitution originated from long-term political questioning: who has the right to name, who has the right to be a political subject, who has the right to determine their own future.

Viewing the Indigenous name rectification movement today, August 1 should not be treated as an already-completed period, but better read as a comma. It marks "Shanbao" exiting the Constitution, while reminding later generations that name, land, autonomy, language, and historical subjectivity remain interconnected. After name rectification, the truly difficult work begins: letting the collective subject the Constitution recognizes also be seen and heard in daily policy and public decisions, and possess actual decision-making capacity.
