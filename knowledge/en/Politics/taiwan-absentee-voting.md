> **30-Second Overview:** Most Taiwanese voters still need to vote at their registered domicile; non-resident voting is not just mail or internet voting. Election staff already have limited options for voting at work, and the new system for Indigenous peoples' voting will be added to local elections in 2026. The real difficulty lies in how to expand convenience while safeguarding personal suffrage, secret ballot casting, ballot custody, and public counting. With an estimated 8,896 types of ballots from the Nine-in-One elections, this multiple-choice question is particularly concrete.

The 8,896 types of ballots are a type estimation made by the Central Election Commission for the 2026 Nine-in-One elections.

On January 26, 2026, a public hearing on non-resident voting legislation was held in Conference Room 202 of the Legislative Yuan's Red House. Cai Ming-ru, Deputy Director General of the Taipei City Election Commission, pointed out that if local elections fully adopted absentee voting, the number of polling places alone could increase by two to three times, and manpower would also need to increase. On the other side, academics supporting reform questioned whether this estimation lacked sufficient basis.[^1][^2]

What was discussed at the conference table seemed like a simple wish: allowing someone outside their domicile to cast a vote for their registered constituency. What was pressing underneath, however, were nearly nine thousand types of ballots, cross-county voter rolls, sealed transport, secret voting, and counting supervision.

> **8,896 Types**
>
> This is the Central Election Commission's estimate of the types of ballots for local public official elections in 2026, not the actual number of printed sheets. Each type of ballot corresponds to a constituency and a set of candidate lists.[^1]

What is most visible about non-resident voting is traveling one less trip. What truly determines how far the system can go is whether the voter leaving their domicile still trusts the results along the way.

## Non-Resident Voting Is Not Just One Method

"Non-resident" describes voters who do not vote at the typically designated time or place, and it is not a technical limitation. The International IDEA places advance voting, mail voting, mobile ballot boxes, proxy voting, special polling stations, overseas voting, and out-of-constituency voting within the scope of special voting arrangements. With each added method, election authorities also increase the work related to identity verification, ballot delivery, custody, transport, counting, and auditing.[^3]

The most common "absentee voting" discussed in Taiwan still retains paper ballots and polling places. Voters first apply to move their polling location elsewhere; on voting day, they go themselves with identification to collect the ballot for their original constituency, mark it, and deposit it into a ballot box. The Central Election Commission condenses this practice into four conditions: "the individual, in person, on the voting day, goes to the polling place."[^1]

Other methods involve more changes:

| Method                     | What Changed                    | Main System Issues                                                                               |
| :------------------------- | :------------------------------ | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Absentee Voting (Transfer) | Changes the polling place       | Ballot preparation from another location, voter roll deduplication, ballot box and result return |
| Advance Voting             | Changes the voting day          | Early sealing, custody period, information lag                                                   |
| Mail Voting                | Changes delivery and collection | Free will, proxy voting, loss and deadline determination                                         |
| Special Polling Stations   | Changes service location        | Secret and public supervision within prisons, hospitals, or military camps                       |
| Internet Voting            | Changes medium                  | Separation of identity and ballot, cybersecurity, auditability                                   |

These systems can all make voting easier, but the costs and risks are different. When lumped together as "non-resident voting" and asked for a simple yes or no answer, it often makes people think they are answering the same question.

Some people think about voting in Hualien while registered in Taipei; some think about mailing ballots home; and some think about voting via mobile overseas. ## The Domicile Principle Already Has Two Narrow Gates

Article 17 of the current _Public Officials Election and Recall Act_ is direct: voters must vote at their registered domicile's polling place. The first narrow gate is for polling station staff. They can vote at their workplace, but their domicile and workplace must be in the same constituency or city/county. This is more like an adjustment of a workplace within the same district, far from cross-county transfer.[^4]

The second narrow gate appeared at the end of 2025. After the Legislative Yuan passed amendments, Article 57 now requires election authorities to establish separate polling places or conduct various public official elections in regional polling places to protect Indigenous peoples' secret voting rights, thereby excluding domicile restrictions. The Ministry of the Interior also publicly pointed out that this amendment only exempts specific statuses and remains controversial regarding fairness and implementation.[^4][^5]

The Central Election Commission conducted a simulation drill for the new system of Indigenous peoples' voting in August 2026 for local elections. Taiwan has already passed the "whether or not there is non-resident voting" stage; the system is moving, but the scope is narrow, and every step encounters the same set of problems: where to keep the ballots, who can collect them, and how to prevent outsiders from guessing choices based on small numbers.[^6]

This new system for Indigenous peoples' voting starts with secret voting, not transportation convenience. In polling places with few Indigenous voters, a small number of votes cast might make individual choices easily guessable. Consolidating or transferring ballots can protect secrecy. The logistics, ballot types, and administrative burden after consolidation require new design.

Taiwan first truly touched the core of non-resident voting and found that convenience and secrecy sometimes stand on the same side, and sometimes pull against each other.

## A Law That Has Been Negotiated for Many Years

A comprehensive system did not suddenly appear in 2026. Article 25 of the _Referendum Act_ has long left room, allowing national referendums to stipulate non-resident voting by law. In February 2024, the Executive Yuan re-passed a draft of the _National Referendum Non-Resident Voting Law_, choosing domestic transfer voting: voters must apply sixty days before the vote, and on the voting day, they still go in person to the designated polling place. This draft does not apply if the referendum is held on the same day as a national election.[^7]

This version deliberately starts from the context of "only one type of national referendum." The Central Election Commission also suggested in its written report for the 2026 public hearing to accumulate experience with national referendums first, and then gradually handle public official elections. A referendum is voting on an issue; when transferring, there is no need to move candidate lists to every corner of the nation.[^1]

Another path went further. The draft _Non-Resident Voting Law_ proposed by the Taiwan People's Party includes presidential, vice-presidential, central and local public official elections, recalls, and national referendums. Legislative Yuan records show that the draft was extracted from committee, passed a second reading, and delivered for negotiation in December 2025, with negotiations held on January 15, 2026. As of September 1, 2026, the page does not show completion of the third reading.[^8]

Therefore, "why Taiwan doesn't have non-resident voting" is not precise. A closer question is: Taiwan has two narrow arrangements, a draft from the Executive Yuan starting with national referendums, and a parliamentary draft covering various elections. Politics and election authorities have not yet reached a consensus on large-scale implementation.

## Who Bears the Burden of Returning Home?

For those whose domicile and living location are the same, voting might just mean going to a nearby school. For those working, studying, or caring for family in other counties or cities, it means a bus ticket, a weekend, or an unmanageable schedule. Residents of outlying islands face an extra layer of uncertainty due to weather conditions.

Military personnel, police, firefighters, medical staff, and others on duty on voting days may be ensuring others can vote smoothly. Voters who are hospitalized, have mobility issues, or require long-term care cannot necessarily reach the polling place even if the law provides assistance within the facility. The common service recipients listed by International IDEA also include those from other locations, inpatients or homebound individuals, people with disabilities, public officials, and detainees.[^3]

The situation of prisoners makes the contradiction most obvious. A case before the Constitutional Court in 2026 recorded a prisoner with voting rights who requested a polling place be set up in a detention center. The ruling was ultimately not accepted, and no substantive judgment on whether the system is unconstitutional was made. The court record clearly shows the current situation: the law has no special procedure for prison voting, and it is difficult for prisoners to return to their domicile under custody.[^9]

As for overseas voters, the National Commission for Indian Overseas Citizens noted in its public hearing report that the constitutional amendment uses the wording "return country" to exercise presidential and vice-presidential election rights; therefore, changing this election to overseas voting involves a constitutional amendment level. Other public official elections and domestic transfer voting must be discussed separately.[^10]

What non-resident voting seeks to fill is the distance between "a vote exists legally" and "the vote cannot reach the ballot box in reality." But if the system only serves those who are easiest to apply for or most familiar with digital tools, it may replace the original geographical barrier with an administrative one.

## 8,896 Types of Ballots: Not Every Sheet Can Go Home

In presidential elections, where the nation is one constituency, there is only one type of presidential ballot. When legislative and presidential elections are combined, the Central Election Commission calculates regional, Indigenous, and non-constituency ballots, totaling 76 different types of ballots. For the Nine-in-One local elections, which hold county/city mayors, councilors, township mayors, representatives, Indigenous district chiefs, and village heads on the same day, the estimate reaches 8,896 in 2026.[^1]

If a voter registered in Hualien applies to vote in Taipei, the polling place in Taipei must prepare the Hualien ballot they are entitled to collect, while ensuring the Hualien roll does not allow them to collect it again. The ballots are cast in Taipei, and the results must be safely and accurately sent back for counting in the original constituency. If the ballots are counted in Hualien after being sent back, the sealing and transport time will be extended.

The candidate lists for local elections look similar, but they cannot be issued incorrectly. Each polling place also has to handle official announcements, notifications, venues, staff training, and abnormality handling. The two to three times increase in polling places mentioned by Cai Ming-ru is a warning from the local election front. Kwei Mei of Kinan University responded at the same hearing: "I don't know how that was calculated," demanding more scientific evidence.[^2]

Putting these two together shows that the dispute is not just about value, but also about an operational model that has not yet been disclosed enough for joint verification. How many polling places need to be added, how many ballots each place needs, and how to control the error rate can all be answered through small-scale trials and public simulations.

Ro Cheng-tsong of Kaohsiung University of Science and Technology judged at the same hearing that a comprehensive system would "take at least until after 2027," because the system and budget could not support the 2026 local elections. This is an academic opinion on the timeline, not a confirmed legal date, but it reminds us of one thing: the date of law passage and the date the system can be safely launched are originally two different dates.[^2]

## Support and Concerns: Protecting Two Types of Democratic Experiences

Supporters of non-resident voting first see voters who are forced to be absent: those who clearly have the right to vote but cannot reach the designated ballot box due to work, study, caregiving, detention, or transportation. If the system can move, citizenship should not be determined by a bus ticket home, and participation in public decisions should no longer depend on whether one day can be cleared and a journey undertaken.

Those who are reserved first see the counting scene after controversy. When numbers approach, any voter roll error, ballot loss, or oversight gap can be magnified into a challenge to the entire election. Convenience has increased, but if the losing side does not trust the process, expanded participation may be offset by distrust.

The entrance to suffrage is connected to the exit of election results: voters must receive their ballots, and they must also believe that every vote was counted correctly. Both these concerns are part of the democratic experience. If reform only calculates how many more voters there are, it misses the latter. If a discussion only lists risks without estimating how many people are excluded by the current system, the former will never have weight.

Therefore, before legislation, there must be repeatable administrative experiments in addition to attitude surveys. Simulate one county or city, publicly showing the types of ballots, venues, and manpower needed under different application volumes. Then, political parties, election staff, and civil groups should go through collecting, sealing, counting, and tallying from scratch. Disputes will still exist, but they can start revolving around the same set of data.

Trial data cannot just conclude with "zero major incidents." The number and reasons for rejected applications, usage patterns by different ages and regions, how small numbers are kept secret, and how much extra time election staff spend should all be recorded for comparison. If convenience is only available to voters in metropolitan areas familiar with online applications, the system must still provide paper application, clear notification, and appeal channels. If errors or duplicate rolls occur, the point where it went wrong must also be disclosed, not pieced together after post-election disputes.

The truly useful questions become smaller and more precise: when is the voter roll locked? How are temporary household transfers handled? How many extra ballots are printed or underprinted? Who transports the ballot box? Where are small batches counted so as not to expose individual choices? Only after answering each question does the system move forward one step.

## A Convenient Vote, Yet Still Trustworthy?

Taiwan's current voting has a very short chain of trust: the individual takes identification to the designated polling place, marks it after screening, deposits the paper ballot into the on-site ballot box, and immediately transitions to counting where anyone can watch the count.[^4] The credibility described by [[選舉過程]] and [[中選會制度]] is largely derived from ordinary people understanding this process.

Non-resident voting lengthens the chain of trust. The design must answer at least five questions:

1. How does one person ensure they only collect one ballot?
2. How do remote polling places obtain the correct constituency and type of ballots?
3. How to prevent pressure from family, employers, prisons, or caregivers during marking?
4. After voting, how are the ballots sealed, transported, tallied, and audited?
5. How can political parties, candidates, and civil observers see every critical stage?

Mail voting pushes free marking and postal custody into the household space. Internet voting must simultaneously prove "this is what you voted" and "no one knows who you voted for." When special polling stations serve prisons, hospitals, or military camps, they also deal with the power relationship between facility managers and voters. The Central Election Commission therefore believes that transfer voting is closer to existing habits than mail, proxy, or electronic voting at this stage.[^1]

The comparison by International IDEA also reminds us that while special arrangements can increase accessibility, they do not necessarily increase voter turnout. Traditional voting remains the benchmark for electoral integrity. With every added arrangement, administrative complexity and cost increase; transparent communication, observation, and auditing are necessary conditions to maintain trust. These international experiences can help Taiwan list problems, but they cannot replace Taiwan's own trial evidence.[^3]

## A Verifiable Progressive Path

Taiwan could first publicly evaluate the application process, ballot collection logistics, error situations, and secrecy protection of the 2026 Indigenous peoples' voting system, and then conduct a domestic transfer voting pilot using only one type of national referendum. The next step would be presidential ballots, followed by handling combined legislative and local elections. This would be a testable path.

The measurement methods for each stage should be announced in advance: application success rate, duplicate roll interception, ballot error, queuing time, manpower, and appeals/recounting cases. After the pilot, the Central Election Commission, local election commissions, political parties, people with disabilities, and Indigenous groups should jointly review to allow supporters and opponents to debate using the same data set.

The applicable scope changes the entire operation. If it is limited only to specific professions, the government must verify who is truly working outside their domicile. If all domestic voters are allowed to apply, the workload increases, but the fairness standard is simpler. The Executive Yuan's national referendum draft adopts the latter, and the Central Election Commission also believes that there should be no status conditions for referendum transfer voting.[^1][^7]

This path does not guarantee reform will succeed, but it replaces abstract fear with observable processes and political slogans with correctable versions. Democratic systems are rarely written perfectly in one go; what matters is that failures can be seen and repairs can be made before the next election.

## What Taiwan Must Choose Beyond Returning Home to Vote

The increased train schedules, family group chats asking when someone will arrive, schools and activity centers becoming democratic sites within a day—this is the familiar rhythm of returning home voting for Taiwanese people. This ritual has costs and carries the trust built from paper ballots, public counting, and neighborhood supervision.

Non-resident voting must find those left outside by the barrier to return home, and it must also ensure that after they come in, everyone still trusts that the ballot was not altered on the way. Directly moving existing rituals online cannot answer these two questions.

Taiwan has opened a narrow slit. The workplace voting for polling staff, the new system for Indigenous peoples' voting, and the draft referendum transfer voting are all measuring the same distance. Next, it must ask whether one vote can travel a few hundred kilometers less, and whether the system is willing to go through every segment that can be supervised for that vote.
