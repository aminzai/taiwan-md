On the eve of every major election, Taiwan's streets undergo a miraculous metamorphosis. Deafening loudspeakers, colorful campaign flags, and those "stage trucks" that can transform from ordinary vans into neon-lit stages within minutes collectively construct Taiwan's unparalleled "election rally culture" [^1]. This is not merely a political struggle for votes, but a "democratic carnival" integrating vernacular aesthetics, social psychology, and legal norms.

From a sociological perspective, Taiwan's election rally activities have long transcended simple policy announcements, evolving into a highly ritualized process of collective empathy [^7]. In these settings, voters often seek not cold policy data, but emotional resonance and a sense of group belonging [^15].

## Stage Trucks and Propaganda Vehicles: The "Transformers" of Vernacular Culture

If one were to select the most representative visual symbols of Taiwan's rally venues, "stage trucks" (Stage Trucks) and modified propaganda vehicles would undoubtedly top the list. These vehicles, which appear ordinary in daily life, can unfold into luxurious stages equipped with top-tier sound systems, LED screens, and dazzling lights within a short time after arriving at their destination, thanks to hydraulic systems and simple operations [^3].

Photographer Shen Zhao-liang (沈昭良) profoundly documented this unique Taiwanese mobile stage culture in his famous _STAGE_ series. He pointed out that the development of stage trucks originated from the frequent needs of Taiwanese folk temple fairs, deity worship, weddings, and funerals [^2]. Early performance stages were mostly temporary structures, time-consuming and labor-intensive. As times evolved, vendors sought to improve mobility and visual effects, converting trucks into electronic keyboard vehicles, eventually developing into the highly automated hydraulic stage trucks seen today.

This image records the dynamic landscape of Taiwan's street political mobilization, with large devices and sound equipment mounted on the vehicles, demonstrating a high degree of vernacular participation and mobility.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b6/908_Taiwan_Republic_Campaign_people_and_Fuso_FM_truck_20060318.jpg)

As shown in the image above, early political mobilization on Taiwan's streets was often accompanied by large modified trucks and long lines of parade crowds. This practice of temporarily transforming transportation vehicles into propaganda platforms broke the spatial limitations of traditional political rallies, allowing political appeals to penetrate directly into every alley and street. The design of these stage trucks and propaganda vehicles is filled with strong local Taiwanese colors; the bright color combinations and exaggerated lighting effects not only bring strong visual impact but also reflect the grassroots society's desire for "liveliness" and "grandeur" [^3].

## Democratic Snacks: Political Mobilization on the Tip of the Tongue

At Taiwan's rally sites, besides the political speeches on stage, the "democratic snacks" below the stage are equally indispensable soul elements. Among them, the most famous is undoubtedly the "democratic sausage" [^18]. This vendor culture, originating from street movements in the 1980s, saw vendors pushing charcoal-grilled sausage stalls following protest crowds, providing physical support for participants and becoming an unofficial symbol of the democratic movement [^18].

![Figure 2: A "democratic sausage" stall at a protest and rally site. White smoke rises from the charcoal grill, and vendors provide vernacular food to the masses amidst the noisy political gatherings. This "smell of charcoal grilling on the side of protest" has become a unique olfactory and visual memory in the history of Taiwan's democratic movement.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/c/c5/318_-_%E6%B0%91%E4%B8%BB%E9%A6%99%E8%85%B8.jpg)

As depicted in the image above, democratic sausage stalls are often adjacent to protest or rally crowds. The sizzling sausages on the charcoal grill and the drifting oil smoke are not only sources of physical sustenance but also carry the shared life experiences of Taiwan's civil society in street protests and election mobilization [^18]. Besides sausages, another food with high political symbolism is "election fried rice noodles" [^17]. In central and southern Taiwan, when candidates hold small-scale policy announcement meetings, they often prepare large buckets of fried rice noodles and meatball soup to treat fellow townsmen. This "rice noodle gathering" culture has been fragrant in Taiwan for over 30 years; it is not just to fill voters' stomachs but also a display of human warmth and a mobilization tool [^17].

## Election Propaganda Vehicles: Mobile Democratic Broadcasting Towers and Noise Anxiety

These modified vehicles are usually equipped with huge loudspeakers, repeatedly playing candidates' policies, promotional songs, or pleading slogans [^9]. Although this is seen as a necessary means to reach the grassroots, the noise it generates often sparks social controversy.

According to Article 54 of the _Act Governing the Election and Recall of Public Officials_, political parties and candidates using loudspeakers must not create noise; otherwise, they will be fined by environmental protection authorities under the _Noise Control Act_ [^10]. However, due to the high mobility of propaganda vehicles, law enforcement agencies often face technical difficulties in actual measurement and issuing fines [^11]. This tension between the "voice of democracy" and "peace of life" reflects the continuous negotiation of citizens' rights boundaries during Taiwan's democratic transition.

## The Economics of Election Merchandise: From Fans to "Wenqing" Design

Another major feature of Taiwan's elections is the dazzling array of "election merchandise." From the most practical tissues, ballpoint pens, and lighters of the past, to plastic fans essential during hot weather, these small items are not only promotional tools but also form a unique "election industry chain" [^12].

As the voter structure becomes younger, election merchandise has evolved from simple giveaways to "brand peripherals." Candidates begin to推出 "Wenqing" (literati-style/hipster) products with design sensibility, such as eco-bags, sports towels, and even figurines, attracting supporters to purchase them with their own money, transforming political identity into a display of lifestyle [^12]. Interestingly, this industry chain previously outsourced part of its production to China to reduce costs, but in recent years, due to geopolitical risks, many orders have returned to local Taiwanese manufacturers, forming another form of "rooting in Taiwan" [^12].

## "Dong Suan" and the Magic of Language: Emotional Catalysts on the Rally Stage

In rally settings, language is not only a tool for communication but also an emotional catalyst. Among them, the most well-known is undoubtedly the term "Dong Suan" (凍蒜). This term, derived from the homophone of "winning election" in Hokkien, has now become an indispensable slogan in Taiwan's elections [^5]. When the host leads tens of thousands of crowd members below the stage to shout "Dong Suan" with a hoarse voice, that overwhelming momentum often instantly pushes the atmosphere of the venue to the climax.

Besides simple and powerful slogans, the "election language" of political figures is also a profound art [^1]. In rally speeches, candidates often use a large amount of vernacular language, metaphors, and contrast techniques to close the distance with voters. This language strategy not only effectively激发 supporters' shared hatred against enemies but also creates memorable points easy to spread in an era of fragmented information [^1].

## The Boundary of the 30-Dollar Bento: The Tug-of-War Between Law and Human Relations

To rectify election conduct, Taiwan's judicial authorities have gradually developed a set of standards for determining bribery in practice. The most well-known is the so-called "30-dollar threshold" [^4]. This standard originates from a 2001 interpretation by the Ministry of Justice, stating that if candidates distribute simple promotional items worth less than 30 New Taiwan Dollars, it is generally considered insufficient to sway voters' intentions, thus not constituting bribery [^13].

However, in actual legal operations, "30 dollars" is not an absolute, un-crossable dead line. When determining whether bribery constitutes, courts still comprehensively consider the candidate's subjective intent, the nature of the items, and whether it is sufficient to influence voters' voting intentions [^4]. For example, providing simple fried rice noodles at a rally evening, although the cost may exceed 30 dollars, is usually viewed as a display of human relations of "treating guests" in Taiwanese society, rather than vote-buying behavior [^4].

## Social Capital and Political Participation: The Deep Functions of Rallies

From an academic perspective, rally evenings have the function of strengthening "social capital" [^16]. Through face-to-face collective participation, the trust and norms among voters are strengthened, and through the transmission of interpersonal networks, voting participation rates are further influenced [^15]. Research shows that Taiwan voters' voting participation is not only influenced by individual rational policy analysis but also swayed by the emotional mobilization of their social networks [^15].

With the advent of the digital age, the battlefield of elections has gradually shifted to online communities and meme dissemination [^14], but physical rally evenings still possess an irreplaceable "theater effect"; it is the most vivid and irreplaceable collective memory in Taiwan's democratic process.
