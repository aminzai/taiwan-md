# The 2026 Advanced Subjects Test Controversy: A Structural Crisis of Educational Integrity

> **30-Second Overview:** In July 2026, the Advanced Subjects Test was historically delayed to July 13 due to the impact of Super Typhoon Bavi. Following the release of results, as many as 149 exam appeals were filed. The College Entrance Examination Center (CEEC) ultimately ruled to uphold all original answers without granting any points, sparking harsh criticism from parent groups calling it a "black box agency." The core of the controversy lies in obvious flaws—such as duplicate options in Biology and incorrect charts in Geography—leading legislators and student groups to demand that the CEEC release "answering big data" to prove its claim that these errors "did not affect performance," alongside a nationwide petition for the full digitalization of the appeals process.

The 2026 Taiwan University Entrance Advanced Subjects Test (for the 115th academic year) was destined to be an exam fraught with turbulence. First, due to the impact of Super Typhoon Bavi, the Association of University Admissions Committees decided to postpone the exam—originally scheduled for July 11—to July 13 and 14. This marked the first time in the history of the Advanced Subjects Test that an exam was delayed due to a natural disaster[^1][^2]. However, the true storm began after the results were released on August 3. The College Entrance Examination Center (CEEC) received as many as **149 appeals** regarding questionable exam questions, yet ultimately ruled that "all original answers shall be maintained, with no points granted for any question"[^3][^4].

📝 **Curator's Note:** Taiwan's entrance examinations have always been viewed as the cornerstone of social equity. When the CEEC responds to obvious layout and chart errors with nothing more than "deep regret," while failing to provide quantitative data to prove that such errors "did not affect performance," this is no longer merely a technical error in item writing; it is a structural collapse of procedural justice.

## Core Controversial Questions and Official Rulings

The flaws in the exam questions that triggered this public outcry were primarily concentrated in Biology, Geography, and Civics & Society. Although the CEEC emphasized the rigor of its review process, its justifications failed to quell public anger[^5]:

- **Biology, Question 24**: The text for Option A and Option D in this multiple-choice question was identical[^6]. Examinees wasted significant time repeatedly comparing them in the exam hall. The CEEC determined that since both A and D were incorrect options, the duplication actually "reduced the difficulty," and thus no points would be granted[^7].
- **Geography, Question 35**: The question stem required observing a "line graph," but the charts in the options were all "histograms"[^8]. The CEEC argued that histograms can also present trends and that examinees could still select the correct answer (D), therefore maintaining the original ruling[^9].
- **Civics & Society, Question 2**: This question explored the rights of children with disabilities; external critics questioned whether both (A) and (C) were equally valid. The CEEC insisted that Option (A) better aligned with the curriculum spirit regarding the limitations on self-development within social norms[^10].

## Five-Tier Score Data and Elective Trends Across Subjects

According to data released by the CEEC on August 3, the total number of registrants for this session was approximately **39,213**, similar to previous years[^11]. Notably, the number of students choosing the "Math B + History/Geography/Civics" track (Humanities) and the "Math A + Chemistry/Physics/Biology" track (Science) were nearly equal, reflecting a stable structure in interdisciplinary subject selection[^12].

## Reform Demands: From "Emotional Appeals" to "Data Verification"

In late July 2026, Legislators Luo Ting-wei (羅廷瑋), Ye Yuan-zhi (葉元之), and parent representative Hsu Tai-ying (許泰英) held a press conference to propose specific institutional reforms[^15]. Unlike previous simple demands for "awarding points," this movement emphasizes **scientific evidence**:

1. **Public Disclosure of Answering Data**: Requiring the CEEC to appropriately disclose "answer distribution," "option selection ratios," "correct answer rates," and "discrimination indices" for controversial questions. If the correct answer rate among high-ability examinees is abnormally low on flawed questions, it would prove the presence of interference, rather than relying solely on the committee's subjective claim that it "did not affect performance"[^15][^16].
2. **Strengthening Cross-Verification**: Establishing an independent three-party verification mechanism involving item writing, review, and proofreading to prevent low-level errors like duplicate text[^15].
3. **Modernizing the Appeals Process**: Abolishing paper-based registered mail appeals in favor of full digitalization and face-to-face discussion mechanisms to increase review transparency[^16].

## The "Ti-Dian-Zi" Petition: The Obligation to Provide Reasoning

A petition initiated by a proposer known as "xin" on the "Participatory Public Policy Online Platform" (Case No. 4f22b2cc) is one of the few channels through which this controversy has entered formal institutional processes[^17]. The proposal contains three points: digitizing appeals, forming an external review committee composed of university professors and high school teachers unrelated to the item writers, and requiring that responses must cite specific curricula, textbooks, or academic literature rather than using abstract terms like "relatively appropriate"[^17].

The third point is the sharpest part of the proposal: it demands the **obligation to provide reasoning**. When the CEEC states that an error "did not affect performance," it must present verifiable evidence rather than just a conclusion. At the press conference held by legislators on July 31, the students, teachers, and parent representatives in attendance raised this exact same point[^18].

Currently, the CEEC's re-examination process is set to conclude on August 6; university preference registration will take place from August 5 to 8, with official results expected on August 17[^19]. This upheaval, triggered by 149 appeals, may serve as a critical turning point in pushing Taiwan's university entrance examination system toward an era of big-data-driven auditing.
