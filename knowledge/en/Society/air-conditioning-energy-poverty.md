> **30-Second Overview**: Energy poverty is not simply about "using too much electricity" or "not knowing how to save energy," but rather a situation where households lack sufficient resources to obtain safe, affordable, and efficient energy services. For families living in old apartments, additions on rooftops, poorly ventilated rooms, or using second-hand high-consumption air conditioners, turning on the A/C can create billing pressure, while not running it may increase the risk of heat injury. Taiwan currently lacks a nationally consistent definition of energy poverty, but private surveys have found that the proportion of disposable income spent on energy by vulnerable households is significantly high.[^1] When high temperatures are treated as an inter-departmental risk by the government, energy poverty can no longer be just about energy-saving campaigns; it must become a public issue addressed jointly by housing, social welfare, health, and climate adaptation.[^2]

# Energy Poverty Is Not About "Not Saving Enough"

Energy poverty is most easily misunderstood as households lacking conservation habits: turning off the A/C less, using hot water less, or switching off lights casually, leading to lower bills. However, this narrative ignores the range of choices available to the household. Living in a well-insulated home with sun protection on windows and high-efficiency air conditioning is not the same as living in a house exposed to west sun, leaks, poor ventilation, and old appliances; even if the indoor and outdoor temperatures are the same, the energy demand will not be identical.

Therefore, energy poverty encompasses at least three levels. First is **affordability**: when energy bills consume too large a proportion of income, forcing families to make trade-offs between food, medicine, transportation, and electricity. Second is **energy service quality**: even if a household can pay the bill, they might live in an overheated, overcooled, damp, or poorly ventilated space. Third is **access capacity**: renters, elderly living alone, people with disabilities, or families without initial capital may be unable to replace A/C units themselves, improve windows, or apply for complex subsidies.

A study by the Taiwan Youth Climate Alliance noted that a recognized definition of energy poverty has not yet been established in Taiwan, citing surveys conducted in collaboration with the Industrial Research Institute and Family Services, which indicated that vulnerable households might spend an average of 18% of their disposable income on energy. This figure should not be directly extrapolated to represent the entire population, but it serves as a reminder: judging whether a household is conserving energy solely by total electricity consumption may mistakenly label families who are "forced into high expenditure due to poor housing and equipment" as wasteful.[^1] Research from National Taiwan University also understands energy burden within the interactive relationship of housing conditions and vulnerable households, rather than attributing the problem solely to individual consumption choices.[^8]

# Why Summer Energy Poverty Is Particularly Dangerous

In the past, discussions on energy poverty often focused primarily on winter heating. However, Taiwan faces another seasonal risk: high summer temperatures. Vulnerable households may not "lack energy needs," but rather have needs they dare not meet. When indoor temperatures rise, families might choose to shorten A/C usage time, set the A/C temperature too high, or only allow children and the elderly into air-conditioned rooms. These are coping mechanisms under billing pressure, not merely consumption preferences.

The National Health Administration explicitly warns that heat injury can occur indoors when indoor air is stagnant and thermal energy cannot be dissipated. The body temperature regulation capabilities of the elderly and children are weaker, requiring special attention to high indoor temperatures. Official recommendations include regularly drinking water, maintaining ventilation, and setting A/C temperatures between 26°C and 27°C.[^3] These suggestions are easier for households who have air conditioning, can ventilate normally, and can afford the electricity; however, for those living on rooftops, under corrugated iron roofs, or with windows that cannot be opened, building and equipment investment is required to truly implement them.

High-temperature risk is also cumulative. Houses absorb heat during the day, and if they cannot dissipate it at night, the elderly may remain exposed to high temperatures during sleep. Clogged A/C filters or poor heat dissipation from outdoor units require more electricity for the same cooling effect. This means "thermal safety" cannot be measured by the temperature at a single moment but must consider how the housing absorbs, stores, and dissipates heat throughout the entire day.

# How an Old House Turns High Temperatures into High Bills

The energy dilemma of old residences is usually not caused by a single piece of equipment. Insufficient insulation in walls and roofs increases the cooling load. Windows without sun protection allow west-sun heat to enter directly indoors. Older A/C units are less efficient, and even after starting up, they may not stabilize the temperature. Long-term lack of cleaning of filters and outdoor units also increases energy consumption and failure risk. If a family lacks initial capital, they might only be able to continuously pay high operating costs without being able to improve the root cause of those costs.

Energy poverty is thus linked to housing rights, tenancy rights, and building governance. Homeowners can decide whether to replace windows, add sunshades, or improve exterior walls. Renters, however, may worry that renovation costs cannot be recouped, or they may lack the authority to perform construction. If subsidies are only given to equipment purchasers, families who most need improvements but have no housing decision-making power might be excluded.

Yilan County previously promoted subsidies for energy-saving home insulation renovation, including sunshades, insulating glass, roof, and exterior wall insulation in the scope of support, reflecting that local governments have begun to view the building envelope as part of energy saving and cooling. This program was a past year's plan and cannot be directly seen as current policy across Taiwan, but it provides an important direction: improving thermal comfort does not necessarily start with buying a larger A/C; it can also start by reducing heat absorption by the house.[^4]

# Why Subsidies Do Not Always Equal Accessibility

Both central and local governments have offered subsidies for appliance replacement, residential energy saving, or insulation. Policy design usually aims to exchange limited funds for energy savings, carbon reduction, and reduced household expenditure, but there remains a gap between "qualifying" and "being able to complete the application."

Taking the housing appliance subsidy application page from the Ministry of Economic Affairs' Energy Agency as an example, applicants must prepare identification documents, bankbooks, utility bills or electricity consumption proof, invoices, warranty cards, and waste disposal slips. Subsidies are also limited by annual budgets and review criteria.[^5] For those with stable bank accounts who can pay for new appliances first and keep complete documentation, this is a manageable process; but for those without initial capital, where the appliance belongs to the landlord, or who rely on social workers and relatives for assistance, it creates a practical barrier.

The Taipei City 2026 Residential Community Energy Storage and Energy Saving Subsidy provided a relatively complete local case: in addition to community energy generation, storage, and glass insulation film, subsidies were also given for first-grade efficient refrigerators and A/C replacement for low-income households listed in the registry. The announcement set upper limits for refrigerator and A/C subsidies, indicating that local governments are beginning to include the efficiency of vulnerable household equipment in net-zero and just transition.[^6] However, these types of programs still need to address three issues: whether subsidies cover renters, whether they can afford installation and removal costs, and when the next support will come after a plan is fully funded or expires.

Taoyuan City's cooling energy-saving subsidy for high-temperature sensitive groups targets children aged 0 to 6 years and seniors over 65, subsidizing A/C cleaning, glass insulation film, and old A/C replacement. The significance of such policies is that they not only identify needs based on low-income status but also attempt to find families who might be overlooked using age and heat sensitivity.[^7] However, to avoid welfare discontinuity, age, income, housing conditions, and health risks must still be reasonably linked between social welfare and health systems, rather than requiring households to repeatedly prove they deserve help.

# Moving from Energy Saving Campaigns to Residential Heat Safety

The core message of traditional energy-saving campaigns is "use less." An energy poverty policy, however, must add another question: "How can one achieve sufficient safety and comfort with less energy?" These are not contradictory; the tools are just different.

| Policy Level   | Traditional Energy Saving Approach              | Residential Heat Safety Approach                                                                         |
| :------------- | :---------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Equipment      | Encouraging lights off, raising A/C temperature | Replacing high-consumption A/C, cleaning filters, improving outdoor unit heat dissipation                |
| Building       | Promoting sun protection and ventilation        | Subsidizing window shading, insulation film, roof and exterior wall insulation                           |
| Social Welfare | Providing subsidies based on low-income status  | Social workers, health centers, and communities proactively identifying high-temperature risk households |
| Information    | Providing energy-saving knowledge               | Providing simplified applications, home energy diagnosis, and usage improvement suggestions              |
| Evaluation     | Calculating how many kWh were saved             | Simultaneously measuring heat injury, indoor temperature, billing burden, and quality of life            |

The first shift is **from post-hoc subsidies to front-end diagnosis**. Social workers, neighborhood offices, in-home care providers, or health center staff can record during a visit whether the house is on a rooftop, exposed to west sun, poorly ventilated, has A/C malfunctions, or uses old appliances. This data should not be used for labeling but for scheduling energy diagnosis and improvement sequences.

The second shift is **from single-household equipment to residential and community scale**. One high-efficiency A/C can reduce electricity use, but it cannot solve the problem of heat absorption by an entire building's roof, stuffiness in common areas, or insufficient power capacity. If a community can collectively improve shading, roofs, elevators, and common area electricity, it may be more efficient than requiring each household to invest individually.

The third shift is **incorporating high temperatures into disaster prevention and health governance**. In 2025, the Ministry of Environment first piloted heat adaptation countermeasure drills, using a scenario where both northern and southern Taiwan exceeded 38°C for three consecutive days and issued red-flag warnings, requiring central ministries and local governments to jointly handle care for key groups, livelihood energy, and community adaptation.[^2] This drill also pointed out that high temperatures were not yet fully included in the scope of natural disasters under the Disaster Prevention Act, meaning the system still needs clarification: when to activate, who contacts elderly individuals living alone, which places become cooling centers, and how these services are maintained during a power outage.

# Energy Transition Cannot Pass Costs On to the Most Vulnerable

Energy transition is often measured by carbon reduction, stable power supply, and industrial competitiveness. However, if low-income families cannot maintain basic thermal safety due to electricity prices, equipment, or housing conditions, the transition can create new inequalities. This is not an opposition to energy saving or improving energy efficiency; it demands that policies simultaneously answer "who gets improved first" and "who bears the cost of transition."

The discussion on energy poverty compiled by the Taiwan Youth Climate Alliance has reminded policymakers that they must combine objective and subjective indicators, not just look at electricity bill ratios or total consumption. Housing conditions, equipment efficiency, demographic structure, thermal comfort, and well-being must also be included.[^1] A special report from public television placed the energy burden within the actual life context of vulnerable households, explaining that if energy transition lacks a distribution design, those who already bear high housing and equipment costs may face new burdens.[^9] This approach is particularly important for Taiwan because one family might use more electricity due to fear of heat, or they might use abnormally low amounts due to inability to pay the bill; neither can be judged solely by the bill.

The high-temperature drills and energy-saving subsidies from local governments provide a space for policy experimentation. The central government needs to establish common minimum standards, including indicators for vulnerable household thermal safety, barrier-free design for subsidy applications, allocation of responsibilities between renters and landlords, and privacy protection for energy diagnosis data. If each county launches short-term programs independently, families may receive completely different levels of protection depending on their household registration, application timing, and information literacy.

# A Viable Taiwanese Solution

Taiwan does not need to establish a complex, single energy poverty score from the outset; it can start with actionable service processes. Previously, the central government supported local energy governance by promoting residential and commercial energy-saving plans across counties, providing an institutional entry point to connect energy diagnosis, social welfare referral, and housing improvement within existing administrative networks.[^10] The first step is for local governments to establish "Residential Heat Safety Referral": when social workers, health centers, fire departments, in-home care providers, or community organizations detect high-temperature risk in a household, they should refer them for an energy diagnosis, rather than just providing one-time utility bill subsidies.

The second step is to divide improvement measures into three cost tiers. Low-cost items include cleaning A/C units, adjusting shading, improving ventilation, and providing thermometers. Medium-cost items include insulation film, curtains, outdoor unit heat dissipation, and energy-saving appliances. High-cost items include roof, exterior wall, window, and community public space renovation. This prevents all families from being required to purchase expensive equipment and allows limited budgets to first address the most dangerous housing conditions.

The third step is to expand outcomes beyond "how much electricity was saved" to "whether risk was reduced." The government can track hours of high indoor temperature, heat injury hospitalizations, household energy expenditure burden, appliance failure rates, and residents' subjective thermal comfort, rather than judging project success solely by total energy savings. If energy consumption decreases but heat injuries increase, it cannot be called a success; if a family's electricity use slightly increases but they gain safe cooling due to high-efficiency equipment, it should not be simplified as a failure.

This system also requires a minimum dataset usable by local workers. Households should not be forced to disclose all consumption details, but at least the floor level, roof type, main heat source, age of A/C and refrigerator, indoor ventilation status, whether there are heat-sensitive individuals in the household, and whether energy expenditure in the last quarter caused life trade-offs, should be recorded after obtaining consent. The data should be used for service improvement, stored stratified with social welfare case files, and households must know who will use which data. If data is only used to determine "whether they deserve a subsidy," it increases stigma; if it can be used to schedule cleaning, repair, insulation, and referral, then energy policy can become preventive public service.

Local governments can also establish a "Heat Safety Service Package": before heatwave warnings, community hubs, in-home care providers, and health centers contact high-risk households. During the warning period, they provide walkable cooling locations, drinking water, charging stations, and emergency contacts. After the warning, they check for new problems with A/C units, fans, windows, and utility bills. This service should not only be activated after a disaster or emergency but should become routine community care in summer. For remote areas and islands, the service package must also include repair personnel, parts transportation, and power outage backup; otherwise, even good subsidies may be useless when equipment fails.

# Conclusion: Making Safe Cooling a Basic Life Capability

The core of energy poverty is not demanding more willpower from vulnerable households, but acknowledging that there are inequalities in housing conditions and energy services themselves. When old houses absorb heat, high-consumption appliances, initial purchase costs, subsidy application barriers, and extreme temperatures intersect, an electricity bill simultaneously reflects income, housing, health, and climate risk.

Taiwan has begun to accumulate policy tools such as local insulation subsidies, appliance replacement programs, heatwave drills, and community energy saving initiatives. The next step is not to add another phrase like "please save energy," but to weave these tools into a human-centered residential heat safety system: proactively finding at-risk families, first improving the most energy-intensive housing conditions, ensuring that subsidies can be used by renters and those who cannot pay upfront, and using health and quality of life to test whether the energy transition is fair.

Only when a family can safely use air conditioning in high temperatures without having to choose between health and bills does energy policy truly move from "supply management" toward public welfare.

Policy evaluation must also include the voices of residents. Local governments can ask before and after services whether it is easier to cool down indoors, whether they have reduced A/C usage time due to electricity costs, whether equipment is more reliable, and whether residents know where to seek help during the next heat warning. This feedback should not replace energy expenditure and health data but should be presented alongside quantitative indicators so that the government knows whether the plan truly improved life or merely completed procurement and auditing. If a subsidy provides a family with equipment but does not solve rental restrictions, repair responsibilities, or subsequent utility costs, the policy needs redesign, not blaming the residents for non-compliance, and continuous public review and correction must occur.
