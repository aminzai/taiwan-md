> 30-second overview: In 2025, Taiwan's Ministry of the Interior announced that the country already has more than 82,000 air-raid shelters, with a total capacity of about 54 million people. In 2022, Reuters visited underground shopping malls and parking lots in Taipei and saw how a city temporarily rewrites its daily spaces into evacuation routes. Eighteen-year-old Harmony Wu practices dancing near the MRT and, for the first time, realizes that the underground street beneath her feet might become a shelter in wartime. The numbers bring comfort, but they don't answer the most bodily question: When the alarm sounds, where do I go?

# From Air-Raid Trenches to Basements: A Historical Path

Taiwan's current air-raid shelter system cannot be understood starting from a smartphone app or an underground parking lot. Its predecessor was the air-raid organization and dugouts established during the Japanese colonial period for wartime mobilization. The Taiwan Archives indicate that Taiwan, as a base for Japan's southward advance, became an important target of Allied air raids during World War II. The Taiwan Governor-General issued the "Regulations on the Construction of Simple Air-Raid Shelters" on July 13, 1941, setting standards for the form, area, location, materials, capacity, and evacuation procedures of air-raid shelters. [^12]

This history shows that air-raid evacuation has never been just "digging a hole." It simultaneously involves building codes, population allocation, alarms, evacuation, supplies, and local organization. Wartime evacuation spaces are not entirely provided by the state alone, but are jointly constructed by agencies, schools, factories, civic groups, residents, and local organizations, forming a governance model that delegates wartime responsibility to everyday society. [^12]

The air raids at the end of World War II later became the starting point where memories of different generations overlap. The curatorial data of the National Culture Memory Bank indicates that after the war, Taiwan had two kinds of air-raid memories: the memory of local residents experiencing Allied bombing in the 1940s, and the resistance-war memory centered on Japanese bombings of Chinese cities, brought by the government's relocation to Taiwan after 1949. Both speak of air raids, but they have different understandings of the enemy, victims, and national narrative. [^13]

Therefore, air-raid shelters are not just concrete and earthwork—they are also spaces of memory politics. When elders say "running from air raids," they may be referring to taking shelter in dugouts during alarms in the Japanese colonial period, or participating in air-raid drills and civil defense training under post-1949 anti-communist propaganda. If air-raid shelters are seen merely as old buildings, one misses how they carry Taiwan's transformation from colonial rule, post-war reception, Cold War mobilization, to a democratized society.

After the government relocated to Taiwan in 1949, the military standoff across the Taiwan Strait meant that the air-raid defense system did not disappear with the end of the war. The Taiwan Archives record that in 1950, the Ministry of National Defense's Air Defense Command held Taiwan Province's first air defense meeting and promoted the construction, numbering, capacity registration, evacuation certificates, and tunnel management of air-raid shelters according to the "Measures for the Construction of Air-Raid Shelters in Cities and Counties of Taiwan Province." In 1955, the government even advocated "one pit per person," designing simple air-raid shelters of different materials and capacities; in 1958, in response to the imagination of nuclear weapons, simple atomic defense pits and atomic defense rooms appeared. [^12]

These measures reflect two characteristics of Cold War civil defense. First, evacuation facilities must be widely dispersed and cannot rely solely on a few large public buildings. Second, the government incorporates residents' homes, courtyards, roadside vacant land, and local communities into the defense network. Air-raid evacuation is therefore not only the military's business, but also a daily task shared by families, factories, schools, villages, and cities.

Post-war air-raid education also entered society through "Air-Raid Defense Days" and propaganda activities. The Ministry of Culture's curatorial data records that as early as 1940, someone proposed establishing an Air-Raid Defense Day; after 1949, the government strengthened air-raid education in Taiwan. From 1951 onwards, the forms of propaganda included lectures, essay competitions, exhibitions, propaganda vehicles, and air-dropped leaflets. The Wan'an exercises implemented from 1978 fixed joint military-civilian alarms, personnel and vehicle evacuation, and traffic control as visible systems for all citizens. [^13]

However, Air-Raid Defense Days did not always carry the same social weight. The Ministry of Culture's data indicates that from the 1970s onwards, as international and cross-strait situations changed, Air-Raid Defense Days and related activities gradually shrank; the Wan'an exercises were still retained, but their meaning slowly shifted from everyday wartime education for all citizens to a fixed annual urban control event. [^13] This transition explains today's gap: the systems and signs are still there, but many residents have lost the understanding of their history and how to use them.

A 2007 study by the Journal of National Defense on nationwide civil defense also shows that the concept of civil defense has gradually shifted from passive "civil air defense" to "civil defense," "air-raid protection," and "wartime support for military duties." The study treats information dissemination, alarm distribution, air-raid evacuation and sheltering, air-raid disaster protection, local security, disaster relief, and unit training as one system. [^14] This shift means that modern air-raid evacuation cannot only pursue the quantity of underground space, but must also consider how alarms, rescue, communications, supplies, medical care, and local governance are connected.

# Capacity on the Map, Entrances Underfoot

In August 2022, Reuters recorded more than 4,600 air-raid shelters in Taipei, with a nominal capacity of about 12 million people; most of them are not dedicated fortresses, but underground parking lots, the MRT system, and underground shopping malls. Eighteen-year-old Harmony Wu was practicing dance in an underground street and realized that this familiar daily space would change its purpose when war breaks out. [^1]

In April 2025, the National Police Agency said it had completed the networked construction of more than 82,000 air-raid shelters across the country, with a total capacity of about 54 million people. Citizens can use the Police Service App to enable positioning, select locations, and navigate, or take screenshots or save offline maps in advance in case of network interruption. [^2]

Because the map answers "where is there a point," not "can I reach that point after the alarm." A shelter marked on the third basement level is not the same distance for someone familiar with elevators, stairs, access control, and exits, as it is for someone pushing a stroller, using a wheelchair, carrying young children, or unable to hear the alarm.

# One System, Turning Basements into Public Responsibility

Taiwan's air-raid shelter system is not temporarily pieced together. The purpose of the National Police Agency's design of the air-raid shelter app is to allow citizens to become familiar with nearby locations in peacetime and find places to go in wartime; this tool turns shelters from administrative data into routes that residents can confirm in advance. [^3]

What the system really needs to handle is whether the number of facilities, their distribution, capacity, and evacuation directions can be grasped. A summary of the current system by Public Radio Taiwan (PTS) indicates that since 1974, designated buildings in densely populated areas or with high air-raid risk have been required to be equipped with air-raid shelters; after completion, the data must be submitted to the police authorities for filing. [^15] The Legislative Yuan's Legal Affairs Office summarized the current legal framework, indicating that air-raid shelters are subject to both civil defense and building management regulations; the police authorities are responsible for filing, while the owners and users of buildings are responsible for maintaining legitimate use and equipment safety. [^4]

But between "filing" and "usable," there is a management problem that is easily overlooked in peacetime. Many facilities are located in the basement of a building and cannot be entered without the owner's consent in peacetime; they are only opened for evacuation during air-raid drills, war, or control orders. [^4]

# Is There a Second Route Within Three Minutes?

In June 2025, Reuters reported that Taiwan is preparing to update its air-raid guidance, partly because high-rise residents in metropolitan areas may not be able to reach designated shelters within three minutes. The new guidance includes scenarios where people cannot enter shelters in time or are driving, and reminds people to find at least two walls for cover, crouch down, and keep their mouths slightly open when they cannot evacuate immediately. [^5]

Of course, shelters are important, but the city will not automatically empty when the alarm sounds; people may be on a bus, in an elevator, in a kitchen on the 17th floor, in an old apartment without a basement, or walking across a street with unclear signs. Reuters also pointed out in 2022 that at that time, the law required shelters to be kept clean and open, but did not require pre-storing food and water. [^1]

The Ministry of the Interior's 2026 revision of the building technical rules will raise the space calculation standard for some areas from 0.75 square meters per person to 1 square meter, and add requirements for smoke blocking, ventilation, lighting, restrooms, and emergency power outlets. [^6]

# Walking Back from Shelters to Communities

An analysis by the East Asia Forum believes that Taiwan's civil defense has increased shelters and volunteers in recent years, but is still affected by unclear missions, insufficient training, policy supervision, and funding limitations. If a crisis requires evacuation, maintaining order, medical care, and disaster response, it is not easy to accomplish with a top-down command alone. [^7]

Therefore, air-raid evacuation should not only be kept by the police and building management departments. The Ministry of the Interior's announced community-based disaster prevention initiative has expanded the training of disaster prevention specialists to include foreign workers, religious groups, apartment complex committees, taxi drivers, and village/community workers. [^8]

# Exercises Test Not Obedience, but Friction

The 2026 Urban Resilience (Air-Raid) Exercise adopted a warning-based, zoned, and time-staggered approach, arranging drills for alarm transmission, evacuation and sheltering, traffic control, disaster rescue, and communication network disruption. [^9] This pushes air-raid evacuation from "where do people go" to "how do the government and residents still know the next step when the network is unstable."

The value of network-disruption drills lies in forcing the city to acknowledge that the app is not the only public infrastructure. Paper signs, radio broadcasts, neighborhood notifications, community management rooms, and on-site guidance must take over when the phone cannot locate.

# Accessibility Is Not an Afterthought

The Ministry of the Interior's interpretation of air-raid shelters and accessible pathways points out that the current building code has clear provisions on which spaces must be accessible via accessible pathways; at the same time, based on aging and universal design, it is recommended that accessible elevators reach all floors and consider appropriate indoor pathways. [^11]

The scope of access stipulated by law and whether a person can safely reach the shelter space in wartime are not necessarily the same. Shelter planning still needs to include slope, door sill, elevator power failure, alternative routes, companions, and restrooms in on-site inspections; otherwise, "having a shelter" may still be an unreachable point on the map for people with disabilities.

On days when the alarm does not sound, the city can still do one small thing first: walk the nearest evacuation route with family, identify the yellow sign, remember the alternative directions without relying on the network, and ask the manager if the basement is really usable. The real evacuation map should include these people, not just the coordinates.
