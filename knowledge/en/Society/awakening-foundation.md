> **30-Second Overview:** In 1982, while Taiwan was under martial law, Li Yuan-ching, Cao Ai-lan, and Cheng Chi-hui founded the monthly magazine _Furen Xin Zhi_. They did not write feminism as slogans; instead, they started with female bodies in advertisements, workplace sexual harassment, the childcare dilemmas of housewives, and legal columns, turning "what happens in my home" into a public issue worthy of discussion. After restructuring into a foundation in 1987, this path that began on paper moved to the streets, the Legislative Yuan, and schools; but it also exposed internal divisions within the women's movement regarding class, sex work, and reform strategies during the 1997 sex worker incident.

## Starting with a Magazine That Wasn't Political Literature

In 1982, "Furen Xin Zhi Society" was founded by Li Yuan-ching, Cao Ai-lan, Cheng Chi-hui, Liu Yu-xiu, and You Mei-nu, publishing the _Furen Xin Zhi_ monthly. The National Taiwan Museum of History positions it as the first magazine in Taiwan to consciously promote gender equality from a feminist perspective. The foundation's own history states that this organization was established initially as a magazine society during Martial Law before being reorganized into a foundation after the lifting of martial law in 1987.[^1] [^2]

This point in time is significant. After the Kaohsiung Sunflower Incident in 1979, the political atmosphere remained tense. It was not easy to openly organize an opposition movement, yet women's issues were often packaged as "family" or "individual" matters and excluded from politics. _Xin Zhi_'s strategy was not to pretend it wasn't political, but rather to address the most undeniable daily realities: how newspapers portrayed women, how advertisements commodified female bodies, what working women encountered in the workplace, and why housewives lacked childcare support.[^1]

📝 Curator's Note: **Its earliest breakthrough was not taking women out of the home, but making people see that the family is inherently connected to law, labor, and the state.**

_Xin Zhi_ was not a one-way publication of editorials. It featured legal columns and reader submissions, allowing readers to submit their experiences to the magazine, which editors then organized into discussable issues from scattered experiences. The magazine also extended paper discussions through lectures, symposiums, and book clubs, and had sent questionnaires regarding sexual harassment, submitted opinions on the legalization of abortion to the Legislative Yuan, and sent public letters to entrepreneurs. The significance of these activities was not merely "propagandizing an ideology," but transforming individual, previously unnamed experiences into material that could be documented and form a claim.[^1]

## Deconstructing "Gender Equality" from Abstract Slogans into Life Issues

In its early days, _Xin Zhi_ faced two misunderstandings. One held that since the constitution already stipulated legal equality between genders, women had no further claims to make. Another imagined women's struggle as wives competing with husbands for household dominance. Data compiled by the National Taiwan Museum of History indicates that in the five years before _Xin Zhi_'s founding, its influence was limited, and it was even criticized as a complaint from middle-class women. These reactions instead demonstrated that between legal "equality" and everyday equality lay an entire set of unspoken divisions.[^2]

Therefore, _Xin Zhi_ broke down abstract gender inequality into scenarios recognizable by readers: Can a working woman refuse sexual harassment in the workplace? Why is the care work of housewives considered non-productive? Are unmarried women allowed to have abortions? Do school textbooks direct boys and girls toward different life paths? A master's thesis studying the _Furen Xin Zhi_ magazine also views it as a cross-section of women's rights development in Taiwan from the 1980s to the 1990s, pointing out that the magazine served as a medium for female self-awareness, social movements, and legal reform.[^3]

This was a pragmatic feminism. It did not first demand that every reader accept the same theory; rather, it first made readers realize: "It turns out I am not the only one who experiences this." Only when a person's shame becomes an experience that a group can discuss does the issue have a chance to leave the private room and enter the organization and system.

## After Lifting Martial Law, Paper Began Sprouting Streets

On January 10, 1987, _Xin Zhi_, in collaboration with the Rainbow Project, the Taiwan Human Rights Association, and indigenous groups, initiated the Huaxi Street march protesting human trafficking and caring for young prostitutes. In November of the same year after martial law was lifted, the magazine society reorganized into the Furen Xin Zhi Foundation. On January 10 of the following year, _Xin Zhi_, Rainbow, and other groups again organized a rescue march for young prostitutes. This marked the transition from "conducting women's work in the name of a magazine" to public street action, forcing the media to address issues raised by women's organizations head-on.[^2]

The reorganization was not just changing an organizational name. Official records state that _Xin Zhi_ began training female legal volunteers starting in 1994, providing telephone consultations for marriage and family law, serving over two thousand people annually. It gradually moved from publishing and education to legislative advocacy, policy monitoring, and public service.[^4]

English researcher Elizabeth Frost compared Furen Xin Zhi with the Housewives Alliance: the former often took a more policy-advocacy and cross-party lobbying approach, while the latter tended to promote environmental action from community and family life. This comparison reminds us that there is not just one "correct" model for women's movements. Institutional lobbying and daily mobilization are two different but mutually supportive political techniques.[^5]

After martial law was lifted, _Furen Xin Zhi_'s issues did not stop at heterosexual marriage and motherhood. The National Taiwan Museum of History records that the magazine subsequently addressed lesbian women's movements, sexual self-determination, and decriminalization of sex work. The foundation's current issues include partner rights, marital equality, bodily autonomy, and sex work. These issues do not need to be stitched into an unbroken line, but it is clear that the boundaries of "gender equality" are continuously being questioned.[^1] [^4]

Images from the 2005 Taiwan Pride parade provide a concrete snapshot of the Furen Xin Zhi Foundation's presence in public space. The photos cannot prove that any law was solely promoted by one organization, nor can they be traced back to the founding scene in 1982. What they can prove is that after entering the 21st century, _Furen Xin Zhi_ continued to bring gender and sexual orientation issues to the streets, allowing the organization's logo and claims to be seen.[^10]

## Domestic Violence Law: When Law First Entered the Home

The 1993 case of Deng Ru-wen killing her husband brought domestic violence, which had long been treated as a household matter, into national public discussion. The National Taiwan Museum of History's compilation notes that Deng Ru-wen suffered violence and sexual abuse during her youth, and she continued to be abused by her family after marriage. The case also exposed the systemic dilemma of police being unwilling to intervene in "household matters." The Domestic Violence Prevention Act, passed on June 24, 1998, brought public power and resources such as protective orders, eviction orders, and restraining orders into the home through mechanisms like these.[^6]

This law is often remembered for being the "first domestic violence law in Asia." However, the museum's article deliberately reminds us that this first place was not just a glory; it also represented victims who were long unprotected. While the law brought domestic violence from the private sphere into the public domain, it could not solve issues of economic dependence, caregiving responsibility, child safety, and gender power with a single piece of legislation. This is where _Furen Xin Zhi_ was important: it promoted systems but did not treat the system as the end of the story.[^6]

## Gender Equality Education: From Textbook Review to Legal Source

Another path for _Xin Zhi_ was bringing gender equality into schools. The National Conference on Public Education in 1988 began reviewing gender discrimination in elementary and middle school textbooks. In 1995, _Furen Xin Zhi_ proposed to the Executive Yuan's Educational Reform Review Committee, requesting research on how to implement gender equality education. These efforts demonstrated that education was not an accessory to "promotion," but a system determining how the next generation understood bodies, work, family, and power.[^7]

In May 2004, the Furen Xin Zhi Foundation jointly established a civil advocacy alliance with groups focused on gender equality education, women's rights, women's organizations, and LGBTQ+ groups to engage in lobbying. On June 4 of that year, the Gender Equality Education Act passed its third reading in the Legislative Yuan and was promulgated on June 23. The first article of the Ministry of Education's current regulations defines the purpose of the law as promoting substantive gender equality, eliminating gender discrimination, maintaining human dignity, and establishing educational resources and environments for gender equality.[^7] [^8]

There is an easily overlooked shift here: _Xin Zhi_ did not just demand that schools "not discriminate," but demanded that schools establish resources, curricula, complaint mechanisms, and investigation processes. In other words, gender equality was no longer just the personal attitude of a teacher, but a responsibility that the education system had to undertake.

## From Statutes to Daily Life: There is Still a Road Ahead

Writing laws into the national system does not mean the problems immediately disappear.

The text of the Gender Equality Education Act must be translated into school curricula, complaint procedures, and teacher training before it can affect students.

The original regulations from the Ministry of Education define "gender equality education" as respecting different gender differences, eliminating gender discrimination, and promoting substantive equality; these terms are therefore not just slogans but public responsibilities that schools must handle.[^8]

_Xin Zhi_'s early legal columns dealt precisely with the distance between statutes and daily life.

A reader might not know how family law is written, but she knows she has no say in her marriage; she may not be familiar with labor law, but she knows when asked about her plans for marriage and children during a job interview.

Writing down these experiences is equivalent to leaving evidence of private suffering that others can understand.

Reader submissions also made the magazine more than just a platform for scholars; it became a place where experiences met each other.

When women from different cities discovered they faced similar problems, the issue was no longer an accident in one family.

It began to have a name and subjects whom the government and employers could be held accountable to.

This act of naming could later be captured by legislation, policy, and public budgets.

A similar gap also exists in domestic violence prevention. Protective orders can restrict abusers from approaching, but they cannot find housing, income, childcare, or social networks for victims. The museum's historical records list medical care, law, child guidance, and employment guidance side-by-side because domestic violence has never been a single criminal case, but rather a chain of dilemmas after life has become entangled.[^6]

_Furen Xin Zhi_ later institutionalized the legal consultation volunteer system, in some ways addressing this gap: statutes needed to be translated into the next step an individual could take today. For readers, the consultation hotline was not an appendix to administrative services but the entrance where law truly reached life.[^4]

This also explains why the publication remained important. There are news reports on the day a bill passes, but how readers understand new laws, how they know if they can file a complaint, and how they articulate their cases usually requires slower text and repeated education. The historical database preserves records of magazines, quarterly issues, commentary, and activities—precisely this work that does not appear in the title of a law.[^9]

## The Cost of Institutionalization: Who is Represented, Who is Left Behind

Entering the system brought results, but it also brought new problems. Starting in 1996, _Furen Xin Zhi_ was revised to _Furen Xin Zhi Communications_, reporting more closely on the foundation's work promoting family law, gender equality in employment, and amendments to the domestic violence prevention act. However, the 1997 sex worker incident also brought internal divisions within the women's movement regarding sex work, class, and reform strategies to the surface. The National Taiwan Museum of History described this dispute as a conflict between feminist factions and sexual rights factions, not an episode that could be glossed over with the four words "women's movement success."[^1] [^2]

English research also pointed out that _Furen Xin Zhi_ was sometimes criticized as an organization of "elite maintenance without large-scale membership," which later attempted to expand grassroots support through local associations and training starting in 1994. This criticism is worth retaining because while legal expertise, academic networks, and Legislative Yuan lobbying can rapidly change systems, they cannot necessarily allow women from all classes, ethnicities, and sexual positions to speak within the organization.[^5]

This is not meant to discount the achievements of _Furen Xin Zhi_, but to place it back into the real social movement: once a movement succeeds, it faces the question of "whose experiences become policy language." The database primarily relies on _Furen Xin Zhi_ and _Sodong_ (Uproar) preserved by the Furen Xin Zhi Foundation to complete this history—the reports, commentaries, special features, reader voices, and movement records that show how many times there was speech, organization, and failure before the law.

📝 Curator's Note: **A truly mature history of a movement remembers not only who passed the bill, but also whose experiences were not truly covered by that bill.**

## The Baton Passed by One Magazine

Today, the Furen Xin Zhi Foundation continues to work on issues such as caregiving, family and marriage, labor rights, bodily autonomy, education culture, and political oversight, providing legal consultation for marriage and family and volunteer training. Its list of tasks seems longer than in 1982, but the core method has not disappeared: starting from a detail of life that feels wrong, finding the underlying system, and putting the system back into human life scrutiny.[^4]

Therefore, the importance of _Furen Xin Zhi_ is not just about which laws it promoted or that it was an early Taiwanese feminist magazine. It demonstrated a way to invent space within constraints: first establishing language through publications, then building organizations with language; first allowing individual experiences to be heard, and then having the law acknowledge that it was not personal fault.

But this path has no clean endpoint. From magazine to foundation, from reader submissions to legislative lobbying, _Furen Xin Zhi_ proved that systems can be changed; from the sex worker incident to criticisms of grassroots mobilization, it also reminded later generations that those who change systems must continuously ask: whose experiences are still not at the table?

This is the baton that the magazine left for Taiwan. It was not a publication that completed its mission, but a methodology of organizing silence into questions and turning questions into action.
