> **30-Second Overview:** On the streets of Taiwan, the neon lights of betel nut stalls and signs for "iced water" (_jiebingshui_) form the first impression for many visiting this land. However, this seemingly ordinary green fruit is more than just a stimulant; it was once a sacred offering and token of affection in Indigenous cultures, and a "miasma-cleansing pill" for early Han settlers. Today, betel nut has become a focal point of social controversy due to its health risks and environmental impact, weaving a complex narrative of class, land, and culture.

### Betel Nut: From Sacred Fruit to Carcinogen—A Microcosm of Class and Land within a Single Seed

In Taiwan, many long-distance drivers stop at neon-lit betel nut stalls by the roadside on hot afternoons to buy a pack of betel nut and two bottles of "iced water." This seemingly routine transaction represents a unique feature of Taiwan's social landscape, tracing the complex journey of a fruit that has spanned centuries—from sacred offering to carcinogen. In 1976, the "Shuangdong Betel Nut Stall" in Caotun, Nantou, pioneered the era of the "Betel Nut Beauties" (_binlang xishi_), forever intertwining the story of this green fruit with Taiwan's working class, cultural shifts, and issues of health and the environment.

#### A Cultural Capsule: Betel Nut and Taiwan's Indigenous Peoples

The history of chewing betel nut dates back five thousand years, and its connection to Taiwan spans over four hundred years. In the cultures of Taiwan's Indigenous peoples, betel nut is not merely a stimulant; it carries profound social and spiritual significance. Yang Cheng-hsien, an associate professor in the Department of Indigenous Studies and Social Work at National Dong Hwa University, notes that betel nut serves as a "cultural capsule" for Indigenous peoples [1](#user-content-fn-1).

For the Amis people, betel nut is a token of love. During the "Night of Lovers" in the Harvest Festival, women present betel nut to men they admire; if the man accepts and chews it on the spot, it signifies mutual affection. This system of "matrilocality" makes betel nut an essential medium for connecting emotions and families [1](#user-content-fn-1). The Siraya people view betel nut as a sacred object used to communicate with ancestral spirits. Duan Hong-kun, director of the Jibei Shuwen Studio, states that betel nut (Siraya: _Abiki_) is an indispensable offering when priests pray to _Alid_ (ancestral spirits), and it even functions as a ritual implement during ceremonies, holding a sacred status comparable to the "Peach Blossom Sword" of the Han people [1](#user $\text{user-content-fn-1}$). Duan also mentions that in Siraya rituals, betel nut used in sacrifices cannot be discarded carelessly; instead, it is placed on rooftops or in remote areas, demonstrating its sanctity [1](#user-content-fn-1).

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The multifaceted role of betel nut in Indigenous culture challenges the modern society's singular "unhealthy" label. It is not merely a substance, but a symbol for maintaining community, passing down beliefs, and expressing emotion—its significance far exceeds mere oral gratification.

#### Han Settlers' "Miasma-Cleansing Pill" and the Laborer's "Green Gold"

When Han immigrants arrived in Taiwan during the Qing Dynasty, they faced a hot, humid environment prone to miasma. Betel nut became an aid in adapting to this new land. Chewing betron nut causes the body to heat up and sweat, which was viewed as a "miasma-cleansing pill" (_xi zhang dan_) to expel dampness and prevent disease [2](#user-content-fn-2). This functional need allowed betel nut to take root in Han society, becoming an essential item for social interaction and wedding gifts. Even during conflict mediation, the party at fault would use betel nut as an expression of apology [2](#user-content-fn-2).

In the latter half of the 20th century, as Taiwan's economy took off, a massive influx of blue-collar workers entered the infrastructure sector. Due to its stimulating effects, betel nut became a daily necessity for the working class, including truck drivers and construction workers. Consequently, betel nut stalls emerged and reached their peak in the 1990s. The area of betel nut cultivation in Taiwan once reached as high as 65,000 hectares—second only to rice—with an annual output value reaching 8 billion TWD, earning it the nickname "Green Gold" [3](#user-content-fn-3). However, research by Professor Wu Chia-chun of the Department of Soil and Water Conservation at National Pingtung University indicates that leaving one hectare of land completely bare for a year would result in the erosion of 294 tons of soil (about three centimeters of topsoil), and the soil retention capability of betel nut plantations is far inferior to that of forests [3](#user-content-fn-3).

#### Betel Nut Beauties: A Unique Highway Landscape and Social Controversy

In 1976, the "Shuangdong Betel Nut Stall" in Caotun, Nantau, pioneered the "Betel Nut Beauty" sales model, using women dressed in revealing clothing to sell betel nut, which rapidly attracted customers. In the 1990s, the culture of the Betel Nut Beauties reached its zenith, becoming a unique feature of Taiwan's highways. They were not just salespeople but also a source of solace for many blue-collar workers during long shifts, representing an aesthetic of grassroots survival and a manifestation of the informal economy. However, the Betel Nut Beauties were often accompanied by social moral controversies, concerns regarding workplace safety, and criticisms regarding the objectification of women [4](#user-content-fn-4).

Another "hidden bestseller" at these stalls is "iced water" (_jiebingshui_). For drivers and workers laboring under the scorching sun, iced water provides more than just thirst relief; it serves as a tool for physical cooling, tied to the body or placed in vehicles to provide momentary respite. This simple commodity reflects the survival wisdom and needs of Taiwan's blue-collar laborers in harsh environments [5](#user-content-fn-5). One netizen shared that in repair shops or construction sites made of corrugated iron where temperatures reach nearly 40°C, iced water is an "oasis in the desert" [5](#user-content-fn-5).

#### The Double Cost to Health and the Environment

However, behind the prominence of betel nut lies a heavy cost to health and the environment. Medical research has confirmed that chewing betel nut is a primary cause of oral cancer. In Taiwan, approximately 2,500 to 3,000 people die from oral cancer annually, and up to 90% of oral cancer patients have a habit of chewing betel nut. The betel nut nut itself has been classified by the World Health Organization as a Group 1 carcinogen; it remains carcinogenic even without the addition of lime or _laoye_ (areca leaf) [6](#user-content-fn-6).

Regarding the environment, the shallow root system of the betel nut tree means its ability to stabilize soil is far inferior to deep-rooted species when planted on slopes. In 1994, Chen Hsin-hsiung, a professor in the Department of Forestry at National Taiwan University, proposed the "Betel Nut Nation-Destroying Theory," pointing out that single-layer betel nut canopies easily cause soil erosion and land degradation, exacerbating debris flows during typhoons and heavy rains [3](#user-content-fn-3). Although some studies suggest that betel nut is not the sole factor in soil erosion—with some views arguing that "all economic crops have shallow roots, but compared to others, the betel nut tree is actually one of the best for soil and water conservation" [7](#user-content-fn-7)—its negative environmental impact remains an indisputable fact. Although the government once promoted programs to convert betel nut plantations into forests, these efforts faced heavy resistance due to concerns over farmers' livelihoods [3](#user-content-fn-3).

#### Transformation and the Future: Swinging Between Tradition and Modernity

Facing the dual pressures of health and the environment, attitudes toward betel nut in Taiwan are gradually shifting. The proportion of the population chewing betel nut is declining annually, and the industry faces a period of transformation. The draft "Betel Nut Health Hazard Prevention Act" proposed by the Ministry of Health and Welfare will impose stricter regulations on the sale and advertising of betel nut, signaling that the era of the Betel Nut Beauties may be coming to an end [4](#user-content-fn-4).

Nevertheless, for many Indigenous communities, the cultural significance of betel nut remains deeply rooted. How to find a balance between respecting traditional culture and maintaining public health and environmental sustainability is a challenge Taiwan must confront. The story of betel nut is not just the rise and fall of a single fruit, but a microcosm of Taiwan's continuous adaptation and reflection during modernization; it reminds us that complex and subtle links exist between culture, economy, and the environment. This fruit stands as both a witness to history and a microcosm of the contradictions and tensions in contemporary Taiwanese society.
