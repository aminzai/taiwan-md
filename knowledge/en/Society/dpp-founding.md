# The Founding of the Democratic Progressive Party: How Outsiders Turned Taboo into Organization at the Grand Hotel

> **30-Second Summary:** On September 28, 1986, non-KMT figures announced the founding of the Democratic Progressive Party using a post-election support meeting held at the Taipei Grand Hotel. At this time, Taiwan was still under martial law, meaning the new party had no secure legal registration path. The formation drew upon years of local elections, service centers, political journals, street struggles, and legislative questioning. For the participants, it was an organizational venture requiring a calculation of political risk. It transformed "outsiders" from a relatively loose political position into an organization with a name, platform, charter, and candidate mechanism, yet it did not eliminate martial law, the all-powerful legislature, or association restrictions on the day of its founding. Understanding this gap is key to seeing what the formation truly changed.

## The Grand Hotel Was Not Originally Called a Party Formation Meeting

The meeting on September 28, 1986, was officially named the "1986 Non-KMT Election Support Group Candidate Recommendation Conference." Outsiders utilized an assembly that appeared to serve elections to place the move to form a party—an action that could not be publicly conducted—onto the agenda. The official history of the Democratic Progressive Party describes this day as the moment of founding, breaking through martial law restrictions; however, later participants recall that the venue, name, and meeting format were strategic maneuvers designed to circumvent political taboos.[^1] [^2] [^3]

This arrangement directly reflected the political climate at the time. The martial law system was still in effect, and organizing a new party could be interpreted as violating the existing order. Participants could not frame "we are founding an opposition party today" as a declaration of a regular social club. Placing the party formation within a candidate recommendation conference meant first borrowing an existing electoral network, and then making the political organization public _within_ that network. The initial task was to ensure people could gather safely, and also to let outsiders know that this meeting went beyond a mere support gathering. Discussions about the party charter and name had to proceed under the precondition that the assembly would not be immediately shut down.

Different sources have inconsistent records regarding the number of signatures taken on site, procedural details, and the time of the announcement. An article compiled by the Peace Foundation recorded approximately 130 signatures. A timeline from the National Human Rights Museum described the founding with 135 non-KMT figures signing.[^1] [^2] [^3] These discrepancies do not change the fact that the formation occurred, but they remind readers: political memory is not an automatically generated roster; later participants preserve the event from different vantage points.

## "Outsiders" Was a Position, Not a Complete Party

Before the formation, opposition forces were often referred to as "outsiders" (_dangwai_). This term did not equate to an organizational name; it referred to those who participated in politics through elections, legislatures, journals, service centers, or street actions without joining the Kuomintang (KMT). Research from the National Historical Park indicated that while the limited-seat by-election of the 1970s provided a space for outsider forces and later the Democratic Progressive Party to survive, studies on provincial assemblies showed that non-KMT legislators accumulated recognizable political issues through questioning in local self-government bodies, national legislative elections, freedom of association, and political cases.[^7] [^8] [^9]

The limited elections provided a gap into the legislature but also brought clear limitations. On one hand, opponents could enter the assembly, engage with voters, establish service centers, and cultivate support staff, giving local society a chance to see views different from those of the KMT. On the other hand, these by-elections did not replace the long-serving central legislators who had not undergone full re-election; martial law and the suppression of unrest still restricted political organization, publishing, and assemblies. The National Historical Park’s research on political reform places the Democratic Progressive Party's founding in 1986, the lifting of martial law in 1987, and subsequent legislative reforms as a continuous but distinct process. This means that while the formation opened an entry point for competition, it did not signify that the system was fully open.[^7]

The outsiders of the 1970s were not solely defined by votes. Research by Hsiao Ah-chin pointed out that diplomatic setbacks, local culture, the "return to reality" of young intellectuals, and political radicalization after the美麗島 (Beilidou) Incident in 1979 collectively formed the intellectual background of the outsider movement in the 1980s. What initially seemed like scattered historical writings, political journals, cultural discussions, and electoral mobilization gradually coalesced into a public debate about national identity, political rights, and Taiwan's social position.[^10]

This table does not portray all outsiders as belonging to one unified force. Research data shows that participants—from grassroots local activists, politicians in the legislature, street activists, journal editors, to overseas democrats—did not share a completely uniform understanding of democratization, Taiwanese independence, electoral strategy, or organizational forms. A thesis from Tsinghua University studying grassroots outsider movements particularly cautions that service centers and local organizations were the main channels through which the masses entered politics, and the logic of grassroots participants cannot be entirely superseded by the memories of a few founding leaders.[^6]

## From Public Policy Association to Secret Working Groups

The preparatory process before the formation involved multiple sub-organizations. The New Taiwan Peace Foundation compiled information on how outsider support groups in the 1970s, the Public Officials' Public Policy Research Association, and later the Federation accumulated experience coordinating views, candidates, and electoral work. An article by Chiu Wan-hsing from Tell Taiwan recorded interactions between the Public Policy Association and the Federation in 1986, the formation committee of the Overseas Taiwanese Democratic Party in May, the ten-person secret working group that began operating in July, and the name negotiations and _Outsider Bulletin_ surrounding the Grand Hotel meeting.[^3] [^4]

These organizations were not the Democratic Progressive Party itself, but they provided the necessary components for the formation. The Public Policy Association gave public officials a venue to exchange policies; the Federation connected information from different localities and candidates; and service centers offered supporters a fixed point of contact. The existence of secret working groups shows that participants had to re-coordinate signatures, platforms, charters, and factional representatives under political risk. A mere list of names could only provide a starting point, not constitute a complete new party organization.

The name "Democratic Progressive" was also not an organically appearing slogan. Both Tell Taiwan and the Peace Foundation's compilations place the name negotiation as a core process before and after the formation. The name needed to be broad enough for various outsider factions to enter together, yet clear enough so that voters knew it was not a temporary support group. The naming negotiations simultaneously addressed political lines: they placed democratic systems and social progress under one banner, but they failed to resolve members' differences regarding national positioning, street action, and electoral strategy on the day of founding.[^3] [^4]

## Political Actions at the Grand Hotel Meeting

The truly critical actions on September 28, 1986, were transforming "we are outsiders" into "we are forming a political party." This transformation involved three levels. First, the opposition forces gained a name recognizable to voters. Second, the organization could formulate a platform and charter, moving from temporary coordination to sustained operation. Third, candidates' participation was no longer just an individual or local support group effort but had a chance to be incorporated into a shared party strategy.

The official history of the Democratic Progressive Party records that after its founding, it held its first National Congress on November 10, 1986, at the Asia Hotel, where the platform, charter, and disciplinary arbitration rules were passed, and the first party chairman was elected. This time lag is significant. September 28 was the announcement of formation; November was when organizational rules, delegate structures, and disciplinary procedures were fixed. The announcement was merely the starting point. Subsequently, charters and procedures were needed to place different individuals into a continuously operating organization.[^1]

The first platform should not be reinterpreted solely through later party policies. The primary challenge facing the Democratic Progressive Party in 1986 was how to survive under martial law, party bans, and limited elections, and to make democracy, freedom, human rights, and social justice a communicable common language. It continuously revised its national positioning, cross-strait relations, social policies, and governing responsibilities later on; the founding declaration of 1986 cannot be treated as a pre-written blueprint for governance. CALD's introduction to the party places the Democratic Progressive Party within the context of authoritarian martial law followed by democratic consolidation, supporting this phased understanding.[^5]

The Grand Hotel meeting completed the _declaration_ of an organization; the legislative arena demanded that the party turn its declaration into verifiable political work. Candidates needed to propose views; caucuses needed to handle votes; local branches needed to respond to voters; and the central leadership needed to allocate resources among different factions. This work transformed "opposition" from a shared emotion into a continuous public responsibility, forcing the party to confront supporters' specific expectations regarding human rights, local self-governance, and constitutional reform. Legislative reform was not completed on the day of founding either. Institutional experience was gradually accumulated through this daily work.

## After Formation, the System Did Not Immediately Become Open Competition

The first reality after the formation was that it had to run in the old system. Research from the National Historical Park cited the 1987 Legislative Yuan presidential election, noting that candidates Hsu Rong-shu and Wu Shu-zhen of the newly formed Democratic Progressive Party participated in voting. This scene looked like multi-party politics, but it actually occurred within a legislature where by-election representatives coexisted with senior central legislators. The D.P.P.'s entry into the legislature did not mean that all legislative seats had been re-elected by the Taiwanese people.[^7]

The Britannica entry on political parties separates the founding in 1986, the lifting of martial law in 1987, the first election participation as an organized party in 1989, and subsequent changes in legislative seats. This periodization helps avoid a common simplification: writing the 1986 formation as a single moment of democratic completion. A more precise description is that the formation first established competitors; the lifting of martial law changed political risks; the reform of censorship and publishing expanded public communication; the legislative reform re-addressed representation; only then did relatively complete party competition emerge.[^5]

## The Historical Significance of the First Major Opposition Party

The importance of the Democratic Progressive Party's founding lies not just in whether it later gained control of the central government. An earlier significance is that it gave opposition politics an organizational form. Without a party, opponents were easily fragmented into individual candidates, local factions, journal editors, or case participants. After the party was established, voters could recognize a common symbol, members could participate in internal procedures, candidates could accept shared policies and organizational coordination, and the government had to view those previously labeled "outsiders" as a long-standing political adversary.

After politicization, existing social mobilization also transformed into new organizational responsibilities. The outsider movement drew strength from various social organizations and public issues; after party formation, the organization needed to handle candidate nominations, party discipline management, resource allocation, and policy prioritization. Street activists might demand faster systemic breakthroughs, local groups might be more concerned with service and votes, while internal leaders had to maintain cross-factional cooperation. The formation concentrated opposition forces while simultaneously making internal differences more visible.

Therefore, September 28, 1986, should be understood as a threshold, not the conclusion of the democratic story. The space provided by the Grand Hotel allowed a group that could not publicly organize to make a public declaration, yet subsequent party congresses, elections, legislative questioning, social movements, and systemic reforms were still required to turn party competition from a single venture into daily politics. Its historical value lies not in attributing all achievements of democratization to one party, but in pointing out how Taiwanese democracy was built layer by layer through local networks, public discourse, limited elections, and organized action.

## References

The history of the founding must be read by consulting official party histories, participant memories, academic research, and institutional data. The D.P.P.'s official page provides organizational self-understanding; articles by Chiu Wan-hsing and the Peace Foundation supplement the network of participants and support groups; while National Historical Park research places the founding within the context of limited elections, provincial assemblies, and national reform trajectories. Reading only one source risks turning the article into a party commemorative piece, a personal memoir, or an abstract overview of democratization.

Readers should also note that the 1986 formation and the later Democratic Progressive Party are not entirely identical political entities. The organization adjusts through elections, constitutional amendments, social movements, and governing experience. The value in researching the history of the founding lies in deconstructing the already familiar party competition back to the risks of that time: speaking publicly "we are forming a political party" before martial law was lifted was itself a challenge to who the state could restrict organization or representation for.

The formation also reoriented political responsibility. Under martial law, the government could attribute many public issues to security, anti-communism, or administrative management, and opponents often bore pressure as individuals. After the party's establishment, candidates, chairmen, delegates, and branches were all responsible for shared claims. This made political conflict more visible and meant that the organization could not rely solely on leader prestige. The platform had to answer the national direction; the charter had to handle internal power; local organizations had to face voters; and legislative caucuses had to translate street demands into legal and budgetary language. These tasks were not automatically completed by the declaration at the Grand Hotel, but they were the actual tests of whether the party could become part of a democratic system.

From this perspective, the relationship between the D.P.P.'s founding and Taiwanese democratization cannot be summarized merely as "the birth of an opposition party." It was also the beginning of organized social movements, institutionalized electoral competition, and the redistribution of national legitimacy. Outsiders originally had to navigate limited spaces; after formation, they could demand that the government publicly respond to a continuous political organization. The government also could not treat dissent merely as isolated incidents because parties accumulated issues, preserved records, cultivated candidates, and extended a single struggle into policy demands for the next election. This accumulation is what provided the organizational capacity to sustain the openness after 1987.

Therefore, the formation in 1986 was neither the completion of democratization by a single party nor an incidental event at the end of martial law. It concentrated political resources that were previously scattered across localities, legislatures, journals, and streets, creating an adversary capable of long-term competition with the ruling party within Taiwanese society. Subsequent lifting of martial law, full legislative re-election, direct presidential elections, and party rotation must be understood in the context of this organizational turning point.
