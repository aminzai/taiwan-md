> **30-Second Overview**: Energy poverty is not simply about "using too much electricity" or "not knowing how to save power"; it is about households lacking sufficient resources to access safe, affordable, and efficient energy services. For families living in old apartments, rooftop additions, or poorly ventilated rooms, or those using second-hand, high-energy-consumption air conditioners, turning on the AC may cause bill pressure, while not turning it on increases the risk of heat stress. Taiwan currently lacks a nationally consistent definition of energy poverty, but civil society surveys have found that the proportion of energy expenditure to disposable income for assisted vulnerable households is significantly high. [^1] When high temperatures are viewed by the government as a cross-departmental risk, energy poverty can no longer be just a matter of electricity-saving campaigns; it must become a public issue jointly addressed by housing, social welfare, health, and climate adaptation. [^2]

# Energy Poverty Is Not About "Insufficient Frugality"

Energy poverty is most easily misunderstood as a lack of electricity-saving habits in households: turning off the AC less, using hot water less, and turning off lights, which would lower the bill. However, this view ignores the actual range of choices available to households. Living in a residence with good insulation, window shading, and high-efficiency AC, versus living in a west-facing, leaking, poorly ventilated residence with old appliances—even if the indoor and outdoor temperatures are the same—does not result in the same energy demand.

Therefore, energy poverty includes at least three layers. The first is **purchasing power**: the proportion of energy bills to income is too high, forcing households to choose between food, healthcare, transportation, and electricity. The second is **quality of energy services**: even if a household can afford the bill, they may live in spaces that are too hot, too cold, damp, or poorly ventilated. The third is **accessibility**: renters, elderly people living alone, persons with disabilities, or families without upfront capital may be unable to replace AC units, improve windows, or apply for complex subsidies on their own.

The Taiwan Youth Climate Alliance, when organizing past research, pointed out that Taiwan has not yet formed a recognized definition of energy poverty, citing a joint survey by the Industrial Technology Research Institute (ITRI) and Family Welfare Foundation, which indicated that energy expenditure for assisted vulnerable households averages 18% of disposable income. This figure should not be directly extrapolated as the proportion for the entire population, but it reminds us: if we only judge whether a household is energy-efficient based on total electricity consumption, we may mistakenly label households that are "forced into high expenditure due to poor housing and equipment" as wasteful. [^1] Research from National Taiwan University also places energy burden within the interactive relationship of housing conditions and vulnerable households, rather than attributing the problem solely to individual consumption choices. [^8]

# Why Summer Energy Poverty Is Particularly Dangerous

In the past, discussions on energy poverty often imagined winter heating as the primary focus. Taiwan, however, faces another seasonal risk: summer high temperatures. Vulnerable households may not "lack energy demand," but rather have demand but dare not use it. As indoor temperatures rise, households may choose to shorten AC usage time, set the AC temperature too high, or only allow children and the elderly to enter rooms with AC. These are adaptive behaviors under bill pressure, not simple consumption preferences.

The National Health Administration explicitly warns that when indoor air is stagnant and heat cannot be expelled, heat stress can occur indoors. The elderly and children have weaker temperature regulation abilities and should pay special attention to high indoor temperatures. Official advice includes regularly replenishing water, maintaining ventilation, and setting the AC temperature between 26 and 27°C when in use. [^3] These recommendations are easier to achieve for households with AC, normal ventilation, and the ability to pay electricity bills; for those living on rooftops, in iron-sheet additions, or with windows that cannot be opened, architectural and equipment investment is needed to truly implement them.

High-temperature risks also have an accumulative nature. Houses absorb heat during the day; if they cannot dissipate it at night, the elderly may be continuously exposed to high temperatures during sleep. Clogged AC filters and poor heat dissipation of outdoor units mean that the same cooling effect requires more electricity. This indicates that "thermal safety" cannot be measured by the temperature at a single moment, but by how a residence absorbs, stores, and dissipates heat throughout the day.

# How an Old House Turns High Temperatures into High Bills

The energy dilemma of old residences is usually not caused by a single piece of equipment. Insufficient insulation of walls and roofs increases AC load. Windows without shading allow west-facing heat to enter the room directly. Old AC units have lower efficiency and may not stabilize cooling even after starting. Filters and outdoor units that have not been cleaned for a long time increase equipment power consumption and the risk of failure. If a household lacks upfront capital, they may only be able to continue paying high operating costs, without the ability to improve the root causes of these costs.

Energy poverty is thus connected to housing rights, rental rights, and building governance. Homeowners can decide whether to replace windows, install shading, or improve exterior walls. Renters may worry that renovation costs cannot be recovered, or they may lack the authority to perform construction. If subsidies are only given to equipment purchasers, it may exclude households that need improvement the most but do not have decision-making power over the property.

Yilan County once promoted subsidies for residential energy-saving home insulation retrofitting, including shading, insulated glass, roof, and exterior wall insulation within the subsidy scope, reflecting that local governments have begun to view the housing shell as part of energy saving and cooling. This program was a past annual plan and cannot be directly viewed as the current nationwide policy, but it provides an important direction: improving thermal comfort does not necessarily start with buying a larger AC; it can also start with reducing the house's heat absorption. [^4]

# Why Subsidies Do Not Necessarily Equal Accessibility

Both central and local governments have recently introduced subsidies for appliance replacement, residential energy saving, or insulation. Policy design usually hopes to exchange limited funds for electricity saving, carbon reduction, and reduced household expenditure, but there is still a gap between "being eligible" and "being able to complete the application."

Take the application page for the Ministry of Economic Affairs' (MOEA) Residential Appliance Subsidy as an example. Applicants need to prepare identity documents, bank books, electricity bills or proof of electricity usage, invoices, warranty cards, and waste four-category appliance recycling forms. Subsidies are also limited by annual budgets and review conditions. [^5] For those with stable bank accounts, the ability to pay for new machines upfront, and the ability to preserve complete documents, this is a manageable procedure; for those without upfront capital, whose appliances are owned by landlords, or whose lives are assisted by social workers and relatives/friends, it may form a substantial barrier.

The Taipei City Government's 2026 Residential Community Energy Creation, Storage, and Energy Saving Subsidy provides a more complete local case: in addition to community energy creation, storage, and glass insulation film, it also subsidizes Level 1 energy-efficiency refrigerator and AC replacement for registered low-income households. Announcing and setting upper limits for refrigerator and AC subsidies shows that local governments have begun to incorporate vulnerable household equipment efficiency into net-zero and just transition goals. [^6] However, such programs still need to address three issues: whether subsidies cover renters, whether they can pay for installation and dismantling costs, and when the next support will appear after the plan is full or the deadline passes.

Taoyuan City's Cooling and Energy Saving Subsidy for High-Temperature Sensitive Groups includes children aged 0 to 6 and elderly people aged 65 and above as targets, subsidizing AC cleaning, glass insulation film, and old AC replacement. The significance of such policies lies in the fact that it does not only identify needs according to low-income qualifications, but also attempts to find potentially overlooked households using age and high-temperature sensitivity. [^7] However, to avoid welfare fragmentation, age, income, housing conditions, and health risks should be reasonably connected between social welfare and health systems, rather than requiring families to repeatedly prove they deserve help.

# From Electricity-Saving Campaigns to Residential Thermal Safety

The core message of traditional electricity-saving campaigns is "use less," while energy poverty policies must add another question: "how to get sufficient safety and comfort with less energy?" The two are not contradictory, but the policy tools are different.

| Policy Level   | Traditional Electricity-Saving Approach                 | Residential Thermal Safety Approach                                                                   |
| -------------- | ------------------------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Equipment      | Encourage turning off lights, raising AC temperature    | Replace high-energy-consumption AC, clean filters, improve outdoor unit heat dissipation              |
| Building       | Campaign for shading and ventilation                    | Subsidize window shading, insulation film, roof, and exterior wall insulation                         |
| Social Welfare | Distribute subsidies based on low-income qualifications | Social workers, health centers, and communities proactively identify high-temperature risk households |
| Information    | Provide electricity-saving knowledge                    | Provide simplified applications, home-based energy diagnostics, and electricity improvement advice    |
| Evaluation     | Calculate how many kWh are saved                        | Simultaneously measure heat stress, indoor temperature, bill burden, and quality of life              |

The first shift is **from post-event subsidies to front-end diagnostics**. Social workers, neighborhood offices, home care workers, or health center personnel can record during visits whether the residence is on a rooftop, west-facing, unable to ventilate, has broken AC, or old appliances. This data does not need to be used for labeling, but to arrange energy diagnostics and improvement sequences.

The second shift is **from single-unit equipment to residential and community scales**. A high-efficiency AC can reduce electricity use, but it cannot solve the heat absorption of the entire building's roof, the stuffiness of public areas, or insufficient power capacity. If a community can jointly improve shading, roofs, elevators, and public electricity, it may be more efficient than requiring each household to invest on their own.

The third shift is **incorporating high temperatures into disaster prevention and health governance**. The Ministry of Environment (MOE) piloted the "High-Temperature Adaptation Countermeasure Drill" for the first time in 2025, using the scenario of Taipei and New Taipei City experiencing three consecutive days over 38°C with red-light warnings, allowing central ministries and local governments to jointly handle key group care, livelihood energy, and community adaptation. [^2] This drill also pointed out that high temperatures had not yet been fully incorporated into the natural disaster category of the _Disaster Prevention and Rescue Act_, meaning the system still needs clarification: when to activate, who contacts the elderly living alone, which venues become cooling centers, and how to maintain these services during power outages.

# Energy Transition Should Not Shift Costs to the Weakest

Energy transition is often measured by carbon reduction, power supply stability, and industrial competitiveness, but if low-income households cannot maintain basic thermal safety due to electricity prices, equipment, and housing conditions, the transition may produce new inequalities. This is not opposing electricity saving or improving energy efficiency, but requiring policies to simultaneously answer "who gets improvement first" and "who bears the transition cost."

The energy poverty discussions organized by the Taiwan Youth Climate Alliance have reminded policymakers that policies should combine objective and subjective indicators, not just look at the proportion of electricity bills or total electricity consumption. It should also include building conditions, equipment efficiency, demographic structure, thermal comfort, and well-being. [^1] Public Television's special report places energy burden within the actual life context of vulnerable households, explaining that if energy transition lacks distributional design, it may cause those already bearing higher housing and equipment costs to bear new burdens. [^9] This approach is particularly important for Taiwan, because a household may use more electricity due to fear of heat, or use abnormally low electricity due to the inability to pay bills; neither can be judged solely by the bill.

Local government high-temperature drills and energy-saving subsidies provide space for policy experimentation. The central government needs to establish common minimum standards, including thermal safety indicators for vulnerable households, accessible design for subsidy applications, responsibility allocation between renters and landlords, and privacy protection for energy diagnostic data. If each county and city launches short-term schemes independently, households may receive completely different protection due to differences in household registration, application timing, and information capabilities.

# A Executable Taiwan Plan

Taiwan does not need to establish a complex single energy poverty score from the beginning, but can start with an operable service process. The central government previously supported local energy-saving governance through the County-City Joint Residential and Commercial Electricity Saving Plan, providing an institutional entry point to connect energy diagnostics, social welfare referrals, and housing improvements to existing administrative networks. [^10] The first step is for local governments to establish "Residential Thermal Safety Referral": when social workers, health centers, fire departments, home care, or community organizations find a household with high-temperature risks, they can refer them for energy diagnostics, rather than just providing one-time electricity bill subsidies.

The second step is to divide improvement measures into three cost levels. Low cost includes cleaning AC, adjusting shading, improving ventilation, and providing thermometers. Medium cost includes insulation film, curtains, outdoor unit heat dissipation, and energy-saving appliances. High cost includes roofs, exterior walls, windows, and community public space retrofitting. This avoids requiring all households to purchase expensive equipment and allows limited budgets to first handle the most dangerous residences.

The third step is to expand effectiveness from "how much electricity is saved" to "whether risks are reduced." The government can track indoor high-temperature hours, heat stress medical visits, household energy expenditure burden, equipment failure rates, and residents' subjective thermal comfort, rather than deciding plan success solely by total electricity savings. A decrease in electricity savings but an increase in heat stress cannot be called success; a slight increase in household electricity use, but obtaining safe cooling due to high-efficiency equipment, should not be simplified as failure.

This system also needs a minimum dataset usable by local workers. Households do not need to be forced to reveal all consumption details, but at least, with consent, they should record the residential floor, roof type, main heat sources, AC and refrigerator age, indoor ventilation, whether there are high-temperature sensitive individuals in the population, and whether the most recent quarter's energy expenditure caused life trade-offs. Data should be stored in layers with social welfare case data for the purpose of service improvement, and households should know which data is used by whom. If data is only used to determine "whether they deserve subsidies," it increases stigma; if data is used to arrange cleaning, repair, insulation, and referral, it can turn energy policy into a preventive public service.

Local governments can also establish "Thermal Safety Service Packages": before heatwave warnings, community centers, home care, and health centers contact high-risk households. During warnings, provide walkable cooling venues, drinking water, charging, and emergency contacts. After warnings, check if new problems arise with AC, fans, windows, and bill burdens. This service should not only start after disasters or emergency room visits occur, but become routine summer community care work. For remote areas and offshore islands, the service package should also include maintenance manpower, parts delivery, and power outage backups; otherwise, even the best subsidies may lose effect when equipment fails.

# Conclusion: Making Safe Cooling a Basic Life Capability

The core of energy poverty is not requiring vulnerable households to have more willpower, but acknowledging that housing conditions and energy services themselves have inequalities. When old houses absorb heat, high-energy-consumption appliances, upfront purchase costs, subsidy application thresholds, and extreme high temperatures overlap, an electricity bill simultaneously presents income, housing, health, and climate risks.

Taiwan has begun to accumulate local insulation subsidies, appliance replacement, high-temperature drills, and community energy-saving policy tools. The next step is not to add another sentence of "please save electricity," but to string these tools into a human-centered residential thermal safety system: proactively find risk households, first improve the most energy-consuming housing conditions, allow subsidies to be used by renters and those unable to pay upfront, and use health and quality of life to test whether energy transition is fair.

When a household can turn on the AC with peace of mind in high temperatures, without having to choose between health and bills, energy policy truly moves from "supply management" to public well-being.

Policy evaluation should also retain the voices of residents. Local governments can ask before and after services whether it is easier to cool the room, whether AC usage time is shortened due to electricity bills, whether equipment is more reliable, and whether residents know which venues to seek help from during the next high-temperature warning. These feedbacks should not replace energy expenditure and health data, but be listed alongside quantitative indicators, allowing the government to know whether the plan actually improved lives or only completed procurement and reimbursement. If subsidies give households equipment but do not solve rental restrictions, maintenance responsibilities, and subsequent electricity bills, the policy needs redesign, rather than blaming residents for non-cooperation and continuously conducting public reviews and corrections.
