> **30-Second Overview**: Social housing is not a construction project that ends once the government builds homes and holds a lottery. It is a public institution that enables people without the means or desire to buy homes to still obtain stable housing. Since Taiwan amended the _Housing Act_ in 2017 to establish the legal foundation for social housing policy, the "8 Years, 200,000 Units" target has placed direct government construction and private-sector lease management within the same policy framework.[^1] But "unit counts" do not mean every household can actually live there: location, rent, application documents, vulnerable quotas, lease terms, transportation, childcare, and management quality determine whether a policy truly changes lives.
>
> This article uses three questions to guide readers through the policy. First, what different types of housing and subsidies does the government actually provide? Second, how do published "unit counts" distinguish between planned, completed, leased, valid contracts, occupancy, and duplicate assistance? Third, when housing policy enters local communities, who is responsible for rental stability, maintenance, accessibility, care services, neighbor relations, and grievances? The answers determine whether social housing is a short-term supply project or public infrastructure that can span unemployment, family formation, child-rearing, disability, and aging.

## Why Housing Justice Is Not Just About Prices

When Taiwan discusses housing issues, the most common metrics are price-to-income ratios, loan burdens, and transaction prices. These indicators matter, yet they do not capture everyone's housing experience. For renter households, the issues also include whether leases can be renewed, whether landlords accept children or pets, whether homes have leaks or fire hazards, whether rents suddenly spike with the market, and whether moving allows them to maintain existing work, school, and care networks.

Housing justice therefore has at least four dimensions. First is **affordability**: rent and management fees must not force households to choose among food, healthcare, education, and housing. Second is **stability**: families must not face repeated displacement due to short-term leases, landlord sales, or use changes. Third is **quality and accessibility**: housing must have basic safety, accessibility, ventilation, daylight, and public services, with reasonable connections to work, school, and care. Fourth is **choice and dignity**: vulnerable status should not confine people to remote, poor-quality, or stigmatized locations.

Social housing plays the role of "non-market, but not temporary relief" housing supply. It does not require every renter to meet the lowest income threshold, nor does it push all households toward homeownership. Instead, the government or government-regulated entities provide long-term, tiered, affordable rental options. The value of this system lies not in replacing the private rental market, but in preserving a public baseline for those whom the market cannot stably serve.

## From the Housing Act to Eight Years, 200,000 Units

The institutional turning point for Taiwan's social housing policy is closely tied to the 2017 amendment of the _Housing Act_. The Executive Yuan's policy explanation notes that the government amended the _Housing Act_ in January 2017 and approved the Social Housing Construction Plan in March that year, incorporating direct construction and lease management into the policy toolkit.[^1] The significance of this turn is that housing policy no longer consisted solely of purchase loans, housing subsidies, or individual vulnerable placements, but began to recognize "affordable and stable renting" itself as a public policy goal.

"8 Years, 200,000 Units" is not a single type of housing, but the sum of two supply methods. The government directly acquires land to build or renovate housing, creating centralized supply that can better control rents, quotas, and public facilities over the long term. Lease management utilizes existing private housing, with operators assisting landlords in leasing, management, and maintenance, allowing policy to reach housing stock dispersed throughout cities more quickly. The two differ in time, land, cost, and community impact; they cannot be measured with a single yardstick comparing only unit counts.

The Executive Yuan explained in 2023 that the social housing policy at the time comprised 120,000 directly constructed units and 80,000 lease management units to form the 200,000-unit target, with an estimated combined total of 163,000 units by the end of 2023.[^2] Here, "achievement" encompasses different stages: some are completed and occupied, some have valid leases, some are still under construction or in matching. Therefore, public communication citing only a single aggregate number easily misleads the public into thinking "that many homes are immediately available for move-in."

In 2024, policy explanations extended the horizon to 2032, proposing a "One Million Renter Household Support" framework composed of 250,000 social housing units, 250,000 lease management units, and 500,000 rent subsidy households.[^3] This represents the government's gradual understanding of housing policy as multi-layered support: stable tenancy for those who can access social housing; enhanced contract and management guarantees for those in private housing through lease management; and rent subsidies to reduce costs for those who obtain neither of the above.

## How the Three Tracks Complement Each Other

| Policy Tool                           | Main Approach                                                                       | Advantages                                                                  | Key Governance Risks                                                                                             |
| ------------------------------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Direct Construction of Social Housing | Build public rental housing on state-owned, local government, or urban renewal land | Rents, quotas, design, and public services can be planned long-term         | Slow land acquisition, high construction costs, prone to concentration in few locations                          |
| Lease Management                      | Operators assist landlords in leasing, management, maintenance, and tenant matching | Rapid use of existing housing, dispersed in private communities             | Housing quality, lease stability, landlord participation, and oversight mechanisms require continuous monitoring |
| Rent Subsidies                        | Direct subsidies to qualified renter households                                     | High application flexibility, can quickly cover large numbers of households | May push up some market rents; subsidies do not necessarily improve housing quality or lease terms               |

## Clarifying What the Numbers Actually Count

The "household" (戶) in policy data is not a single concept. Directly constructed social housing refers to public-sector housing supply; lease management typically involves matching private housing stock and valid leases; rent subsidies are households receiving cash or rent support. Simply adding the three together risks double-counting the same household and conflating "already moved in," "already have valid contract," and "receiving subsidy" as the same outcome. Policy reports should therefore simultaneously present supply type, statistical timepoint, completion stage, whether assisted households overlap, and exit and renewal situations for each tool.

This distinction of statistical scope is not a technical detail; it is the core of housing justice. If the government only publishes cumulative matches, renters cannot know whether units are currently available; if only planned units are published, the public cannot know when homes will be completed and leased; if only subsidy households are published, society cannot judge whether subsidies helped tenants move to safer, more stable, or more job-accessible housing. A public policy dashboard could be established in the future, separating each household by stage—"planning, construction, completion, leasing, move-in, renewal, exit"—with quarterly updates.

Data transparency should also include "those not served." For example, the government could publish per-county application numbers, reasons for ineligibility, post-lottery withdrawal rates, post-move-in departure reasons, average repair complaint handling times, and actual tenancy durations by household type. These indicators are not personal data disclosure; they transform the policy's most easily overlooked leakage points into verifiable governance questions: Is supply too low? Are rent and management fees too high? Is location unsuitable? Is the application process too complex? Is community support insufficient? If tracking only "how many built" and "how much subsidized," we cannot know why families leave at the system's threshold, or why they must move again after moving in.

Lease Management 4.0 shows the government attempting to solve the dual problem of "social housing not built fast enough" and "private vacant homes not entering formal rental markets." The Executive Yuan explained at the time that landlords could receive tax incentives, three years of management services, and assistance with repair, notarization, and insurance costs; operators also helped tenants apply for rent subsidies and relaxed cross-county relocation for seniors and persons with disabilities.[^4] These designs address landlord transaction risk, tenant application costs, and seniors' accessibility needs within a single policy.

However, lease management cannot be reduced to "slapping a social housing label on ordinary homes." Its public nature derives from regulation of contracts, rents, eligibility, services, and tax incentives. If only matched unit counts increase without continuous auditing of housing quality, lease changes, repair responsibilities, and tenant complaints, the policy may resemble a market broker more than a housing guarantee. The government should publish valid contracts, departure reasons, lease term distribution, housing quality, and vulnerable household retention, enabling society to distinguish "matching success" from "long-term stable residency."

## After Unit Counts Rise, Who Actually Gets to Live There

The MOL's 2026 announcement stated that as of end-2025, directly constructed social housing nationwide totaled 122,680 units, lease management 104,803 units, and rent subsidy beneficiaries 913,867 households; after deducting policy overlap, over 1.1 million renter households received assistance.[^5] These figures show the policy's significant scale, and also that the three tools' statistical units differ: direct construction counts housing units, lease management counts matches and contracts, rent subsidies count assisted households. Evaluation cannot mechanically add the three, nor infer equal effectiveness of other tracks from one track's growth.

Moving into directly constructed social housing typically involves eligibility review, application documents, lottery or scoring, viewing, deposit, and lease signing. The current _MOL Regulations on Leasing Social Housing_ require applicants to meet conditions including no self-owned housing, income and asset limits, and allow application based on local study or employment needs; regulations also mandate publication of rent, management fees, vulnerable quotas, application methods, lease terms, and ranking determination.[^6] These provisions provide a skeletal framework for fair competition, but for those without stable internet, incomplete household registration documents, complex family situations, or needing accessible housing, the application process can still be a barrier.

The National Housing and Urban Regeneration Center's 2025 announcement of the first wave of four projects open for lease offered 1,470 units, with quotas designed for economically or socially disadvantaged, good-neighbor, and frontline police/firefighter categories; the announcement also noted rent includes management fees, cross-county application is allowed, and maximum lease terms vary by identity.[^7] The significance of the quota system is to prevent all housing from going to those with the most information, best application skills, or greatest capacity to bear moving costs. But quotas are not outcome guarantees: if housing is far from work and school, rent plus management fees still exceed household affordability, or lottery winners cannot afford moving and furniture costs, formal eligibility may not translate into actual occupancy.

## Housing Price and Location Are Equally Important

Social housing rent cannot be described merely as "below market." Market rent itself varies by location, floor area, building age, management fees, furnishings, transportation, and housing quality; the same rent in a city center, near an MRT station, in a remote industrial zone, or in an area lacking public transit represents completely different living costs. If a family must add two hours of commuting, childcare pickup/drop-off, or eldercare transport costs for cheaper rent, the apparent rent discount may be offset by other costs.

The _MOL Regulations on Leasing Social Housing_ stipulate that central government social housing rent may be tiered with reference to cost, appraisal, and tenant affordability, not exceeding market rent levels, and should be reviewed regularly.[^6] This makes rent not just cost recovery but also distributive policy. Future rent publication should simultaneously disclose management fees, parking fees, deposits, furnishings, public facility fees, and household income tiers; otherwise "how much is rent" remains insufficient for applicants to estimate true post-move-in expenditure.

Location also determines whether social housing integrates into the city. The Executive Yuan policy explanation mentions social housing can acquire land through state/public land, urban renewal, and transit-oriented development, and incorporate senior daycare, infant/toddler care, childcare, and youth entrepreneurship services.[^1] This is an important direction: social housing should not be just a residential volume, but planned together with schools, healthcare, transportation, care, commerce, and green space. If public services are insufficient, social housing residents may be forced to bear extra care costs; if services exist only at large sites, dispersed lease management households may be excluded from community resources.

## Local Governments Are Not Mere Implementation Windows

Social housing construction, leasing, and management require central and local government collaboration. The central government can legislate, provide land and funding, standardize data formats and minimum service standards. Local governments are closer to rental markets, welfare networks, schools, transportation, and community conflicts. Localities do not finish by merely "executing central policy"; they must adjust housing portfolios based on population structure, housing prices and rents, industrial locations, disaster risks, and long-term care needs.

The MOL's latest policy explanation places central-local cooperation, direct construction, lease management, and rent subsidies in the same housing support framework, noting different policies have different immediacy, accessibility, and supply progress.[^8] This division of labor needs more transparent accountability lines. For example, when central subsidies are delayed, who explains to tenants? When local eligibility review rejects an application, is there a unified appeal deadline? When lease management operators fail to handle repairs, which level of government can tenants turn to? If answers exist only on different websites, phone lines, and in case officers' experience, the policy creates inequality for those with weaker information capacity.

Local social housing must also handle neighbor relations. Social housing residents are not a homogeneous "vulnerable population"; they may include youth, single-parent families, persons with disabilities, Indigenous peoples, seniors, police/firefighters, newlyweds, and child-rearing families. If the government only emphasizes resident identities without publishing community management, public space, noise, waste, childcare, and care service collaboration mechanisms, neighbors may mistakenly attribute daily management issues to resident identities. Good social housing governance should let residents participate in rules and public space decisions while providing professional social workers, property management, and mediation resources.

## Vacant Homes, Carbon Emissions, and Choices Beyond "How Many Built"

Housing policy has an often-overlooked trade-off: how to balance new construction with existing housing revitalization. The MOL has noted that Taiwan has a large number of vacant homes, some of which have potential for conversion to lease management social housing, linking vacant home revitalization, house tax, and rental incentives to rental policy.[^5] This does not mean all vacant homes can directly become suitable housing. Vacant homes may be in areas with few job opportunities, lack elevators and accessibility, require costly repairs, or involve inheritance, property rights, and urban planning restrictions.

But vacant home policy reminds us that social housing need not be measured only by new construction units. Existing building renovation can reduce land development, preserve street life and transportation conditions, and may better suit dispersed living. New social housing can incorporate earthquake resistance, energy efficiency, accessibility, and public services, providing long-term controllable rental space. The two should be combined based on local conditions, not simplified into "new construction is always progressive, revitalization is always cheaper."

The MOL's 2026 residential support announcement sets diversified social housing construction, lease management, and rent subsidies as continuing parallel policy tools, and lists newlywed and child-rearing families as subsequent support priorities.[^8] For such a policy mix to remain effective long-term, environmental and social indicators should be added: distance from each unit to mass transit, childcare and daycare capacity, energy expenditure, repair response time, lease renewal rate, post-move school and employment stability, and actual occupancy rates of vulnerable quotas. Only then will public discourse not be trapped in the single number of "how many more units this year."

## A Trustworthy Housing Justice Institution

First, the government should establish consistent and understandable public data. Different policies' "approved, planned, completed, leased, occupied, valid contracts, renewed" must be presented separately, with clarification on whether the same household receives duplicate assistance. The MOL's August 2026 data further publishes social housing, lease management, and rent subsidy household counts, and the median share of rent subsidies in rent expenditure; such disaggregated data enables better public scrutiny than a single aggregate count.[^9]

Second, the application system should be designed around "successful move-in" rather than "application submitted." The government can provide cross-county joint application portals, phone and in-person assistance, so seniors, persons with disabilities, those lacking digital devices, and domestic violence survivors need not repeatedly explain the same identity. For those who win the lottery but cannot move in immediately, deposit, moving, furniture, transportation, and housing adjustment support should be provided; otherwise lottery success may remain an unaffordable option.

Third, social housing management must guarantee tenants' lease stability and grievance rights. The government and management operators should publish repair timelines, lease change rules, visit boundaries, data usage methods, and complaint response times. Tenants should not fear retaliation against renewal for raising reasonable repair or public facility issues. Vulnerable quotas should not only be identified at move-in, but track whether different groups leave faster due to rent, accessibility, care, or community conflict.

Fourth, direct social housing, lease management, and rent subsidies should be mutually transferable. When household income rises, families should not suddenly lose all housing support. When seniors need accessible housing, they should be able to transfer from general rental or lease management to suitable units. When families face unemployment, illness, domestic violence, or disaster, there should be temporary rent and social service buffers. If housing policy only judges "eligible or not" at qualification review, it cannot respond to real life's changes.

## Making Application Fair Beyond the Lottery

Social housing uses lottery or scoring to prevent limited housing from being monopolized by connections, financial power, or information advantage, but pre-lottery procedures can still create gaps. Those who learn of announcements early, understand income calculation, prepare asset documents, fill forms online, and submit supplements within deadlines usually have advantage over applicants juggling shift work, childcare, or caring for disabled family members. This does not mean abolishing public lottery, but treating application assistance as part of housing policy. Local governments can set up regular consultation at community sites, household registration offices, libraries, and welfare centers, providing multilingual, accessible, and paper channels, and explaining rejection reasons in language ordinary people understand.

Fairness also means responsibility for "hard-to-see" renters. Those without fixed household registration, just leaving placement institutions, suffering domestic violence, separated from family, or working in different counties may not fit the typical family image, yet equally need stable housing. Regulations have designed exceptions and protective clauses in eligibility, vulnerable status, and household member calculation; actual implementation must ensure case officers have sufficient training to not treat complex families as simply incomplete documentation. For tenants, whether they receive clear supplement notices, appeal channels, and reasonable wait times often determines trust in the institution more than the policy's name.

## Conclusion: Turning "Afford to Live" into "Can Stay"

Taiwan's next phase of social housing should not merely replace "200,000 units" with a larger number, but answer three harder questions: Are homes where they are needed? Do rental conditions let families stay long-term? Are public resources allocated transparently, appealably, and without stigma? Executive Yuan policy data shows direct construction, lease management, and rent subsidies have formed a three-track system of different scales and speeds.[^10] The next challenge is turning three tracks from statistical parallels into continuous support that families can enter, switch between, and exit at different life stages.

Housing justice does not guarantee everyone lives in the same housing, nor does it hand all housing problems to government alone. It requires government to acknowledge housing's public nature, markets to accept basic rental norms, and local communities to have capacity to jointly manage living space. When a person changes jobs, forms a family, gives birth, becomes disabled, or ages, and can still find safe, affordable, and dignified housing in a familiar city, social housing truly becomes a public institution rather than a construction project.
