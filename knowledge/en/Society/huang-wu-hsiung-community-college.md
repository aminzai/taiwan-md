# Huang Wu-shiong and Taiwan’s Community Colleges: Bringing Democracy Beyond the Ballot into the Classroom

> **30-Second Overview:** In 1994, Huang Wu-shiong proposed the concept of democratized community colleges. In 1998, Taiwan’s first community college was established in the Wenshan District of Taipei City. This article traces how an educational movement originally aimed at bringing adult learning into public life became a daily practice of Taiwan’s civil society through courses, clubs, local research, and legal frameworks.

In 1994, Huang Wu-shiong, a professor in the Department of Mathematics at National Taiwan University, proposed the concept of democratized community colleges. In 1998, Taiwan’s first community college was established in the Wenshan District of Taipei City. On September 20, 1999, the Yonghe Community College held its founding ceremony at Fuhe Junior High School, with Huang Wu-shiong serving as the founding director. [^1] [^2]

These three years may appear as a timeline of educational institutions, but the real question lies not in “how many community colleges Taiwan has.” The question is: Why did a school with no academic degree requirements and no primary focus on furthering academic degrees bring its curriculum into rivers, old streets, community newspapers, and local issues?

Huang Wu-shiong’s answer was direct. He did not envision community colleges as merely cheaper universities, nor as stepping stones to conventional universities. He sought to address another gap in the post-Martial Law era: People had ballots, but they did not necessarily have sufficient experience in public discussion to collectively address problems in their immediate surroundings. [^3]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** The most counter-intuitive aspect of community colleges is that, while they look like schools, they actually turn the question of “should schools only teach classes” into a democratic issue.

## A School, Starting with “What It Is Not”

In Huang Wu-shiong’s 2012 compilation of local government implementation plans, community colleges are distinct from community colleges that emphasize only lifelong learning or vocational training. That plan places educational reform and social reform together, arguing that community colleges should expand the public sphere for citizens and cultivate the abilities to reflect, think critically, and critique. [^4]

This difference is not merely semantic. If community colleges simply moved existing courses to the evening, students would remain consumers after class. The students Huang Wu-shiong envisioned would move from “I want to learn a course” to “I want to understand an issue together with my neighbors.” In his design, club activities are not extracurricular decorations, but learning fields where people engage with public affairs.

The historical pages of Yonghe Community College preserve this curricular division. Academic courses handle humanities, natural sciences, and social sciences; club activity courses allow students to practice participating in public affairs; and life skills courses handle practical skills and daily life. The three are not about slicing people into three different uses, but about reconnecting a person’s world. [^1]

This design also explains why woodworking, astronomy, film, and local history can appear simultaneously in community colleges. A report in _Taiwan Panorama_ noted that the Wenshan Community College initially offered common university courses such as economics and political science, but later found that the “packaged knowledge” in academia was not necessarily suitable for adults. It gradually added experiential courses such as woodworking, astronomical phenomena, and analyzing ethnicity through film, while encouraging students to organize clubs and participate in community building. [^5]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** Community colleges do not lower the difficulty of knowledge; rather, they return knowledge to the places where people truly live, allowing a schedule to begin touching a river, a building, and a neglected history.

## Wenshan: The Place Outside the First Classroom

The Wenshan Community College, established in 1998, had no existing Taiwanese example to copy. It had to answer why adults should return to the classroom outside of work and family, and what happens to learning after a class ends—whether it can remain in the community. This made “the place” not just a school address, but the teaching material itself.

An English edition of _Taiwan Panorama_ reported on the local research of Wenshan Community College, recording teachers and students leaving the classroom to measure the water quality of the Dahan River, observe the surrounding environment, and accumulate “Wenshan Studies” through local literature and resident work. The same report also mentioned that community college teachers and students across Taiwan engaged in environmental care, historical records, and local cultural actions, making community colleges a node in grassroots cultural activism. [^6]

Here lies an easily overlooked turning point. Community colleges did not start with a centrally planned “local revitalization curriculum” and then fill in the local context. Often, they first let residents walk together, conduct interviews, measure, and organize data, only then slowly discovering what their community lacked. The curriculum thus became not just a delivery of answers, but a generator of questions.

The self-account of Yonghe Community College provides another concrete example. On the eve of New Taipei City’s upgrade to a special municipality in 2010, Yonghe Community College, along with Shuanghe professionals and NGOs, formed the Ecological Shuanghe Vision Alliance. They listed green transportation, green living, green space, and green rights as demands, placing school administration, community building, and civic participation on the same axis. This is a case study of a single school and should not be directly generalized to all community colleges, but it illustrates how curriculum might step out of the school gates. [^1]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** When a class begins measuring river water, consulting local gazetteers, and asking residents how they live, the school is no longer just a building “providing services,” but becomes a tool for the community to think together.

## What Huang Wu-shiong Truly Worried Was Not Empty Classrooms

In an article published in the Wenshan Community College journal, Huang Wu-shiong placed community colleges within the social context after the lifting of Martial Law in 1987. He argued that democratization brought freedom and space for association, but the problems left by long-term authoritarian rule would also emerge sequentially. For him, social learning and democratization must proceed together; otherwise, ballots will not automatically translate into public judgment. [^3]

This is a strongly held claim, not a historical conclusion that everyone must accept. Its value lies in pulling “education” back from the language of individual career advancement into civic life: How do people understand public issues, how do they discuss with people of different stances, and how do they organize action when no political figures speak for them?

In a 2006 academic paper, Cai Hung-cheng synthesized this理念 into “Liberating Knowledge, Catalyzing Civil Society.” The paper pointed out that the philosophical foundation of community colleges includes the distinction between “packaged knowledge” and “experiential knowledge,” further refining this distinction with tacit knowledge, and discussing the practical problems faced by community colleges as a cultural enlightenment movement. This is the analysis of a researcher, not a verbatim quote from Huang Wu-shiong. [^7]

This reminder is important. The story of community colleges cannot be written solely as a victory history of idealism. For curriculum to maintain its public nature, it requires long-term collaboration among teachers, administrators, students, and local organizations. The amount of resources, organizational division of labor, government commissioning methods, and school administration philosophies will all change what a community college can do.

A 2013 study focused on three community colleges that had received certification from the National Community College Promotion Association for “School Administration and Public Participation,” categorizing them into three models: professional division of labor, consciousness enlightenment, and NPO strategic alliance. The study also listed different methods such as administrative capacity building, curriculum guidance, club guidance, community building, and strategic alliances. [^8]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** The difficulty of community colleges is not “who will win between ideal and reality,” but that once ideals enter budgets, venues, and evaluations, they must be renegotiated every day.

## From Civilian Movement to Legal Text

The Executive Yuan’s policy explanation records that in the 87th year of the Republic of China (1998), the first community college was established in the Wenshan District of Taipei City. By the 106th year (2017), there were 86 community colleges nationwide, with student attendance reaching 400,000 person-times. These figures belong to policy data from around 2017, not the current situation in 2026. [^9]

The same explanation does not write institutionalization as a purely good news story. Community colleges faced problems such as fixed venues, insufficient funding, and the lack of a higher-level legal basis, leading the Ministry of Education to propose the draft _Community College Development Act_. The Legislative Yuan passed the act on third reading on May 18, 2018, addressing matters such as competent authorities,办学 venues, subsidies, evaluation, and learning certificates. [^9]

An English article from the Ministry of Education noted that this 17-article law was promulgated by the President on June 13, 2018, with one purpose being to give community colleges a clearer legal status and assist in addressing venue and operational funding issues. The Ministry also listed civic literacy, public affairs participation, local identity, local culture, and community sustainability as goals for community colleges. [^10]

The law makes it less likely for community colleges to be treated as temporary projects, but it brings another problem: When an educational movement originally explored by civilians enters the administrative system, how does it retain sensitivity to local issues? If evaluations only look at the number of courses, enrollment numbers, and execution rates, will the hardest-to-quantify dialogues, trust, and civic abilities be pushed to the last priority?

An English academic chapter included in Springer placed Taiwan’s community colleges within the context of civil society construction in the 1980s, arguing that adult education could become a pathway for people to participate in social movements and bring marginalized voices into the public sphere. The chapter also noted that after the establishment of the first community college in 1998, this model continued to evolve into a social force promoting civil society. [^11]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** Institutionalization is not the end of the story; it merely moves the question of “who decides what is learned and for whom” from the classroom into public policy.

## The Resonance Left by a Schedule

Looking back at Huang Wu-shiong and community colleges today, what is most worth remembering may not be the record of being “the first,” but his attempt to bind learning with public life. That attempt does not guarantee that every course will become a social movement, nor that every student will join a local organization.

It only poses an inconvenient question: Can education only serve to make people more suitable for the existing system? If students learn in the classroom to identify river pollution, trace the history of a market, understand a local policy, and then discuss with others whether to change it, these actions may not immediately form results, but they do change the relationship between people and their place.

Huang Wu-shiong wrote in an advocacy article: “Promoting community colleges means conducting social learning comprehensively.” [^3] The weight of this sentence lies not in its grand sounding nature, but in expanding the object of learning from textbooks to shared life. It also leaves community colleges facing an unavoidable standard: Does it truly enable students to better understand public issues and handle them together with others?

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** What community colleges leave behind is not a degree certificate, but a possibility—you can, after class, begin to treat your place of residence as a world that needs to be understood together.
