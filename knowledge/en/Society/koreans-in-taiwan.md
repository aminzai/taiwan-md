> **30-Second Overview:** The presence of Koreans in Taiwan did not suddenly appear after short videos. Kaohsiung Korean schools, local Korean associations, the seasonal work program, overseas students, and transnational families have long integrated Korean life into Taiwanese cities. In recent years, internet celebrities have made this community more visible, but they also remind us: being seen is never the whole story.

In 2013, a part-time class at a Korean school in Kaohsiung had only 20 students. The children attended primary school in Taiwan in the morning and returned to classes in Korean language, Korean culture, and _sihoule_ (a local activity) at one in the afternoon.[^5] In 2024, there were 2,418 Korean students studying in Taiwan.[^2] The same "Koreans in Taiwan" actually encompasses children from a small school, youth on exchange, families who married Taiwanese and settled in the south, and creators who explain Korea to Taiwanese audiences daily via mobile phones.

If one only looks at Koreans in Taiwan through YouTube, the easiest answer is bright and fast: they are _yusanimin_ (stomach acid people), gold needle mushrooms, and Meiling Hanmai—people who film everyday differences. But this answer is incomplete. The presence of Koreans in Taiwan did not suddenly appear after short videos. Kaohsiung Korean schools had been operating for 51 years as of reports from 2013, and local Korean associations played a mediating role in immigration agency rights advocacy and cultural activities.[^3] [^5]

## Four Entry Points, Four Types of Life in Taiwan

"Koreans in Taiwan" can be understood through at least four entry points. These are not mutually exclusive statuses; some people transition from students to workers, while some families navigate school, residency, and content creation simultaneously. The table below only organizes life paths directly supported by the sources, without reducing individual cases to represent all Koreans.

There is no natural ladder connecting these four paths. Official documents categorize people by visa, residency, or study purposes; life often causes different purposes to overlap. The article chooses to list them side-by-side to avoid "Koreans coming to Taiwan" being reduced to only one type of immigration story.

## One School, Two Calendars

The story of Kaohsiung Korean International School begins with its timetable. The report states that students attend regular Taiwanese primary school in the morning, studying with Taiwanese classmates, and then return to the Korean school from 1 p.m. to 5:30 p.m. to study Korean language, Korean culture, and _sihoule_. The school also holds classes on Saturdays, giving students who cannot attend during weekdays a chance to maintain contact with Korean language.[^5]

This is not about putting two cultures in separate rooms. Every day, the children switch between the rhythm of the Taiwanese school system and another set of languages and holidays. The curriculum even includes "Kimchi Day," where students actually make Korean food.[^5] A child switching between two school systems on the same day means that cross-culturalism is placed on a schedule they must remember themselves.

Behind the afternoon timetable lies real weight. Children must remember homework, class times, and languages from both sides, while parents have to reschedule between work, family, and transportation. The report does not portray this life as a romantic bilingual advantage; rather, it points out that bilingual and cross-cultural learning is a challenge for children, and parents are particularly concerned about the workload.[^5] "Preserving one's mother tongue" is not just a slogan; it requires a school, an afternoon slot, and a family willing to bear the cost of commuting and classes.

Principal Kim Jin Sub mentioned in the report that language is the key to connecting cultures. He also believes that working in a cross-cultural environment requires maintaining a positive attitude, open-mindedness, and accepting the uniqueness of local culture. These words sound like a principal's professional principles, but in the context of Kaohsiung's afternoon classes, they are more like a daily technique: first let the child live in Taiwan, then ensure the child does not lose their Korean language because of it.

The scale of this school also reminds us not to misread "Koreans in Taiwan" as one large and homogeneous group. The 20 students in the afternoon class in 2013 and the 2,418 Korean students studying in Taiwan in 2024 represent two entry points: family education and higher education. The former is concerned with how children preserve their mother tongue; the latter is concerned with degrees, research, and future work. These two numbers can be placed in the same table, but the lives behind them are two different arrangements.[^2] [^5]

## Kaohsiung Is Not a Coincidence

In 2016, the Southern Joint Service Center of the Executive Yuan reported on rights exchanges for foreign residents between the Immigration Agency and the Korean association in Kaohsiung. At that time, there were 402 resident Koreans in Kaohsiung City, ranking 4th among all counties and cities, and the Korean Association in Kaohsiung had 120 members.[^3]

This number is a historical snapshot from 2016 and cannot be taken as today's demographics. What it can show is that the life of Koreans in Kaohsiung involves restaurants, tourism, schools, local organizations, and administrative services. It is where these nodes intersect to form a visible network where people can find each other. The Korean Association helps connect resources, while the Immigration Agency explains residency and stay rights during activities.[^3]

Immigration systems usually classify people by documents. Life, however, leads people to finer issues: where children study their mother tongue in the afternoon, who parents ask about residency extensions, how the community holds a New Year's party, or whether they can find someone familiar when facing legal or administrative problems. The case of Kaohsiung makes "community" a visible institution, not just a nationality field in statistics.

The Immigration Agency of the Ministry of the Interior now publishes monthly statistics on foreign resident numbers and continuously updates downloadable data.[^6] This type of data is suitable for answering demographic questions like "how many foreigners reside here," but it cannot answer how they live in the city alone. To understand Koreans in Taiwan, one must put the statistical tables together with the experiences of schools, local associations, families, and work.

## Short-Term Life Starting with Seasonal Work

An official statement from the Ministry of Foreign Affairs, Consular Affairs Bureau, indicated that the Taiwan-Korea seasonal work visa was implemented on January 1, 2011. This system is primarily for youth long vacations, making work a secondary arrangement to cover living expenses; therefore, not every Korean youth who comes to Taiwan intends to settle. Applicants must be between 18 and 34 years old and can only come to Taiwan once under this plan.[^1]

The official statement also drew boundaries for the system. The main purpose of entry must be long vacation, with legal work being merely a secondary means to fund travel. Applicants are not allowed to bring spouses, dependents, or children.[^1] These conditions separate someone temporarily moved to Taiwan from a family living in Taiwan under reasons like dependency, study, or work.

However, for the youth, short-term systems can leave long memories. Seasonal workers must find housing themselves, buy transportation tickets, deal with language friction, and encounter Taiwanese accents and rhythms at their workplaces. The system writes "exchange" into the visa description; true exchange happens in a work schedule, a rental group chat, or an administrative window that is difficult to translate.

Therefore, Koreans in Taiwan should not be divided merely as "immigrants" or "tourists." Some come with degrees, some with families, some for one year, and some decide whether to stay after the term ends. The Ministry of the Interior's guidelines for permanent residency applications even list the phrase "Korean residents staying long-term in Taiwan approved by the Ministry of Foreign Affairs' project."[^7] This shows that there are different paths to residency status and reminds us not to write every Korean in Taiwan as the same story.

## Universities Open Two Directions

A 2025 report from the Ministry of Education provided another entry point. Hanyang University in Korea established sister schools with 34 universities in Taiwan, where 107 Taiwanese students were studying at Hanyang University at that time. The Ministry of Education also announced that in 2024, there were 2,418 Korean students studying in Taiwan, and 2,150 Taiwanese students studying in Korea.[^2]

There is a symmetry easily overlooked in these numbers: both the Korean students coming to Taiwan and the Taiwanese students going to Korea have people who temporarily treat the other society as their classroom. Student exchange does not necessarily lead to immigration, but it brings back the other's language, school system, and living habits to their home place.

The Ministry of Education placed cooperation in the same report across engineering, medicine, industry-academia collaboration, and talent cultivation.[^2] This is different from Taiwan-Korea exchanges that only discuss K-dramas or K-pop. When Korean students come to Taiwan for degrees, research, or internships, the field they face shifts from cultural consumption to laboratories, hospitals, campus administration, and peer relationships. The understanding between Taiwan and Korea slowly forms in these scenes that are not suitable for a thirty-second video clip.

Research on international students at National Chengchi University reminds us that cross-national study should be understood within the context of student mobility and educational systems, not just as foreign scenery on campus.[^8] For Korean students, Taiwan might be a research location, an environment for learning Chinese, an exchange experience, or a next step without an answer.

## Why Koreans on Screen Became Familiar

![Taipei sunset cityscape. Cross-national students must also learn to read the rhythm of a city outside the classroom.](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/37/Taipei_-_Skyline_Sunset.jpg)

_Image: Jorge Cancela, 〈Taipei - Skyline Sunset〉, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY 2.0. Original file and license page see [^13]._

The 2022 report by _Taiwan Banker_ turned the lens onto _yusanimin_, gold needle mushrooms, and Meiling Hanmai. The article mentioned that Yusanimin graduated from the Department of Economics at National Taiwan University and became a Taiwanese citizen, while Gold Needle Mushroom graduated from the Chinese Department at National Taiwan University.[^4] Their fame made Koreans in Taiwan transition from a minority presence in the city to content that Taiwanese audiences might actively click on daily.

The turning point here is not just "Koreans starting as YouTubers." The report mentioned that Yusanimin once reflected on the unfriendly atmosphere caused by diplomatic ties between Korea and Taiwan and international sporting competition, and believed that Taiwanese attitudes toward Koreans changed significantly after the rise of Hallyu (Korean Wave).[^4] An individual's visibility in Taiwan becomes intertwined with bilateral relations, popular culture, and platform algorithms.

The report described two types of daily cultural collisions at the moment a person first arrived in Taiwan. At that time, the interviewed Korean still habitually greeted teachers by standing up and bowing, but saw Taiwanese students greeting teachers with high-fives or even eating fried chicken in class.[^4] These details cannot be used to draw conclusions about all Koreans or all Taiwanese, but they give action to cultural differences rather than a string of adjectives like "Korea is stricter, Taiwan is freer."

Meiling Hanmai's experience also brought the focus to families in Tainan. The report pointed out that before marrying her Taiwanese husband, she had not studied or worked in Taiwan; after marriage, she entered a large family in Southern Taiwan.[^4] Her background spanned Chinese Korean, Chinese schools, work in Seoul, and Taiwanese marriage.[^4]

This story cannot be summarized merely as "Korean wife integrating into Taiwan." Before arriving in Tainan, she already had experience with Chinese education and a transnational family history. Post-marriage life added fruit, hot weather, scooter traffic, parenting, and group buying.[^4] Foreigners coming to Taiwan often do not start from a blank slate but bring prior experiences of language, family, and mobility.

## The End of Comparison: Who Can Adjust?

The interviews in _Taiwan Banker_ placed differences in concrete life situations: interviewees discussed Korean requirements regarding age, seniority, and honorifics, as well as how Taiwanese workplaces sometimes use English names or where supervisors and employees occasionally joke with each other.[^4] These descriptions are the observations of the interviewees, not the statistical average of every person in two countries, so they can only be read within their lived context.

The interviewees also talked about the fruit, hot weather, and scooter traffic in Southern Taiwan. For those familiar with Seoul's public transportation, scooters bring mobility but also risk.[^4] Adapting to a city relies on daily choices: whether or not to ride a motorbike, how to cross the street, and how to buy things at the market. These repeated small decisions gradually form a feeling for a city.

Studies on Taiwanese perceptions of Koreans and Taiwan-Korea relations focus on Taiwanese society by analyzing different demographic variables against perceptions of Korea.[^9] The value of such research is that it reminds us that the lives of Koreans in Taiwan are also influenced by existing impressions held by Taiwanese people. Some first see Hallyu, some first think of historical competition, and some treat the other as a colleague, classmate, or neighbor.

Therefore, Taiwan's "inclusiveness" toward Koreans cannot be limited to self-praise. It is seen in whether schools can accommodate bilingual children, whether administrative windows can clearly explain residency rules, whether workplaces allow foreign professionals to work, and whether a Korean person, after being recognized as having nationality, can still be understood through their complex experiences.

## Placing Korea Back into the Fabric of Taiwan's Daily Life

Returning to the school in Kaohsiung, children study Korean in the afternoon and attend classes with Taiwanese classmates in the morning. Returning to the family in Tainan, Korean language, Chinese, Chinese Korean background, and Taiwanese kinship coexist. Returning to university, 2,418 Korean students came to Taiwan for study in 2024, while 2,150 Taiwanese students went to Korea.[^2] On mobile screens, several creators from Taiwan who are Korean package politeness, weather, politics, and fruit into stories that Taiwanese audiences can understand.[^4]

These entry points are different, yet they collectively change the imagination of "Koreans in Taiwan." This theme cannot be confined only to news about foreigners coming to Taiwan, nor can it be limited to the Hallyu consumption market. What is more worth looking at is how a community preserves its language and then rearranges life in local schools, Taiwanese universities, families, and workplaces.

Administrative systems will continue to classify by nationality, visa, and residency purposes. Cultural activities will continue to recognize Korean holidays, kimchi, music, and festivals. Platforms will continue to put the lives of certain people before more Taiwanese audiences. What truly needs attention is that these three systems can only illuminate a part.

If asked what Koreans have left in Taiwan, the answer may not be fixed "Korean-ness," but rather an ability to translate repeatedly: translating between two timetables, translating between different levels of honorifics, translating between family and administrative documents, and translating oneself before and after the camera. What Taiwan sees is Korean people; and Koreans are also learning to explain Taiwan on this island.
