> **30-Second Summary**: On August 15, 2017, a natural gas supply interruption caused rolling blackouts affecting 17 counties and cities and 6.68 million users; on March 3, 2022, operational errors at the Xindu power plant affected over 5.49 million households. Neither incident was simply an issue of "insufficient generation," but rather how a single equipment failure, procedure error, or hub malfunction could escalate into a national event. Taipower responded by proposing a decade-long grid resilience plan with an investment of NT$564.5 billion, shifting from a centralized grid toward one that is decentralized, robust, and defensive. But the real question remains: when a power outage occurs, who—the hospital, the community, the remote area, or the city—can restore life first?

# Taiwan's Power Problem Is Not Just About Generation Capacity

Power outages are most easily understood as a supply-demand issue: is there enough electricity, is the reserve capacity high enough, and which energy source should be increased? But when a light goes out, what disappears is not just illumination. Elevators stop, trapping people in high-rise buildings between floors; base stations and network equipment require backup power, or families cannot communicate; hospitals must rely on generators to maintain surgeries, ventilators, and refrigerated medicines; cold chains, water pumping, traffic signals, and continuous industrial processes all have to decide how to sustain themselves in a short time.

Therefore, grid resilience is not about "never having an incident," but about the system's ability to limit the scope of failure, maintain critical services, quickly locate the problem, and restore supply after an accident. Taipower's definition of resilience also includes the capacity to respond to accidents and stabilize operations in a short period.[^1]

This definition shifts the problem from "whether there is enough power" to four more difficult questions: Is the power concentrated in a few large nodes? Can failures be isolated? Does the local area have its own backup? Who determines the order of repair? If only generation capacity is increased without addressing transmission lines, substations, protective relays, operating procedures, and local distribution, the city can still lose widespread power from what appears to be a localized incident.

# 815: How One Valve Caused Half of Taiwan to Blackout

The August 15, 2017 (815) major power outage was a turning point where Taiwanese society widely recognized grid resilience issues. The Executive Yuan investigation pointed out that the sudden interruption of natural gas supply to the Datang power plant occurred because an external vendor from CPC used temporary splicing wires while replacing the gas meter station's power supply; after removal, it caused a disconnection of controller power and communication, leading to the controller restarting and automatically closing the electric valve, ultimately causing a complete gas supply cutoff.[^2]

The core of the incident cannot be explained by simply saying "someone pressed the wrong button." The investigation also pointed out that shared controllers for critical electric valves, insufficient diversification of Datang's gas supply, inadequate management changes, and risk assessment meant that a localized maintenance action had consequences far beyond the site scale. After the natural gas supply was interrupted, the units shut down, system power capacity dropped sharply, leading to rolling blackouts across 17 counties and cities affecting 6.68 million users.[^3]

The Center for Environmental Information noted that the impact of the 815 incident was not just the blackout itself, but also reignited public debates regarding energy transition, gas dependency, grid stability, and backup capacity.[^3] This debate is often simplified into supporting or opposing a certain type of energy, but another lesson provided by 815 is: regardless of whether power comes from gas, coal, nuclear, or renewable energy, if key supply, control, and transmission/distribution systems lack multi-layer protection, a single error can still be amplified.

The Executive Yuan investigation therefore proposed directions such as implementing multi-layer protection, increasing fuel supply margins, strengthening the disaster resilience of gas supply and power supply systems, and improving independent power for public transportation and life support systems.[^2] These suggestions brought "energy security" out from inside the power plant fence to the entire city: critical services cannot just wait for the main grid to recover; they must also have their own safety boundaries.

# 303: More Than Just an Operational Error

The March 3, 2022 (303) power outage incident made "procedures" and "organizational culture" central topics in grid discussions. The Control Yuan investigation pointed out that during major maintenance of Unit 2 at the Xindu power plant, testing was conducted before the circuit breaker insulation gas had fully recharged, violating isolation switch and auxiliary card procedures, which caused a flashover grounding fault on the 345kV busbar, subsequently triggering system splitting and widespread blackouts. The incident occurred that morning, with full power restored at 9:31 PM, affecting over 5.49 million households.[^4]

The Ministry of Economic Affairs' review report also indicated that a single operational error could cause a major blackout due to insufficient multi-layer protection and the risk associated with the Southern grid being concentrated at the Longqi ultra-high voltage substation. Improvement directions included establishing dedicated risk units, promoting decentralized grids, reviewing protective relays and switchgear systems, and strengthening professional training and external expert diagnosis.[^5]

Here is a systemic issue that is often overlooked: if every unit only handles its local tasks without anyone seeing "how one operational error crosses multiple defense lines," the system builds safety on the assumption that no one will ever make a mistake. True resilience, conversely, assumes that people will make mistakes, equipment will fail, and weather will worsen; therefore, errors must have an opportunity to be discovered and isolated, rather than penetrating the entire system directly.

After 303, Taiwanese society's expectations of power outages became more precise. The public not only asked "why did it go out," but also "why does one power plant's accident affect so many people," "why wasn't the same error prevented at a previous layer," and "why was the restoration sequence and information disclosure unclear." These questions are essentially demanding that the grid be a comprehensible, governable public system.

# Moving from Centralized Efficiency to Decentralized Resilience

Taiwan's past grid structure has its historical reasons. Taiwan is geographically narrow with high population density; finding land for power plants is difficult, and electricity demand is concentrated in urban and industrial areas, leading the grid to gradually form a centralized structure connecting large power plants, north-south transmission lines, and a few hub substations.[^1]

Centralization can bring efficiency and allow different regions to support each other; the problem is that when hubs become bottlenecks, efficiency can turn into a common vulnerability. Both the Executive Yuan and Taipower's plans following the 303 incident listed "reducing centralized risk" as a core objective. The grid resilience construction plan announced in 2022 planned an investment of NT$564.5 billion over ten years, with three main axes focusing on decentralization, continuous robustness, and enhanced defense.[^1]

Decentralization is not just about building more power plants. The plan includes direct supply from power plants to science parks and industrial parks, distributed green energy supply, increasing delivery nodes, grouping hub substations, expanding energy storage, internalizing substation functions, and real-time dynamic defense.[^1] Its logic is to bring power closer to the consumption center, making fault points easier to isolate, so that an entire region does not have to wait when a main transmission line or a specific substation has an accident.

Taipower stated that the grid resilience plan will construct 28 substations and promote some internalizing of substation functions; simultaneously, through green energy storage, distributed energy is not just a power source but also a tool for regional supply stability.[^6] This direction is intertwined with energy transition: while renewable energy has decentralized potential, wind and sunlight are variable. Without storage, dispatching, and distribution capabilities, distributed power does not automatically equal decentralized resilience.

# The Distribution Grid: Where Outages Most Frequently Occur

While national blackouts are the most noticeable, they are not the only form of power outage residents encounter daily. Data compiled by the Office of Economic Advisors of the Executive Yuan from Taipower indicates that the number of distribution accidents leading to outages decreased from 21,019 times in 2012 to 6,115 times in 2023, a drop of 70% over ten years.[^7]

These figures show that grid resilience also occurs on streets, utility poles, transformers, and feeders. There are over 10,000 feeders, about 410,000 kilometers of lines, 1.55 million switches, 1.48 million transformers, and 3.2 million utility poles across Taiwan; over half of the outages are related to external forces and environmental factors such as animals, trees, lightning strikes, salt damage, construction, vehicles, or user equipment.[^7]

Therefore, resilience is not just a matter of ultra-high voltage substations. Feeder automation can detect and isolate fault zones during an accident to restore normal lines first; undergrounding disaster-resistant lines can reduce outages caused by trees, strong winds, and foreign objects.[^7] But engineering also involves choices: what level of priority should be given to densely populated or critical medical/transportation areas? Do remote line areas with long distances require a different evaluation method? Undergrounding all lines is not necessarily the only answer; the key is to allocate limited resources where they can best reduce public risk.

# Microgrids: Bringing "Recovery" Back to the Local Level

The main grid remains the primary foundation of life in Taiwan, but during a disaster, whether the local area can temporarily operate independently from the main grid may determine if shelters, communication, and basic lighting can be maintained first. A Taipower monthly report mentioned that disaster-resilient microgrids capable of islanded operation have been established in areas like Wulai Fushan, Alishan, mountainous regions of Pingtung, and outlying islands, integrating solar power, energy storage, and energy management systems.[^8]

The "islanded operation" of a microgrid is not about making an entire township permanently self-sufficient; it is about disconnecting the local power system when the external grid is damaged to prioritize maintaining shelters, communication, lighting, and necessary equipment. This capability is particularly important for remote areas, mountainous regions, and outlying islands because repair personnel and equipment take longer to reach if main lines or roads are damaged.

Taipower has also collaborated with universities to promote microgrids, bringing technology into education, demonstration, and community support.[^8] However, a microgrid is not complete just by installing solar panels and batteries. It requires someone to be responsible for startup, defining which equipment is prioritized, how long the fuel and battery can last, how to operate when communication is lost, and how to safely re-connect with the main grid after a disaster.

These issues make the microgrid both an engineering and a governance challenge. If there is only equipment without local maintainers, it may fail to start during an accident; if only a few organizations know how to operate it, the community cannot treat it as a shared emergency infrastructure. True local resilience must bring together technology, training, responsibility, and drills.

# Resilience Must Be Measurable, Not Just Declared

Grid investment is often presented in terms of engineering quantities, such as how many substations are added, how many lines are undergrounded, or how much energy storage equipment is installed; but engineering quantity does not equal the safety of residents during an accident. A more complete evaluation should place "the probability of failure," "the scope of failure propagation," "the duration critical services can be sustained," and "whether information can be understood" into a single set of indicators.[^1][^7]

| Resilience Aspect | Observable Indicators                                                                                                   | Governance Questions That Must Be Answered                                                              |
| :---------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Prevention        | Equipment failure rate, outages from tree/lightning strikes, backup level of critical nodes                             | Which risks warrant priority investment, and who is responsible for regularly updating the risk list?   |
| Isolation         | Fault localization time, feeder automation coverage, zoned supply capability                                            | Can local failures be limited to the smallest scope, or will they affect the entire area?               |
| Sustaining        | Backup hours for hospitals, water supply, communication, and shelters                                                   | Which services must operate before the main grid recovers, and what is the minimum standard?            |
| Recovery          | Average restoration time, disparity in recovery between remote areas and outlying islands, information update frequency | Is the restoration sequence transparent, and are vulnerable residents receiving substantive assistance? |
| Learning          | Public disclosure of accident reports, drill frequency, completion rate of improvements                                 | Has the lesson from one outage truly changed the procedure and budget for the next?                     |

Such indicators also prevent "averages masking differences." A decrease in national average power outage time does not mean every community is equally safe; a large hospital having a generator does not mean surrounding long-term care facilities, elevator buildings, or small businesses can last for the same amount of time. The goal of resilience governance should be to address the gaps that are most likely to cause life hazards and public service interruptions first, rather than just pursuing a beautiful overall average.

# From Equipment Resilience to Institutional Resilience

Hardware upgrades in the grid must occur alongside institutional observability. Strengthening substations, undergrounding lines, and building energy storage can reduce failure probability, but they cannot alone answer three governance questions after an accident: Who has the authority to decide on priority restoration? Who must disclose accident information? Who is responsible for turning experience into procedures and rules for the next time?

The common lesson from 815 and 303 is that accidents often cross multiple organizational boundaries. Gas suppliers, power plant operators, transmission dispatchers, local governments, and public service organizations may each complete their local tasks, but no single unit sees the cross-system risk in advance. Therefore, resilience requires cross-agency situational awareness, clear escalation reporting, traceable change management, and an investigation method that checks institutional defenses—not just individual accountability—after an accident.

For the public, transparency is not complete with publishing a technical report. During a power outage, public information should at least state the affected area, the confirmed degree of the current cause, the time of the next update, locations where water and charging can be obtained, and the assistance available to the elderly, people with disabilities, medical dependents, and residents in remote areas. If information is only circulated in professional jargon, even if power is restored quickly, society may still lose trust due to uncertainty.

# Grid Resilience Also Becomes a Social Distribution Issue

The impact of a power outage is never equal. The way residents in buildings with elevators experience an outage differs from those in old apartments without elevators; people who work from home, those needing refrigerated medicine, those relying on electric medical equipment, and small shops running cold chains do not have the same backup capacity. Large corporations may have generators and uninterruptible power supplies, but small businesses and low-income families can only wait for restoration.

Therefore, grid resilience plans cannot be measured solely by "average outage time" or "national restoration ratio." They must also ask: Which critical public services have independent power? Which communities have accessible shelters? Which remote areas and outlying islands have microgrids or backup systems? Is the power outage information disseminated in a way that everyone can understand? Is the restoration sequence transparent?

The Executive Yuan's investigation into the 815 incident has listed the independent power of public transportation and life support systems as an area for improvement.[^2] This means the grid is not just the service quality of a single industry, but a common condition for healthcare, water supply, communication, transportation, and public safety. When a power outage occurs during extreme heat, typhoons, or disasters, the lack of electricity can quickly turn into health risks and social inequality.

# A Island's Energy Transition Must Be a Backup Transition

Taiwan's energy transition often debates between types of generation: gas, coal, nuclear, and renewable energy each have their own costs, risks, and political controversies. But grid resilience demands another perspective: energy choices must be planned together with the transmission/distribution structure, energy storage, demand management, fuel supply, and local backup.

When renewables are combined with storage and microgrids, they are not just a power source but can also become a local safety boundary during a disaster. This transition requires new maintenance capabilities: locals need to know which equipment is usable during an outage; managers must be able to start islanded operation; utility providers must be able to safely re-connect; and the government must incorporate these capabilities into disaster drills, rather than waiting for an accident to confirm if the equipment truly works.

The Global Taiwan Institute's analysis pointed out that the 2017, 2021, and 2022 power outages exposed Taiwan's economic structure's high reliance on electricity, and also indicated that the core of the NT$564.5 billion plan proposed in 2022 was to reduce vulnerable bottlenecks by decreasing the possibility of widespread blackouts caused by a single node through decentralization and microgrids.[^9]

The Center for Environmental Information reminded readers when reviewing 815 that the power outage incident is also linked to gas dependency, grid stability, reserve capacity, and social trust in energy transition.[^3] If energy transition only adds new generation sources without simultaneously explaining how power is transmitted, stored, or how critical services are maintained during an accident, the public may still feel like they have "chosen an energy source but haven't solved the power outage problem."

A more mature energy governance should plan both "how much power" and "where to restore first." Renewable energy, storage, demand response, direct supply from power plants, regional microgrids, and distribution automation are not mutually exclusive single answers, but a combination of tools to reduce different risks.

Energy transition also requires new public communication methods. The government should not only announce installed capacity and construction costs but also explain how the new equipment operates during cloudy days, typhoons, gas supply interruptions, or communication failures; local residents need to know the safety distance of facilities, maintenance responsibilities, land use impact, and contact points during an accident. Only when technical information can be translated into a comprehensible living context can energy policy move from abstract supply-demand debate to risk management that can be jointly supervised.

# What Must The City Restore After the Power Outage?

The final question of grid resilience is not technical, but about sequencing. After an accident, not all areas can restore power simultaneously. The restoration sequence for hospitals, water supply, communication, transportation, shelters, cold chains, and residences must be discussed in advance, not decided arbitrarily in the dark. The 815 investigation also required reviewing warning notifications for zoned rolling blackouts, tracking affected feeders and user numbers, and gradually enabling public transportation, security, and life support systems to establish independent power circuits.[^2] These requirements remind us that restoration is not just a matter of "how fast," but also an issue of information justice—whether the government can let people know "who restores first and who is still waiting."

If the restoration sequence for critical services is not publicly disclosed in advance, during a power outage, support is easily given to those with the most resources, the loudest voices, or those closest to the decision-making center. Conversely, if local governments and utility providers survey medical facilities, water supply, communication, shelters, and elderly care points in advance, and incorporate backup capacity and restoration priority into drills, resilience will not just remain a list of equipment but become a public commitment that can be socially audited.

This also requires the government, Taipower, local governments, medical institutions, communities, and industries to establish a joint recovery plan. During a large-scale power outage, what residents need is not just a "repairing" message, but also knowing how long it might last, where they can get water and charge, which roads and transportation facilities are still usable, and who can receive priority assistance.

Taipower's shift from a centralized structure to one that is decentralized, robust, and defensive in recent years, along with the downward trend in distribution accidents, shows that long-term engineering efforts can indeed improve power quality.[^1][^7] However, the lessons left by 815 and 303 are not outdated: any system lacking alternative paths, error isolation, and public review can turn a local problem into a crisis for an entire city.

True grid resilience is not guaranteeing that there will never be a power outage; it is ensuring that a power outage does not mean life comes to a complete stop. It should ensure hospitals have power, remote areas have basic support, communities know how to care for the vulnerable, and the government can clearly explain who restores first, why they restore first, and how to prevent the same mistakes next time.

When Taiwan invests NT$564.5 billion over ten years to redesign its grid, the most important outcome should not just be a new line or a new substation, but rather that after an accident occurs, more places retain some light, some communication, and some order until the entire city is reconnected to power.
