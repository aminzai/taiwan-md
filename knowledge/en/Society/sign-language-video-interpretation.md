> **30-Second Overview**: For most people, making a phone call is a routine action; for some hearing-impaired individuals, the phone can isolate them from hospitals, banks, ride services, police reporting, government offices, and workplaces. On August 1, 2024, Taiwan launched a nationwide Video Relay Service (VRS), with the Ministry of Digital Affairs responsible for technical development and the Ministry of Health and Welfare managing service operations, allowing users to connect with an interpreter via sign language or text, who then communicates by voice with the hearing person. [^1] This is an important public service innovation; subsequent policy documents from the Ministry of Health and Welfare state that VRS is a cross-ministerial arrangement approved by the Executive Yuan, developed by the Ministry of Digital Affairs, and operated by the Ministry of Health and Welfare. [^10] However, what it truly aims to solve is not "whether a platform exists," but whether hearing-impaired individuals can be understood and responded to—without constantly having to prove their worthiness for assistance—at every moment they need to make a decision, access services, or handle a crisis. [^2]

## Why a Phone Call Becomes a Public Issue

Telephone design presumes that the user can hear the ringtone, understand spoken language, respond immediately, and confirm identity even if the other party cannot see them. This assumption does not hold for hearing-impaired individuals. Even if a person can speak, it does not mean they can clearly comprehend the rapid explanations from customer service representatives, medical staff, or government officials. Even if they can read and write, it does not guarantee that every agency provides real-time text, email, or online application alternatives.

Therefore, information equity cannot merely be understood as "whether a website has subtitles." It must encompass at least four capabilities: first, **information acquisition**, including announcements, news, health education, and emergency alerts; second, **expressing needs**, including reporting incidents, registration, service cancellation, complaints, or account inquiries; third, **completing interaction**, including identity verification, follow-up questions, confirmation, and record-keeping; and fourth, **taking action under time pressure**, such as during disasters, accidents, medical treatment, or judicial proceedings. Providing only static text may improve the first layer but does not necessarily complete the subsequent three layers.

Educational materials on information equity for the hearing impaired collected by the National Library point out that what hearing-impaired individuals need is not just isolated subtitles or sign language, but clear, clean, and non-obstructed visual information. In major events and emergencies, sign language interpretation, subtitling, and screen composition all affect comprehension. [^3] This perspective shifts the issue from "is there an interpreter?" to "can the message be reliably understood at the right time."

## From Welfare Services to Language Rights

Sign language interpretation and real-time captioning in Taiwan did not appear in 2024. Local governments have long provided communication assistance for public affairs, medical appointments, schooling, judicial/police matters, social participation, and emergencies through social welfare programs. Taking the Taipei hearing-impaired communication service program as an example, sign language interpretation services are marked as 24 hours, while real-time captioning is generally available from 08:00 to 22:00. Applicants can apply via phone, SMS, fax, email, messaging software, or online systems, generally three days before the service begins, with no such requirement in emergencies. [^4]

The value of these local programs lies in their recognition that communication is not a private individual issue but a reasonable accommodation required when public agencies provide services. However, local programs also have clear limitations: service windows, application rules, available hours, interpreter staffing, and budgets can vary by city or county. When hearing-impaired individuals travel across counties for medical treatment, schooling, work, or leisure, the familiar process may not continue seamlessly.

The _Act on the Protection of Rights of Persons with Disabilities_ and the CRPD provide a higher level of rights framework. The Ministry of Health and Welfare's regulatory page lists the _Act on the Protection of Rights of Persons with Disabilities_ as an important law ensuring equal participation for persons with disabilities, providing Chinese and English legal documents. [^5] The Social Welfare Agency’s sign language video translation center directly cites Article 9 of the CRPD, placing information and communication technology, public services, and equal use within disability obligations. It also points out that national laws require competent authorities to provide accessible public information services. [^6]

This shift is significant. If sign language interpretation is viewed only as a social welfare subsidy, the service may be relegated to "provided if there is a budget." If it is understood as a right to information and communication, the government must ask: which critical services cannot rely solely on self-application by the user? Which areas require proactive preparation? Who is responsible for ensuring translation quality? Who must explain and remedy errors when they occur?

## What VRS Does and What It Doesn't Do

The Ministry of Digital Affairs and the Ministry of Health and Welfare launched VRS in 2024, announcing the platform went live on August 1 of that year. The official explanation was that this is a cross-ministerial service involving public-private cooperation: the Ministry of Digital Affairs handles software technology development, the Ministry of Health and Welfare manages platform operation, and they expect it to serve hearing-impaired individuals nationwide. [^1] The basic process of VRS is for the user to log in via mobile phone, tablet, or computer, select sign language video or text communication with an interpreter. The interpreter then communicates by phone or related tools with the hearing person, conveying content between both parties. [^6]

The institutional breakthrough of VRS is partially transforming the problem of "the other party not providing a textual channel" into a real-time supporting service. Hearing-impaired individuals do not have to find a relative to make the call for them, nor do they have to entrust personal medical, financial, or family matters to a family member to explain. This has direct implications for self-reliance and privacy.

However, VRS does not eliminate all barriers. First, it requires equipment, internet, cameras, login capability, and a certain level of digital familiarity. For the elderly, low-income households, users in remote areas, or those uncomfortable with video, there may still be a digital barrier to the service. Second, the service hours of VRS do not necessarily overlap with the business hours of every agency. The Social Welfare Agency's public page currently lists translation services as Monday to Saturday from 09:00 to 19:00, excluding national holidays. This still requires a clearer handover mechanism for 24-hour needs such as medical emergencies, disasters, nighttime transportation, or financial loss reporting. [^6]

Third, the interpreter can only translate; they cannot make decisions for the agency, nor can they guarantee that the other party will respond in a friendly, clear, and reasonable manner. If bank customer service is unfamiliar with hearing-impaired clients, if hospitals lack visual queuing systems, or if police procedures do not include simultaneous recording, VRS may just deliver the deaf person to another unfriendly institutional gate.

## Service Quality Is Not Just "Connection"

Public services often evaluate digital platforms using connection rates, waiting times, or service volume, but information equity for hearing-impaired individuals requires more indicators. These can be divided into four categories:

| Evaluation Aspect | Insufficient Single Indicator | Questions to Track                                                                                                      |
| :---------------- | :---------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Accessibility     | Does a platform exist?        | Can users in remote areas, with low bandwidth, or elderly persons log in and operate it?                                |
| Comprehensibility | Is there an interpreter?      | Is the conversion from sign language, text, and spoken language complete? Are specialized terms accurate?               |
| Completeness      | Is there a connection?        | Does the user successfully complete registration, complaint filing, payment, incident reporting, or other actual tasks? |
| Accountability    | Is there a record?            | If misinterpretation, omission, or service refusal occurs, can it be tracked and remedied?                              |

Educational materials on information equity for the hearing impaired collected by the National Library concretize the issues faced by hearing-impaired individuals in television and public messages: subtitles may be incomplete, sign language visuals may be too small or obscured by other visual elements, and policies may "exist" without being implemented. [^3] This observation also applies to VRS. Launching a platform does not mean every agency that takes calls is prepared. The existence of interpretation services does not mean all professional terminology and procedures can be correctly translated.

Quality also involves language and culture. Taiwanese sign language is not a simple encoding where Mandarin is word-for-word converted into gestures. Different users may prefer sign language, spoken language, lip-reading, real-time captioning, or a mixed method. If policies only categorize individuals as "hearing impaired," they easily overlook the differences between Deaf culture, late-deafened individuals, elderly hearing-impaired persons, and those with multiple disabilities. Good service should allow users to choose their communication method, not require the user to adapt to the government's most convenient method.

## Emergency Information: The Most Institutionalized Moment

When registering or inquiring about bills in normal times, users might be able to wait, use family assistance, or handle it another day. But earthquakes, typhoons, epidemics, fires, and public safety incidents cannot be remedied by individual patience. CRPD-related educational materials clearly list the timely provision of information during disaster situations as an important right, stating that sign language, subtitles, and visual messages should appear together in public broadcasting. [^3]

Therefore, emergency information should not just be a small sign language box temporarily placed in the bottom right corner of a news screen. The government needs to establish cross-platform standards: can alert SMS messages be fully presented in text? Do local government livestreams provide simultaneous subtitles and sign language? Are there visual processes for evacuation centers, medical stations, and transportation disruptions? If VRS does not provide service at night or on national holidays, how are emergency reporting, medical, and disaster windows handled?

The minutes from the 2025 meeting of the Executive Yuan's Working Group on Disability Rights show that the government has discussed requiring various ministries to provide sign language interpretation, real-time captioning, or AI subtitles as needed in their self-managed or contracted activities, and demanding that costs be included in budget planning. The same minutes also point out that VRS should not concentrate all specialized services into a single window; each ministry should gradually incorporate sign language interpretation according to its responsibilities. [^7] This resolution reveals a key principle: VRS is common infrastructure, but it cannot be an excuse for ministries to evade their own accessibility responsibilities.

## Moving from a Single Platform to Cross-Agency Responsibility

For sign language video translation to truly become public infrastructure, at least four institutional adjustments are needed.

First is **embedding service responsibility into agency processes**. Medical, financial, police, labor, education, and transportation units should each establish standard operating procedures for using VRS, sign language interpretation, and real-time captioning, detailing identity verification, sensitive data handling, record retention, translation error protocols, and complaint remedies. It cannot be that the hearing-impaired person has to explain "what I need" on the spot every time.

Second is **establishing quality indicators centered on user tasks**. The government should not only announce service volume but also know if a user successfully completed a task, how long they waited, which agencies most frequently rejected them, and which terms or procedures are most prone to error. Data disclosure must adhere to de-identification and minimum necessity principles; personal privacy risks cannot be increased merely for service evaluation.

Third is **keeping non-digital alternatives available**. VRS is an important tool, but it should not weaken paper documents, SMS, email, in-person interpretation, or real-time captioning simply because "an app exists." True digital inclusion is not forcing everyone onto a single platform; it is providing multiple interconnected entry points.

Fourth is **involving hearing-impaired individuals in co-design and oversight**. The third national report on the CRPD released by the Social Welfare Agency pointed out that actions from 2020 to 2024 were tracked by the Executive Yuan's Working Group on Disability Rights, emphasizing social integration through accessible formats and public-private cooperation. The report also mentioned various aspects such as service coverage, family support, and readable information. [^8] This tracking cannot only be done by agencies reporting completion rates; actual users must participate in testing, reviewing subtitles, checking service hours, and judging the standard for "task completion."

## This Is Not Just Changing Sound into Gestures

The core of public information accessibility is not mechanically translating a piece of audio into sign language, but enabling everyone to acquire information, express intentions, make decisions, and fulfill life responsibilities under equal conditions. VRS provided Taiwan with a national, cross-ministerial communication equity platform for the first time, but the platform itself is only the starting point.

When media reports on important issues concerning the Deaf and hearing communities in 2024, they also regard the official launch of VRS as one of the annual institutional changes. This reflects that it has indeed responded to long-standing daily communication needs. [^9] However, the true measure should not be whether the press conference was successful, but whether a person can have their medical appointment canceled at night, confirm evacuation information during a disaster, handle banking matters, or report an incident at the police station without needing a family member as a spokesperson—and still be fully understood.

Taiwan has moved from "whether accessible services exist" to "whether services are truly usable." The next step is not adding another beautiful feature, but ensuring that every public agency takes responsibility for its own information equity, allowing VRS, sign language interpretation, real-time captioning, subtitles, readable information, and in-person services to connect. Only when hearing-impaired individuals can handle their lives directly like others will information equity be more than a promise in policy documents; it will become a practical experience in daily institutions.

## Service Boundaries and Data Governance

VRS involves not just communication but also personal data and sensitive situations. When users seek help in medical, financial, domestic violence, judicial, or labor disputes, the translated content may contain highly private information. If the platform stores call records, service times, agency names, or user identification data, it must clearly state the purpose of storage, retention period, access rights, and complaint channels. If communication content that is identifiable is stored long-term merely for usage statistics, a service originally designed for autonomy may inadvertently create new surveillance risks.

This also involves the division of labor between human translation and AI tools. Real-time AI subtitling can reduce waiting time and supplement some speech-to-text needs, but in situations involving dialects, specialized terminology, multiple speakers simultaneously, background noise, emotional shifts, or semantic turns, errors can directly affect rights. If the government adopts AI, it should be positioned as an aid, not a means to shift responsibility for errors onto the user. For high-risk matters such as medical consent, judicial statements, financial authorization, and emergency reporting, mechanisms for human verification, restatement, and correction must be retained. The Executive Yuan's Working Group on Disability Rights has discussed the application of AI real-time subtitling and sign language interpretation in meeting materials, but "whether it can be used" and "what situations cannot rely solely on AI" must be regulated separately. [^7]

Another overlooked issue is the human resources supplying the service. Sign language interpreters, real-time captioners, and staff familiar with hearing-impaired communication all require training, reasonable workload, and professional support. If policies only pursue extending service hours without simultaneously increasing manpower and professional specialization, the result may be longer waiting times, interpreter fatigue, declining quality, ultimately forcing the user to bear the cost of institutional expansion. The application deadlines, emergency cases, and cross-county coordination issues presented by local services demonstrate that there is still a gap between "service exists" and "service is available at all times." [^4]

Taiwan can establish a minimum standard for inter-agency service: general public information must have readable text and subtitles. High-risk services must offer human sign language or real-time captioning options. Emergency events must have after-hours and holiday alternatives. Every agency must publish responsible contacts and complaint procedures. The platform should periodically disclose de-identified waiting times, task completion rates, cancellation rates, and user feedback. These standards should not only be set by administrative agencies but also tested collaboratively by hearing-impaired individuals, interpreters, captioners, medical professionals, and legal experts.

If VRS is viewed as a standalone digital product, the policy may end with download numbers, call volumes, and promotional videos. If it is seen as public infrastructure for information equity, the focus of evaluation shifts to whether it can reduce reliance on proxies, whether important life tasks can be completed, whether reliable information can be obtained in a crisis, and who is responsible when errors occur. This is the gap that Taiwan must continuously track as it moves from welfare services toward rights governance.
