> **30-second overview:** In 2020, lesbian couple Mota and City traveled to Cambodia for IVF, spending about NT$2 million over four years. Taiwan's medical technology had long been mature, but the law for a long time only allowed heterosexual married couples to use it. From the 2007 Artificial Reproduction Act to the 2025 draft amendments, this article is not about whether one "can give birth," but about who can enter the clinic, who is recognized as the parent after the child is born, and the issues left behind by surrogate mothers. [^1]

In 2020, lesbian couple Mota and City underwent artificial reproduction in Cambodia. During Mota's pregnancy, she experienced hypertension, gestational diabetes, and ascites. Their daughter Doby was eventually born safely. Two years later, City underwent IVF again, but at 37 weeks of pregnancy, she encountered fetal demise. Within four years, this family spent about NT$2 million on cross-border treatment. [^1]

They were not lacking in medical technology to use. What they lacked was whether they could be legally recognized as people eligible to receive this technology in their own country. **Embryos can cross borders, but families cannot automatically cross legal boundaries.**

## A Medical Technology That Matured Before the Law

Taiwan's first test-tube baby was born in 1985. At that time, embryos could already be fertilized in vitro, but there was no complete system to handle the donation of reproductive cells, embryo storage, recipient consent, and the child's identity issues. [^3]

On March 21, 2007, the _Artificial Reproduction Act_ was promulgated and implemented. The law placed artificial reproduction within the framework of medical institution licensing, recipient eligibility, donation management, and data reporting. Artificial reproduction institutions must report the number of patients treated, success rates, causes of infertility, and the assisted reproductive technologies used, allowing the competent authority to establish a database. [^2]

The original scope of this law was about how "infertile couples" use artificial reproduction. Scholars analyzing data from 1998 to 2017 pointed out that the basic unit at that time was married couples, and at least one party had to have healthy reproductive cells. Donors and recipients could not specify each other, and donations of reproductive cells from abroad were also restricted. [^3]

Therefore, the Artificial Reproduction Act is not just a hospital operations manual. It also draws a line around "family." Doctors handle eggs, sperm, embryos, and implantation. The law handles who qualifies to request this service, and who becomes the legal parent after the child is born.

📝 Curator's note: Technology is born first, and the law only names it later. Even later is the recognition that the people using the technology are not limited to one type of family.

## An Expanding Medical Network

The system did not stop at 2007. The National Health Commission's 2022 _National Report on Artificial Reproduction Technology in Taiwan_ pointed out that in the early 1998, there were 48 artificial reproduction institutions. By July 2024, the number of licensed medical institutions had increased to 101. The 2022 statistics analyzed treatment cases from 99 institutions. [^2]

Artificial reproduction thus ceased to be an edge technology that a few doctors were groping through on their own. It has a licensing system, treatment records, regulations for the acceptance, storage, and provision of donated reproductive cells, and regular quality management reviews of artificial reproduction institutions by the competent authority.

The expansion of this medical network is also reflected in birth statistics. Calculated based on data from 1998 to 2016, the proportion of artificial reproduction births to total births rose from 0.86% in 1998 to 1.44% in 2007, and then to 4.33% in 2016. Studies estimate that five years after the implementation of the Artificial Reproduction Act, the number of artificial reproduction births increased by 78.51% relative to before. [^4]

An increase in numbers does not mean a disappearance of thresholds. The medical system can expand, but it must still serve recipients as defined by law. The state can encourage childbirth, and at the same time leave certain people who want to give birth outside of legal treatment.

📝 Curator's note: The most counterintuitive aspect of artificial reproduction is not that it can keep embryos in vitro, but that the more complete the medical network becomes, the clearer it is who is excluded.

## Subsidies Make Infertility a Public Policy Issue

In July 2021, the government launched an expanded IVF subsidy program, providing public resources to eligible infertile couples. This policy changed not only family expenses, but also how society discusses artificial reproduction. It was no longer just a medical choice that individuals had to bear, but was placed into discussions about declining birthrates, population aging, and public finances. [^5]

A study published in 2024 established a fiscal model based on Taiwan's subsidy policy and artificial reproduction birth data. The study estimated that for every NT$1 invested, the government could potentially gain about NT$5.6 in fiscal returns over the lifetime of IVF-born individuals. The study also cautioned that this result was based on long-term assumptions such as wages, working years, taxes, welfare, and discount rates, and should not be treated as a fixed return for every family. [^5]

This model brings about an important contrast. When the state calculates the value of subsidies based on future labor force and tax revenue, artificial reproduction is no longer just helping individual families, but is also treated as a tool of population policy.

But subsidies are not synonymous with "available to everyone." Subsidies can reduce the costs for eligible couples, but cannot alone change who qualifies to enter the system of the Artificial Reproduction Act. For single women and lesbian couples, the problem is often not whether they can afford a round of treatment, but whether medical institutions in Taiwan can legally begin treatment for them.

📝 Curator's note: If the state hopes that artificial reproduction will increase the number of births, it cannot only ask whether a family can afford it, but must also ask whether this family is seen by the law.

## The Law Chasing Families That Have Already Formed

After same-sex marriage was legalized in 2019, the legal recognition of family forms took a step forward. However, the Artificial Reproduction Act still mainly applied to heterosexual married couples. Marriage can be established, but it does not mean that every type of family within marriage can use the same reproductive medicine.

The draft amendments passed by the Executive Yuan in 2025 proposed including unmarried women and married female same-sex partners as applicable subjects for artificial reproduction. The Executive Yuan also clearly pointed out that the current provisions can no longer equally protect the basic rights of unmarried, divorced, widowed, and same-sex women in managing family life. [^6]

The National Health Commission said that since 2020, the amendments have convened more than 30 meetings and two public hearings with experts in children's rights, gender equality, law, and medicine. In addition to expanding the scope of application, the draft also includes informed consent, management of donated reproductive cells, the best interests of the child, and the right to know one's genetic origins. [^7]

The Executive Yuan's draft compiled by Public Radio pointed out that children born through artificial reproduction in the future can inquire about the donor's non-identifying information such as height, blood type, skin color, and nationality. When encountering major genetic diseases or organ transplants, they can further inquire about the donor's identity. The draft also stipulates that with the consent of the recipient, the storage period of embryos can be extended to 15 years. [^8]

These provisions push the issue from "whether the hospital can do it" to "how the child understands its origin after birth." When embryos are cryopreserved, they are like medical materials waiting to be disposed of. As the child grows up, they may become the starting point for questioning their origins, genetic relationships, and health risks.

📝 Curator's note: The preservation of embryos is a medical issue. Giving a child the opportunity to understand their origin of birth is a responsibility left behind by both the family and the law.

## One Embryo Involves Three Types of Rights

The first is the recipient's reproductive autonomy. Unmarried women and lesbian couples are not just fighting for "wanting a child," but for the right to use medical technology without first conforming to the family model of heterosexual marriage.

The second is the child's legal status. The Executive Yuan's draft proposes that children born through artificial reproduction by female same-sex partners and unmarried women should have clear legal status. Even if the same-sex marriage relationship of the female partners is later revoked or declared invalid, the child should not thereby fall into the disadvantaged status of being illegitimate. [^6]

The third is the right to information between donors and children. In the past, the system emphasized donor anonymity and medical management. The amendments begin to face another issue: when a person grows up, do they have the right to know how they were born?

This does not mean that all family relationships can be simplified into genes. It means that genetic data may have significance in genetic diseases, organ transplants, and self-identity. The child's rights are not just a field of data on a birth certificate, but information that they may still need when they grow up.

📝 Curator's note: What artificial reproduction truly preserves is not just one embryo, but a family's future that has not yet been decided, and the questions that a child may raise in the future.

## Why Surrogate Mothers Are the Hardest to Handle

The 2024 version of the amendments once put single women, female same-sex partners, and surrogacy together for discussion, and proposed to only allow altruistic surrogacy, limit compensation for surrogate mothers, allow each surrogate mother to be pregnant only once, and conduct economic, psychological, and family status evaluations. When the Judicial Reform Foundation sorted out the related controversies, it pointed out that the controversies surrounding single women and lesbian reproduction were relatively small, and what truly deepened social divisions was whether to open up surrogacy. [^9]

The version sent by the Executive Yuan in 2025 chose to first include female same-sex spouses and unmarried women, and to decouple the issues of surrogacy and male same-sex spouses. This does not mean that the controversy over surrogacy has disappeared, but that different issues are being separated.

The former mainly involves who can use their own reproductive cells and uterus. The latter involves the surrogate mother's body, the entrusting party's parental rights, commercialization risks, and the legal relationship after the child is born.

The reason why surrogacy is difficult is not because it cannot be done technically, but because reproductive responsibilities are distributed among more people. The egg provider, sperm provider, pregnant and giving birth person, and the legal parents may not be the same group of people. The autonomy, health, and withdrawal rights of any one person cannot be dealt with by just one consent form.

Therefore, the problems that the Artificial Reproduction Act will face next are not just about whether to open up. It still needs to answer who bears the medical risks, who can withdraw consent, when the child's parental rights are determined, how cross-border births are registered, and who protects the surrogate mother when conflicts arise between contracts and the body.

📝 Curator's note: Artificial reproduction can split fertilization into several steps. But the law cannot split each person's risks and pretend they are unrelated.

## What Taiwan Is Really Deciding

The history of Taiwan's artificial reproduction seems like a straight line of medical progress. Looking back, it is actually a line where qualifications are constantly being redrawn.

In 1985, the question was whether embryos could be fertilized in vitro. In 2007, the question was how to bring artificial reproduction into the law. In 2021, the question was whether public resources should share the cost. In 2025, the question became which families should no longer be excluded.

A study pointed out that artificial reproduction has indeed increased the number of ART births in Taiwan. But the study also placed artificial reproduction in the context of low birth rates, late marriage, and social pressure. [^4] If this technology is only seen as a tool to increase the birth rate, the person behind each treatment will be invisible.

These people include the couples waiting for embryo results in the clinic, the women who freeze their eggs, the lesbian families who must seek treatment abroad, and the children who grow up wanting to know where they came from.

This is also why the 2025 draft amendments are important, and why they should not be written as "Taiwan has fully opened up artificial reproduction." The draft still needs to be reviewed by the Legislative Yuan. The issues of surrogacy and male same-sex spouses have not been automatically resolved by decoupling.

The law has taken a step forward, but the details of medicine, parental rights, and children's rights still need to catch up. Taiwan does not lack reproductive technology, but it took a long time to begin recognizing that the people who want to use the technology are not limited to one type of family.

📝 Curator's note: An embryo can be seen under a microscope. A family needs to be seen, which requires medicine, law, and society to move forward at the same time.
