> **Thirty-Second Overview**
>
> - **Core Achievement**: Taiwan’s national blood donation rate has long remained between 7% and 8%, reaching nearly 8% in 2023. This far exceeds the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended standard of 5%, securing Taiwan’s position as number one globally.
> - **Historical Transformation**: Before 1974, Taiwan’s medical blood supply relied on "blood cows" (professional blood sellers). A grassroots-led donation movement successfully transformed the system into a 100% voluntary, non-remunerated model.
> - **Operational Mechanism**: The Taiwan Blood Services Foundation, a medical foundation, centrally coordinates the system, integrating blood donation vehicles and stations across the island to build an efficient blood supply chain.
> - **Current Challenges**: Facing a decline in young donors due to low birth rates and rising blood demand from an aging society, Taiwan is striving to promote precision blood use and digitalized donation management.

On the streets of Taiwan’s cities, it is common to see large buses painted in bright red or pink, bearing the slogan "One bag of blood saves one life." This is not ordinary public transport; it is Taiwan’s most proud mobile landmark of civil society—the blood donation vehicle. According to World Health Organization statistics, the median national blood donation rate in high-income countries is approximately 2.89% [^12]. Taiwan’s data in 2023, however, reached nearly 8%. This "world’s first" record hides a profound history of medical and ethical transformation as Taiwan transitioned from an authoritarian era to a democratic society.

## From "Blood Cows" to "Hot Blood": The Transformation History of Taiwan’s Blood Donation Movement

Before 1974, Taiwan’s medical blood environment was extremely dire. Hospital entrances were often crowded with professional blood sellers known as "blood cows." To make a living, they frequently sold blood, sometimes concealing infectious disease histories, which exposed recipients to significant infection risks [^7]. Blood cow leaders extracted high commissions, forming a black industrial chain filled with exploitation and opacity.

On April 19, 1974, the Republic of China Blood Donation Movement Association was established by civil figures including Tsai Pei-huo, officially launching Taiwan’s era of voluntary, non-remunerated blood donation [^8]. The success of this movement lay not only in technical transformation but also in the reshaping of concepts. Through joint advocacy by the government and civil society, "blood donation" was transformed from an activity perceived as harmful to the body into a noble sentiment demonstrating compassion and civic duty. In 1992, Taiwan officially ended the last commercial blood transactions, fully entering the era of voluntary, non-remunerated donation [^11].

## The Secret Behind the World’s Highest Donation Rate: Dual Support of System and Culture

Taiwan’s ability to maintain the world’s highest blood donation rate for a long time is primarily attributed to a unique "Taiwan Model." This model consists of three core pillars:

| Core Pillar                             | Specific Content                                                                                                                                                                                                                                               |
| :-------------------------------------- | :------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Unified Foundation Authority**        | The Taiwan Blood Services Foundation (formerly the Blood Donation Enterprise Foundation) coordinates Taiwan’s six major blood donation centers, ensuring standardization and safety in blood collection, testing, and supply [^2].                             |
| **High-Penetration Collection Network** | Beyond fixed donation stations, Taiwan possesses the world’s most dense network of mobile blood donation vehicles, stationed at train stations, parks, and campuses, significantly lowering the threshold for public donation [^6].                            |
| **Deeply Rooted Mutual Aid Culture**    | The slogan "One bag of blood saves one life" is deeply ingrained. Many enterprises, associations, and religious groups regularly hold donation activities, viewing blood donation as a form of collective social interaction and public welfare participation. |

Taiwan’s blood donation success is no accident. It reflects a spirit of "small-island mutual aid." In a geographical environment with scarce natural resources, Taiwanese people are accustomed to solving survival problems through collective collaboration. This spirit was particularly evident during the 1999 Jiji Earthquake and the 2021 COVID-19 pandemic; even under the pressure of lockdowns, long lines formed at blood donation stations [^1].

## Blood Donation Vehicles and Urban Soundscape: Entering the Daily Lifeline Network

In Taiwan, blood donation vehicles are more than medical facilities; they are a unique urban landscape. On weekends, in bustling commercial districts like Taipei’s Ximending or Taichung’s Yizhong Street, young people are often seen queuing beside blood donation vehicles. This "mobile collection" model is key to Taiwan’s ability to maintain high donation rates.

In recent years, the donation process has also gone digital. Through the "Blood Donation Little Butler" Line official account and dedicated app, donors can instantly check inventory levels for all blood types across the island (categorized as "Normal," "Low," and "Critical Shortage") and track the test reports of their donated blood [^3]. This immediate feedback mechanism enhances donors’ sense of participation and trust.

## Hidden Worries Under High Donation Rates: Challenges of Aging and Transfusion Overuse

Despite having the world’s highest donation rate, Taiwan frequently faces "blood shortage" alerts. This may seem contradictory, but it hides two major structural problems:

1. **Imbalanced Demographic Structure**: The young donor demographic aged 17 to 24 has decreased by nearly half over the past 20 years [^6]. As low birth rates intensify, the future main donor population faces a generational gap.
2. **Excessive Transfusion Volume**: Taiwan’s clinical blood usage is abnormally large. Statistics show that the transfusion rate for hospitalized patients in Taiwan is approximately three times that of the United States [^4]. This has prompted the medical community to reflect on the importance of "precision blood use," promoting Patient Blood Management (PBM) to reduce unnecessary blood waste [^12].

## Conclusion

Taiwan’s blood donation system is a history of struggle from chaos to civilization. It proves that when a society can establish a high level of trust and mutual aid mechanisms, it can create world-class public health miracles even without monetary incentives. However, as times change, ensuring this "hot blood" is passed on to the younger generation and promoting more scientific blood use standards on the clinical side will be Taiwan’s important task for the next fifty years.
