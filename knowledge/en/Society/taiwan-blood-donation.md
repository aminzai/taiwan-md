> **Quick Overview**
>
> - **Core Achievement**: The national blood donation rate in Taiwan has long been maintained between 7% and 8%, reaching nearly 8% in 2023, far exceeding the World Health Organization (WHO) recommended standard of 5%, securing the top global position.
> - **Historical Transformation**: Before 1974, medical blood in Taiwan relied on "blood cows" (professional blood sellers), which successfully transitioned to a 100% voluntary and unpaid donation model through civil initiatives.
> - **Operational Mechanism**: The Medical Foundation of Taiwan Blood Center unifies management, building an efficient blood supply chain with mobile donation vehicles and stations across the island.
> - **Current Challenges**: Facing declining young donors due to low birth rates and rising demand in an aging society, Taiwan is committed to promoting precision medicine and digital blood donation management.

On the streets of Taiwanese cities, one often sees large buses painted in vibrant red or pink, bearing the slogan "One bag saves one life." These are not ordinary public transport vehicles but the most proud mobile landmarks of Taiwan's civil society—the blood donation vehicles. According to WHO statistics, the median national donation rate among high-income countries is about 2.89 [^12], yet Taiwan’s data in 2023 approached 8%. This "world number one" record embodies a profound history of medical and ethical transformation as Taiwan transitioned from an authoritarian era to a democratic society.

## From "Blood Cows" to "Vibrant Blood": The Transformation History of Taiwan's Blood Donation Movement

Before 1974, the medical blood environment in Taiwan was extremely poor. Hospitals were often crowded with professional blood sellers known as "blood cows," who frequently sold blood for a living, sometimes concealing infectious disease histories, leading recipients to face extreme infection risks [^7]. The leaders of these blood cow syndicates extracted high commissions, forming an opaque and exploitative black industry chain.

On April 19, 1974, the "Republic of China Blood Donation Movement Association" was established by private citizens such as Tsai Pei-hwo, officially commencing Taiwan's era of voluntary and unpaid donation [^8]. The success of this movement was not merely technical; it was a reshaping of consciousness. Through concerted efforts between the government and civil society, "blood donation" was transformed from an act perceived as physically damaging into a noble virtue demonstrating compassion and civic duty. In 1992, Taiwan officially ended the last commercial blood trade, fully entering the era of unpaid donation [^11].

## The Secret to World Number One Donation Rates: Dual Support from System and Culture

Taiwan has maintained the world's highest blood donation rate primarily due to a unique "Taiwan Model." This model is composed of three core pillars:

| Core Pillar                             | Specific Content                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
| :-------------------------------------- | :---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Centralized Foundation**              | The Medical Foundation of Taiwan Blood Center (formerly the Blood Donation Business Foundation) manages six major blood centers across the island, ensuring standardization and safety in blood collection, testing, and supply [^2].     |
| **High-Penetration Collection Network** | In addition to fixed donation stations, Taiwan possesses one of the densest mobile donation vehicle networks globally, stationed at train stations, parks, and schools, significantly lowering the barrier for public participation [^6]. |
| **Deeply Rooted Mutual Aid Culture**    | The slogan "One bag saves one life" is deeply ingrained. Many corporations, associations, and religious groups regularly host blood donation events, viewing donation as a form of collective socialization and public service.           |

Taiwan's success in blood donation is not accidental. It reflects a spirit of "island mutual aid." In a geographically resource-scarce environment, Taiwanese people have habitually solved survival issues through collective cooperation. This spirit was particularly evident during the 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake and the 2021 COVID-19 pandemic, where long queues still formed at donation stations even under lockdown pressure [^1].

## Blood Donation Vehicles and Urban Scenery: Entering the Life-Saving Network of Daily Life

In Taiwan, blood donation vehicles are not just medical facilities; they are a unique urban landscape. On weekends, in bustling commercial areas like Ximending in Taipei or Yizhong Street in Taichung, one can always see young people queuing next to a donation vehicle. This "mobile collection" model is key to maintaining the high donation rate in Taiwan.

In recent years, the donation process has also moved toward digitalization. Through the official Line account and dedicated App of the "Blood Donation Steward," donors can instantly check the inventory status (categorized as "Normal," "Low," or "Critical") for various blood types across the island and track their own blood test reports [^3]. This real-time feedback mechanism enhances the sense of participation and trust among donors.

## Concerns Amidst High Rates: The Challenges of Aging and Transfusion Overload

Despite being world number one in donation rates, Taiwan frequently faces warnings of "blood shortages." This sounds contradictory, but it hides two major structural problems:

1. **Imbalanced Population Structure**: The young donor demographic aged 17 to 24 has decreased by nearly half over the past 20 years [^6]. As low birth rates worsen, the future core donor population faces a gap.
2. **Excessive Transfusion Volume**: Taiwan's clinical blood usage is abnormally large. Statistics show that the transfusion rate for hospitalized patients in Taiwan is about three times that of the United States [^4]. This has prompted the medical community to re-examine the importance of "precision medicine," promoting Patient Blood Management (PBM) to reduce unnecessary blood waste [^12].

## Conclusion

Taiwan's blood donation system is a history of striving from chaos toward civilization. It proves that when a society can establish a high degree of trust and mutual aid mechanisms, it can create world-class public health miracles even without financial incentives. However, as times change, how to pass on this "vibrant blood" to the younger generation and promote more scientific blood usage standards in clinical settings will be an important task for Taiwan over the next fifty years.
