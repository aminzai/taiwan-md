> **30-Second Overview:** The concept of community colleges was proposed by Huang Wu-hsiung in 1994; the Wenshan Community College opened in Muzha Junior High School in 1998; and the _Community College Development Regulations_ granted this grassroots educational experiment legal status in 2018. [^1] [^3]
>
> This is not about moving traditional universities into communities, but about transforming local history, rural life, rivers, old houses, and care issues into materials that residents can jointly study, discuss, and act upon. This article examines how community colleges continuously balance lifelong learning, publicness, government resources, and institutional autonomy while borrowing school buildings and local public spaces. [^5] [^8]

In 1994, Professor Huang Wu-hsiung of the Department of Mathematics at National Taiwan University proposed the concept of community colleges. Four years later, Taiwan's first community college opened in Wenshan District, Taipei City. [^1] On September 28, 1998, the Wenshan Community College began its first semester, adding an entrance to adult education in Taiwan that was not quite like a school. [^2]

On June 13, 2018, the _Community College Development Regulations_ were promulgated, granting community colleges independent legal status. [^3] These three years form a seemingly linear history of progress. But what is truly interesting is that it was never about moving universities into communities; rather, it attempted to turn "learning" into a tool for locally addressing public issues.

## The First Class at Fuhua Junior High School

Imagine the opening day: no university town, and no ivory tower. The Wenshan Community College classes were held in Muzha Junior High School. Students did not need prior academic credentials, and instructors did not just bring a set of standard answers into the room. The emergence of the first community college originated from grassroots educational reformers, not from a central government plan. [^4]

This starting point is crucial. It shows that what community colleges initially dealt with was not merely "what adults study after work," but who had the right to access knowledge, who could discuss their own lives, and whether people could continue participating in social reconstruction after democratization. Huang Wu-hsiung later described the initial two goals as "liberating knowledge and fostering civil society." [^5]

> **Curator's Note:** The most counterintuitive aspect of community colleges is that they treat a school as a space for practicing communal life, rather than merely a service station for acquiring skills.

In early 1999, five community colleges in Taipei County were established simultaneously. On September 20, 1999, the Yonghe Community College held its founding ceremony at Fuhua Junior High School. [^4] The courses at the Yonghe Community College had no departments, nor did they distinguish between required and elective subjects; instead, they placed academics, club activities, and life arts in a single learning venue. [^4]

This arrangement might seem loose, but it actually embodies a clear vision. Studying local history, learning plumbing and electricity, and discussing ecology were not three unrelated interests. They were all asking the same question: how can an individual regain the capacity to understand and change their life?

## Moving from "Taking Classes" to "Doing Things"

If one only looks at course titles, community colleges are easily mistaken for cheaper hobby classes. However, Huang Wu-hsiung understood the difference in a 1999 interview: his concern was not that community colleges would disappear, but that they might lose their original public nature after becoming widespread. [^6]

This worry was not an abstract philosophical debate. Early community colleges faced issues such as unstable venues, insufficient funding, and weak social support. Teams outside of Wenshan often had to simultaneously recruit students, raise funds, and explain to local governments whether they were a school, a club, or a social movement. [^7]

A sharper dilemma was that community colleges needed public resources to maintain low tuition but could not simply become outsourced units for the government. Huang Wu-hsiung once criticized the process of awarding contracts based on the lowest bid, which might lead to "anti-elimination," causing genuinely capable community colleges to be replaced by cheaper contractors. [^7]

> **Curator's Note:** A school that aims to teach independent judgment cannot itself only learn how to follow evaluation forms.

The most distinctive classes at community colleges are often outside the classroom. The Qimei Community College in Kaohsiung treated rural life as a curriculum, placing learning and local restoration—from environmental education and rural workshops to post-Morakot typhoon community reconstruction—on the same table. [^8]

The Fengshan Community College studied local governance through Cao Gong Canal and its surrounding hydrology, while the Gangshan Community College incorporated new immigrant women, elderly learners, sugar mills, and Ah Gong Dianxi into its courses. [^8] These cases were not mere decorations of "extracurricular activities"; they transformed local experience into knowledge that could be jointly studied, discussed, and acted upon.

The law later recognized this. Article 3 of the _Community College Development Regulations_ defines community colleges as lifelong learning institutions outside the formal education system, with missions including participation in public affairs, local culture, local knowledge studies, and community sustainability. Enrollment is not restricted by academic background or nationality. [^3]

## What the Law Guaranteed and What It Did Not

In 2017, the Executive Yuan reported that there were 86 community colleges across Taiwan with a total of 400,000 participants. When the Ministry of Education promulgated the special law in 2018, it used the scale of 87 community colleges nationwide as the benchmark for institutionalization. [^2] The difference in numbers may stem from different statistical periods, but they point to one thing: community colleges have evolved from a grassroots experiment in Wenshan into an important network for adult learning across Taiwan.

The special law requires local governments to assist in providing stable venues and allocating budgets, establishing systems through evaluations, learning certificates, and review committees. No less than one-third of the members of the review committee must have practical experience in community colleges. [^3] These regulations partially converted what was once sustained by individual passion into a responsibility that the government must address.

But law is not magic. The National Association of Community Colleges pointed out during its 2024 review of the legislation that although the special law passed, there are still discrepancies in how local governments implement mandates, provide venues, conduct evaluations, and ensure publicness. Community colleges may also gradually drift toward becoming mere hobby classes under financial pressure or be dictated by administrative demands. [^9]

This is community colleges' second contradiction: they need institutional support, but the institution must not grind them down. Without venues and funding, publicness is just a slogan. With venues and funding, community colleges might become another type of administrative service counter.

> **Curator's Note:** What community colleges truly seek to protect is not the word "informal," but whether learners can form judgments, relationships, and actions regarding their own locality.

## A Taiwanese Study Without a School Gate

When speaking about community colleges, Huang Wu-hsiung often described "knowledge liberation" concretely: it was not just changing professional jargon into plain language, but enabling learners to start from their own experiences, connect a sudden "point" that strikes them into a "line," and expand it into a "plane" capable of understanding the world. [^7]

This perspective also explains why community colleges emphasize local studies. The rural courses in Kaohsiung, the ecological integration in Yonghe, and the river patrols and cultural preservation across various locations all transform experiences that residents initially considered "just daily life" into local knowledge that can be recorded, discussed, and shared. [^4] [^7]

A report on community colleges by Taiwan Panorama described them as sites of cultural nodes and civic action, illustrating how they transcend the boundaries of traditional educational institutions through tree surveys, local research, and community service. [^10] This observation reminds us: the importance of community colleges lies not in the number of courses they offer, but because they force the question of "who can produce knowledge" back into the community.

Therefore, community colleges can also be seen as a form of Taiwanese studies without a school gate. They may not issue traditional degrees, nor do they necessarily have fixed campuses. But they transform local canals, farmlands, old houses, ethnic memories, and public disputes into subjects that society can jointly learn about.

## After More Than Twenty Years

The history of community colleges—from one to dozens, from no legal basis to a special law—is easily written as a success story. However, Huang Wu-hsiung warned back in 1999 that the hardest part for community colleges was not running a popular course, but not shrinking "civil society" into a pretty recruitment slogan. [^6]

The issues they face today have also changed. Aging populations, declining birth rates, learning resources in remote areas, extreme climate, local revitalization, and immigrant societies all demand that community colleges do more than just offer courses; they must reimagine how learning connects with the future of the locality. The Executive Yuan once viewed community colleges as part of a lifelong learning system. Field workers continue to question whether this system can retain the grassroots nature of community colleges. [^2] [^9]

The answer will not be given by a single piece of legislation. It may appear in an afternoon when a participant leaves class to conduct a river survey, or it may appear on an evening when residents begin debating a canal, a plot of farmland, or an old house. The value of community colleges is not in making every person an expert, but in giving more people the opportunity to no longer be merely objects of governance.

> **Curator's Note:** What the first community college in Wenshan truly left behind was not a model for a campus, but a question—can we learn together how to treat our locality as our own business?

Perhaps the most memorable image of Taiwanese community colleges is not the lecturer on the podium, but a borrowed classroom at Fuhua Junior High School: someone entering with post-work fatigue, and someone leaving with questions about their locality. Twenty years later, institutions can maintain their position, but only continuous public participation can prevent that position from becoming hollow.

Community colleges frequently use junior high schools, elementary schools, or other public buildings for classes. This spatial arrangement both brings learning closer to residents and reminds people that a community college is not an independent building, but a method of education that reassembles previously dispersed public resources. Whether the venue is stable becomes an issue of learning rights from administrative details. [^3] [^9]

## Public Knowledge Also Appears on Bulletin Boards

![A public bulletin board in a downtown area of Tainan City; Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.0](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/5/56/2009-01-18_public_bulletin_board_of_an_urban_village_in_West_Central_District%2C_Tainan.jpg)

_Figure: A public bulletin board in a downtown area of Tainan City. This is not a community college course announcement, but an example of how local public information enters daily life; the original file is marked with CC BY-SA 2.0, and the image page provides author and reuse conditions. [^12]_

The "publicness" of community colleges is sometimes not as conspicuous as a large forum. It may first appear on a bulletin board, in a local survey, during a community walk, or in records of people meeting up next week. These things are not necessarily systems themselves, but they give local residents an opportunity to know what others care about.

From the perspective of "experiential knowledge" as described by Huang Wu-hsiung, the significance of the bulletin board is not how much information it provides, but that it places information in a location where someone can stop to read, compare, and respond. [^5] When residents begin writing down their observations, public issues have a chance to transition from private feelings into material that can be jointly addressed.

## A Criterion That Is Easily Overlooked

To judge whether a community college remains a community college, one cannot only look at how many courses it offers or the number of registrants. What is more worthy of attention is whether participants can bring knowledge from the classroom back to the locality and encounter the experiences of others there.

For example, a course about rivers might stop merely at water quality terminology, making little difference from a general lecture. But if students begin to know which stream runs through their life circle, who is responsible for management, and how pollution is perceived, the class has already moved beyond the classroom.

This "moving out" does not mean every course must become an act of struggle, nor does it mean all participants must join the same organization. It is more like a change in scale: people no longer see local issues as someone else's news story, but begin to know that they can ask questions, look up data, find people to discuss with, and even propose different solutions. [^7]

Similarly, life arts courses should not be excluded from publicness. Data from the Kaohsiung community college shows that courses on woodworking, winemaking, organic farming, old house reuse, and local stories can connect daily skills to issues of environment, industry, or memory. [^8] The key is not whether the course title is deep, but whether the course leaves space for learners to connect with each other.

This is also what sets community colleges apart from general marketized learning services. The market asks if a course can quickly fill up. A community college must also ask if this course has added some capacity for locals to understand one another. The former can be answered with enrollment numbers; the latter requires time to reveal.

## Why It Is Still Needed Today

Taiwan's public problems are often not characterized by a lack of experts, but by a lack of common language between experts, government, and residents. Community colleges cannot replace research institutions or local governments, but they can provide a common starting point that does not require formal credentials or mastery of professional jargon. [^3]

This starting point is particularly important for the elderly, immigrants, laborers, caregivers, and those who have been away from formal education for a long time. The regulations that do not restrict enrollment by academic background are not merely administrative leniency. They pull the question of "who qualifies to learn" back to public life itself, rather than just educational thresholds. [^3]

Of course, openness does not equal automatic equality. Proximity of venues, class times, tuition fees, language, and care responsibilities still determine who can more easily access community colleges. Therefore, the responsibility of local governments to provide venues and funding cannot be seen merely as subsidizing the educational team, but as a fundamental investment in the right to learn. [^3]

The next step for community colleges should not just be moving courses online or adding a few popular themes. More importantly, it is whether new generations can bring climate, care, immigration, local industry, and housing issues into the learning environment, allowing community colleges to become places where contemporary social movements and local knowledge meet. [^9]

They do not need to offer grand answers every time. Enabling someone to move from "this is not my business" to "I want to know how this works" is already a concrete advancement in a democratic society.
