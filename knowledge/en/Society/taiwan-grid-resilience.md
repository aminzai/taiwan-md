> **30-Second Overview**: On August 15, 2017, an interruption in natural gas supply caused rolling blackouts across 17 counties and cities, affecting 6.68 million users; on March 3, 2022, an operational error at Hsingda Power Plant affected over 5.49 million households. Neither incident was simply a matter of "insufficient generation capacity," but rather how a single equipment, procedural, or hub failure could escalate into a nationwide event. In response, the Taiwan Power Company (Taipower) proposed a ten-year, NT$564.5 billion grid resilience plan, shifting from a centralized grid to a distributed, robust, and defensive one. But the core question remains: when a blackout occurs, who can restore life first—hospitals, communities, rural areas, or the city?

# Taiwan’s Power Problem Is Not Just About Generation Capacity

Blackouts are most easily understood as a supply-and-demand issue: Is there enough electricity? Is the spinning reserve high enough? Which energy source should be increased? But when a light goes out, what disappears is not just illumination. Elevators stop, trapping residents between floors; base stations and network equipment require backup power, or families cannot contact each other; hospitals must rely on generators to sustain surgeries, ventilators, and refrigerated medicines; cold chains, water pumping, traffic signals, and continuous manufacturing processes in factories must all decide within a short time how to hold on.

Therefore, grid resilience is not about "never having an accident," but about whether the system can limit the scope of a fault, maintain critical services, quickly identify the problem, and restore power after an accident occurs. Taipower’s definition of resilience also includes the ability to respond to accidents and restore stable operations within a short time. [^1]

This definition shifts the problem from "whether there is enough electricity" to four more difficult questions: Is power concentrated in a few large nodes? Can faults be isolated? Do localities have their own backups? Who decides the order of repair? If we only increase generation capacity without addressing transmission lines, substations, protection relays, operational procedures, and local distribution, the city may still lose large-scale power supply in what seems like a localized accident.

# 815: How a Valve Blacked Out Half of Taiwan

The 815 blackout on August 15, 2017, was the turning point when Taiwan’s grid resilience was seen by society on a large scale. An investigation by the Executive Yuan found that the sudden interruption of natural gas supply at Datun Power Plant was caused by a contractor replacing the power supply unit of a natural gas metering station using a temporary jumper wire. After removal, the controller’s power and communication were interrupted. The controller restarted and automatically closed the electric valve, ultimately causing a complete gas supply interruption. [^2]

The core of the accident cannot be explained by "one person pressing the wrong button." The investigation also pointed out that shared controllers for critical electric valves, insufficient diversification of gas supply at Datun Power Plant, and inadequate change management and risk assessment meant that a localized maintenance action had consequences far exceeding the site scale. After the natural gas supply was interrupted, units shut down, system power supply capacity dropped sharply, and finally caused rolling blackouts across 17 counties and cities, affecting 6.68 million users. [^3]

The Environmental Information Center recalled the 815 incident, noting that the impact was not just the blackout itself, but also reignited public debate on energy transition, gas dependence, grid stability, and backup capacity. [^3] This debate is often simplified into supporting or opposing a certain energy source, but another lesson from 815 is: regardless of whether power comes from gas, coal, nuclear, or renewables, as long as key supply, control, and transmission/distribution systems lack multi-layer protection, a single error can still be amplified.

The Executive Yuan’s investigation therefore proposed directions such as multi-layer protection, increasing fuel supply redundancy, strengthening the disaster resilience of gas and power supply systems, and improving independent power supply for public transport and vital systems. [^2] These suggestions moved "energy security" from inside the power plant fence to the entire city: critical services cannot just wait for the main grid to recover; they must have their own safety boundaries.

# 303: More Than Just an Operational Error

The 303 blackout on March 3, 2022, made "procedures" and "organizational culture" the center of grid discussion. An investigation by the Control Yuan found that during the major overhaul of Unit 2 at Hsingda Power Plant, the open-circuit switch and sub-card procedures were not followed. Testing was conducted before the circuit breaker’s insulating gas had fully recharged, causing a 345kV busbar flashover ground fault, which subsequently triggered system splitting and large-scale blackouts. The accident occurred in the morning of that day, and all power was restored by 9:31 PM, affecting over 5.49 million households. [^4]

A review report released by the Ministry of Economic Affairs also pointed out that the reason a single operational error could cause a major blackout was related to insufficient multi-layer protection and the risk of the southern grid being concentrated at the Longqi Extra-High Voltage Substation. Improvement directions include establishing a dedicated risk unit, promoting distributed grids, reviewing protection relays and switchyard systems, and strengthening professional training and external expert diagnosis. [^5]

Here lies an easily overlooked institutional problem: if every unit only handles its own local task, and no one can see "how an operational error crosses multiple defense lines," the system will build safety on the assumption that everyone never makes mistakes. True resilience, conversely, assumes that people will make mistakes, equipment will fail, and weather will deteriorate; therefore, it must allow errors to be discovered and isolated, rather than penetrating the entire system directly.

After 303, Taiwan society’s expectations for blackouts became more precise. Citizens no longer only ask "why did it go out," but also "why did a power plant accident affect so many people," "why was the same error not prevented at the previous layer," and "why was the power restoration order and information release not clear enough." These questions are actually demanding that the grid become a public system that can be understood and supervised.

# From Centralized Efficiency to Distributed Resilience

Taiwan’s past grid architecture had its historical reasons. Taiwan is densely populated with limited land for power plants, and electricity demand is concentrated in urban and industrial areas. Thus, the grid gradually formed a centralized architecture connecting large power plants, north-south transmission, and a few hub substations. Taipower explains that the existing system consists of three 345kV main lines, connected by three major hubs: Longtan, Zhongliao, and Longqi, linking the northern, central, and southern regions. [^1]

Centralization can bring efficiency and allow different regions to support each other; the problem is that when hubs become bottlenecks, efficiency may turn into shared vulnerability. The plans from the Executive Yuan and Taipower after the 303 incident all listed "reducing centralized risk" as a core goal. The 2022 published Grid Resilience Enhancement Construction Plan planned a ten-year investment of NT$564.5 billion, with three main axes: striving for distribution, continuous strengthening, and enhanced defense. [^1]

Distribution is not just building more power plants. The plan includes direct power supply from power plants to science and industrial parks, green energy distributed supply, increasing distribution nodes, clustered hub substations, expanding energy storage, indoorizing substations, and real-time dynamic defense. [^1] Its logic is to make power closer to the center of consumption, make fault points easier to isolate, and ensure that when a main line or a substation has an accident, the entire region does not have to wait together.

Taipower stated that the grid resilience plan will build 28 new substations and promote the indoorization of some substations; meanwhile, through green energy plus storage, distributed energy will not only be a power source but also a tool for regional power supply stability. [^6] This direction is intertwined with energy transition: renewables have distribution potential, but wind and solar irradiation are volatile. Without storage, scheduling, and distribution capabilities, distributed power sources do not automatically equal distributed resilience.

# Distribution Grid: Where Blackouts Most Often Occur

Nationwide large blackouts are the most conspicuous, but they are not the only form of blackout citizens encounter in daily life. Data compiled by the Executive Yuan Economic Advisory Office from Taipower shows that the number of distribution accident blackouts decreased from 21,019 in 2012 to 6,115 in 2023, a 70% drop over ten years. [^7]

These numbers indicate that grid resilience also occurs on streets, utility poles, transformers, and feeders. Taiwan has over 10,000 feeders, approximately 410,000 kilometers of lines, 1.55 million switches, 1.48 million transformers, and 3.2 million utility poles; over half of accident blackouts are related to external and environmental factors such as animals, trees, lightning, salt damage, construction, vehicles, or user equipment. [^7]

Therefore, resilience is not just engineering for extra-high voltage substations. Feeder automation can detect and isolate fault sections when accidents occur, restoring normal lines first; disaster-proof line undergrounding can reduce blackouts caused by trees, strong winds, and external object contact. [^7] But engineering also involves choices: to what extent should densely populated, medically, and traffic-important areas be prioritized? Rural lines are long, and maintenance distances are far; do they require another evaluation method? Undergrounding all lines is not necessarily the only answer; the key is placing limited resources where they can most reduce public risk.

# Microgrids: Bringing "Recovery" Back to the Local Level

The large grid is still the main foundation of Taiwan’s life, but in disasters, whether localities can temporarily operate independently of the main grid may determine whether shelters, communications, and basic lighting can be maintained first. Taipower Monthly reported that disaster-resilient microgrids with islanding capabilities, combining solar photovoltaics, energy storage, and energy management systems, have been built in areas such as Wulai Fushan, Jiashi in Alishan, Chiayi, mountainous areas in Pingtung, and offshore islands. [^8]

The "islanding operation" of microgrids is not to make the entire township permanently self-sufficient, but to disconnect the local power system when the external grid is damaged, prioritizing the maintenance of shelters, communications, lighting, and necessary equipment. This capability is particularly important for rural, mountainous, and offshore areas, because when main lines or roads are damaged, repair personnel and equipment need more time to arrive.

Taipower is also collaborating with universities to promote microgrids, bringing technology into education, demonstration, and community support fields. [^8] But microgrids are not complete just by installing solar panels and batteries. They also need to know who is responsible for starting them, which equipment is prioritized, how long fuel and batteries can last, how to operate when communication is interrupted, and how to safely reconnect with the main grid after the disaster.

These questions make microgrids simultaneously an engineering and governance issue. If there is only equipment but no local maintainers, it may not start during an accident; if only a few institutions know the operation method, the community cannot treat it as a common shelter infrastructure. True local resilience must put technology, training, responsibility, and drills together.

# Resilience Must Be Measurable, Not Just Claimed

Grid investment is often presented in engineering quantities, such as how many new substations are added, how many lines are undergrounded, and how many energy storage devices are installed; but engineering quantities do not equal resident safety during accidents. A more complete evaluation should place "the probability of fault occurrence," "the scope of fault expansion," "the time critical services can hold," and "whether information can be understood" in the same set of indicators. [^1][^7]

| Resilience Aspect | Observable Indicators                                                                               | Governance Questions That Must Be Answered                                                                       |
| ----------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Prevention        | Equipment failure rate, tree/lightning blackout, backup degree of critical nodes                    | Which risks are most worthy of priority investment, and who is responsible for regularly updating the risk list? |
| Isolation         | Fault location time, feeder automation coverage, zoning power supply capability                     | Can local faults be limited to the smallest scope, or will they affect the entire region?                        |
| Maintenance       | Backup hours for hospitals, water supply, communications, and shelters                              | Which services must continue to operate before the main grid recovers, and what are the minimum standards?       |
| Recovery          | Average power restoration time, rural/offshore island restoration gap, information update frequency | Is the power restoration order transparent, and do vulnerable residents receive substantial assistance?          |
| Learning          | Transparency of accident reports, frequency of drills, completion rate of improvement items         | Do the lessons from one blackout truly change the next procedure and budget?                                     |

Such indicators can also avoid "averages masking differences." A decrease in the national average blackout time does not mean every community is equally safe; large hospitals having generators does not mean surrounding elderly care institutions, elevator buildings, and small businesses can withstand the same length of blackout. The goal of resilience governance should be to first fill the gaps that are most likely to cause life danger and public service interruption, rather than just pursuing a pretty total average.

# From Equipment Resilience to Institutional Resilience

Hardware upgrades to the grid must occur alongside institutional observability. Substation reinforcement, line undergrounding, and storage construction can reduce the probability of faults, but they cannot alone answer the three governance questions after an accident: Who has the authority to decide priority power restoration? Who must disclose accident information? Who is responsible for turning experience into the next drill and rule?

The common lesson from 815 and 303 is that accidents often cross multiple organizational boundaries. Natural gas suppliers, power plant operators, transmission dispatchers, local governments, and public service institutions may each complete their local tasks, but no single unit can see cross-system risks early enough. Resilience therefore requires cross-agency shared contexts, clear escalation reporting, trackable change management, and post-accident investigation methods that not only find individual responsibility but also check institutional defense lines.

For citizens, transparency does not end with publishing a technical report. Public information during a blackout should at least explain the affected scope, the current degree of certainty regarding the cause, the next update time, locations where drinking water and charging can be obtained, and assistance available for the elderly, persons with disabilities, medical dependents, and rural residents. If information only circulates in professional jargon, even if power is restored quickly, society may still lose trust due to uncertainty.

# Grid Resilience Will Also Become a Social Distribution Issue

The impact of blackouts is never average. Living in a building with an elevator versus an old apartment without one results in different ways of bearing a blackout; people working from home, those needing refrigerated medicine, those relying on electric medical equipment, and small shop owners operating cold chain food do not have the same backup capabilities. Large enterprises may have generators and uninterruptible power systems, but small businesses and low-income families can only wait for power restoration.

Therefore, the grid resilience plan cannot be measured only by "average blackout time" or "national power restoration ratio." We must also ask: Which key public services have independent power supply? Which communities have accessible shelters? Which rural and offshore areas have microgrids or backups? Is blackout information released in a way everyone can understand? Is the power restoration order transparent?

The Executive Yuan’s investigation of the 815 accident has listed independent power supply for public transport and vital systems as an improvement direction. [^2] This indicates that the grid is not a service quality of a single industry, but a shared condition for healthcare, water supply, communications, transportation, and public safety. When blackouts occur simultaneously during heatwaves, typhoons, or disasters, power shortages can quickly turn into health risks and social inequality.

# An Island’s Energy Transition Must Simultaneously Be a Backup Transition

Taiwan’s energy transition often debates between generation types: gas, coal, nuclear, and renewables each have costs, risks, and political controversies. But grid resilience requires another perspective: energy choices must be planned together with transmission and distribution architecture, storage, demand management, fuel supply, and local backups.

When renewables are combined with storage and microgrids, they are not just a power source; they may also become a local safety boundary during disasters. This transition requires new maintenance capabilities: locals need to know which equipment is still usable during a blackout, managers must be able to start islanding operations, power companies must be able to safely connect, and the government must incorporate these capabilities into disaster drills rather than waiting for accidents to confirm if equipment actually works.

Analysis from the Global Taiwan Institute points out that the 2017, 2021, and 2022 blackout events exposed Taiwan’s highly dependent economic structure on power, and also indicated that the core of the 2022 NT$564.5 billion plan is to reduce vulnerable bottlenecks, using distribution and microgrids to lower the possibility of a single node causing large-scale blackouts. [^9]

The Environmental Information Center’s review of 815 reminded us that blackout events simultaneously involve gas dependence, grid stability, spinning reserve, and social trust in energy transition. [^3] If energy transition only adds new power sources but does not simultaneously explain how power is transmitted, stored, and how critical services are maintained during accidents, what citizens feel may still just be "chose one energy source, but did not solve the blackout."

More mature energy governance should plan "how much electricity" and "where to restore first" together. Renewables, storage, demand response, direct power supply from power plants, regional microgrids, and distribution automation are not mutually exclusive single answers, but a toolkit for reducing different risks.

Energy transition therefore requires new public communication methods. The government should not only publish installed capacity and construction amounts, but also explain how new equipment operates on cloudy days, during typhoons, when fuel supply is interrupted, or when communication is damaged; local residents need to know the safety distance of facilities, maintenance responsibility, land use impact, and contact windows during accidents. When technical information can be translated into understandable life contexts, energy policy has the opportunity to move from abstract supply-demand debates to risk management that can be jointly supervised.

# After Blackouts, What Should the City Restore First?

The last question of grid resilience is not technical, but prioritization. After an accident, all places cannot recover simultaneously. The power restoration order for hospitals, water supply, communications, transportation, shelters, food cold chains, and residences must be discussed in advance, and cannot be decided temporarily in the dark. The 815 investigation also required reviewing early warning notices for zoning rolling load shedding, grasping affected feeders and user counts, and gradually establishing independent power supply circuits for public transport, security, and vital systems. [^2] These reminders tell us that power restoration is not just a question of "how fast," but also whether the government can provide information justice on "who recovers first, who is still waiting."

If the power restoration order for critical services is not publicly disclosed in advance, blackouts easily result in support being prioritized for places with the most resources, the loudest voices, or those closest to the decision-making center. Conversely, if local governments and power companies regularly inventory healthcare, water supply, communications, shelters, and elderly care sites during peacetime, and incorporate backup capabilities and power restoration priority into drills, resilience will not just stop at an equipment list, but become a public commitment that can be tested by society.

This also requires the government, Taipower, local governments, medical institutions, communities, and industries to establish a common recovery plan. During large-scale blackouts, citizens need not just the message "repairing in progress," but also to know how long it may last, where to obtain drinking water and charging, which roads and traffic facilities are still usable, and who can receive priority assistance.

In recent years, Taipower has shifted from a centralized architecture to distributed, robust, and defensive, and distribution accident blackouts have shown a downward trend, indicating that long-term engineering can indeed improve power supply quality. [^1][^7] But the lessons left by 815 and 303 are still timely: any system that lacks alternative paths, error isolation, and public review may turn a local problem into a crisis for the entire city.

True grid resilience does not guarantee that blackouts will never happen, but that blackouts do not equal the complete stoppage of life. It should ensure hospitals have power, rural areas have their own basic support, communities know how to care for the vulnerable, and the government can clearly explain who recovers first, why they recover first, and how to avoid the same mistakes next time.

When Taiwan invests ten years and NT$564.5 billion to redesign the grid, the most important outcome should not be just one new line or one new substation, but that after an accident, more places can retain a bit of light, a bit of communication, and a bit of order, until the entire city reconnects to power.
