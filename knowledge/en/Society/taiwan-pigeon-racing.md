# Taiwan's Pigeon Racing: Letting Young Birds Fly Into a Wind Without Referees

> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan's pigeon racing integrates young-bird sea racing, multi-stage elimination, loft technology, and betting into one rule set. It uses the "one race per lifetime" principle and high uncertainty to narrow the experience gap, making participants believe everyone has a chance, while forcing the debut young birds to bear the costs of wind direction, disorientation, and loss of contact. This article begins with electronic leg rings, lofts, and sea releases, then traces the animal welfare, public governance, and local social issues left behind by disqualified birds entering cities and offshore islands. [^3]

Reading time: approximately 10 minutes.

**Keywords**: pigeon racing, sea racing, young birds, one race per lifetime, animal welfare, underground economy

_Image: Andreas Trepte, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 2.5. [License and original file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Homing_pigeon.jpg). This image is a freely licensed hotlink and is not a photo of a Taiwan pigeon racing scene._

On the morning of November 17, 2024, nearly 90,000 pigeons took off from the waters off Keelung. Only 7,712 pigeons completed the qualifying race within the stipulated time, resulting in a return rate of 8.6%. [^1] This was not a majestic flock of birds from a movie, but a morning in which a system sliced "going home" into qualified and disqualified categories.

About 60 years ago, Taiwan's pigeon racing activities began to take their current form. Young-bird sea racing, multi-stage elimination, and large-scale betting were gradually integrated into the same rule set. [^2] Pigeons must fly; pigeon owners must bet; pigeon clubs must convince everyone that the competition is fair enough.

The truly counter-intuitive part lies here: **the harshest rules of Taiwan's pigeon racing are not just to find the fastest pigeons, but also to make ordinary pigeon fanciers believe they still have a chance. However, when fairness must be established by increasing uncertainty, the young birds are often the first to bear that uncertainty.**

## See an Electronic Leg Ring First

When a racing pigeon returns to the loft, the electronic leg ring on its leg leaves a timestamp on the感应 board (receiver plate), and the data is then transmitted back to the pigeon club. What the pigeon owner sees is not merely a bird that flew back, but a competitor that can be timed, verified, and ranked. [^2]

During the 2024 Winter North Sea Race, pigeon fanciers handed over their months-trained competitors to the pigeon club. The pigeon boat carried them to the open sea. After release, they had to cross the sea surface, find the narrow land entrance, and fly back to their own lofts. [^1]

The "goalpost" in the North Sea is not a visible structure. Reports indicate that the coastline from Shihmen to Sandiaojiao, approximately 80 kilometers long, is the critical area for racing pigeons to land on Taiwan's main island. If the wind direction changes, the flock may miss this entrance and fly towards Hualien or even further. [^3]

**Curator's Note #1: The Leg Ring Is Not Decoration**
_The most important object in pigeon racing is not the trophy, but the leg ring. It translates a bird's homing instinct into seconds that humans can accept._

## How "One Race Per Lifetime" Is Framed as Fair

![Parent pigeon and young bird. Image by Hery blur, CC BY-SA 4.0.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Racing_Pigeon.jpg)

_Image: Hery blur, Wikimedia Commons, CC BY-SA 4.0. [License and original file page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Racing_Pigeon.jpg). This image is used to contrast the life stages of breeding and competitor cultivation in the pigeon racing industry._

The uniqueness of Taiwan's racing system is that competitors are mostly young birds aged 4 to 8 months, and each pigeon typically participates in only one season of competition. Before the official races, there are qualifying races, followed by several stages of sea racing. Pigeons that fail to return within the time limit are disqualified, regardless of whether they eventually find their way home. [^3]

In other regions, pigeon racers can accumulate competition experience. Taiwan's young-bird sea racing pushes all competitors to the same starting line. They have no previous season's results to compare, and pigeon owners cannot rely on the same bird to compete repeatedly. This design reduces the advantage of experience but simultaneously increases the weight of weather, wind direction, and chance. [^3]

Research from the Institute of Sociology, Academia Sinica, describes this phenomenon as "fairness completed by luck." The researchers point out that Taiwan's pigeon racing places legal competition and illegal gambling, controllable scientific rationality, and uncontrollable chance within the same system. The more complex the rules, the more participants can believe that cheating has been excluded, and the more the outcome feels like an opportunity everyone can encounter. [^3]

This does not mean pigeon fanciers are unaware of capital gaps. The research abstract also notes that funds, experience, and connections still bring advantages. However, when wind direction, disorientation, and chance are pulled to a sufficiently large scale, ordinary people can still imagine that they might win one day. [^3]

**Curator's Note #2: Fairness Is Not the Same Starting Line**
_"Everyone has a chance" is not "everyone has the same conditions." It is a belief that society is willing to continue betting, and a language that packages risk as fairness._

## How Pigeon Clubs Turn Risk Into Rules

Taiwan holds sea racing events every spring, summer, and winter. A report compiled data from various pigeon clubs, indicating that a season of competition usually lasts 3 to 7 weeks. The North Sea and South Sea races release pigeons from the waters off Keelung Port and Kaohsiung Port, respectively. Annually, approximately 400,000 to 500,000 racing pigeons across Taiwan are ringed and enter competition. In the 2024 Winter North Sea and South Sea races, over 9,800 lofts submitted approximately 130,000 pigeons. [^2]

The pigeon clubs' rules appear to be solely for timing, but they are actually addressing issues of trust. Release positions, GPS coordinates, flight speed per kilometer, and clock-in times are written into the racing system. Electronic leg rings and pigeon clocks turn the return into a verifiable record. Pigeon clubs also set up different anti-cheating procedures between the handover, release, and return verification, because concerns about kidnapping, swapping, and cheating always affect participants' trust in the results. [^2][^3]

This system simultaneously disperses risk to many people. Pigeon owners are responsible for breeding and training; pigeon clubs handle rules and verification; boat fleets handle transportation; veterinarians and feed suppliers provide peripheral services. The more professional the industry, the easier it is for participants to view the competition as a learnable technique rather than simple betting. However, technology can improve management capabilities but cannot eliminate the contingency of sea conditions, wind direction, and the physical condition of the birds. [^2][^4]

## Technology and Emotion in the Loft

![Batman Park Pigeon Loft. Image by Nick carson, Public domain.](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:FilePath/Batman_Park_Pigeon_Loft.JPG)

_Image: Nick carson, Wikimedia Commons, Public domain. [Original file and license page](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Batman_Park_Pigeon_Loft.JPG). The image displays a loft space and does not represent the configuration of private lofts in Taiwan._

Pigeon racing is not just about the few minutes on release day. Pigeon fanciers interviewed by reporters had to select breeds, pair them, ring them, train them, and adjust their feed 4 to 8 months before the race. The loft must be cleaned daily; the pigeons' condition must be observed; and training distances must be gradually extended. [^2]

A single loft can be divided into different floors. Competitor pigeons are on the top floor, breeding pigeons are responsible for reproduction, and veteran pigeons that have previously competed remain in another space. These arrangements stack family, agricultural technology, and competitive strategy together. To the pigeon owner, pigeons are not abstract "counts," but individuals that require care, waiting, and may also change the lives of the whole family. [^2]

Therefore, if pigeon racing is only written as gambling, it misses the time pigeon fanciers truly invest. If it is only written as bird love, it misses the pressure brought by prize money, underground groups, and competition. The contradiction of pigeon racing is not that each side has half the truth, but that care and calculation coexist in the same loft.

## When Competition Has No Clear Referee

In 2012, the Control Yuan investigated the gambling issues in pigeon racing and determined that the Ministry of the Interior failed to urge local governments to require private pigeon clubs to apply for licenses or accept penalties according to the law. The Police Administration also failed to urge police units to fulfill their duties of investigation and reporting. The Control Yuan believed that this caused the problems of gambling and tax evasion on prize money to recur repeatedly. [^4]

The key to this investigation report is not to declare all pigeon fanciers as gamblers, but to point out an institutional void: the competition is operated by private pigeon clubs, prize money and betting involve huge interests, yet the government lacks a clear and continuous responsible authority. When things go smoothly, private rules can be called autonomy. When pigeons go missing in large numbers or prize money triggers disputes, responsibility is easily pushed back to private parties.

The Ministry of Agriculture's 2024 letter interpretation also brings the issue back to the basic principles of the _Animal Protection Act_. The Miaoli County Animal Protection and Epidemic Prevention Station publicly reposted this letter, explaining whether pigeon racing activities involve illegality must still be judged based on individual acts, whether animals are harmed, and the results of local authority investigations. [^5]

This legal position is important. It does not treat the word "competition" as an exemption from liability, nor does it use a simple ban to conclude all situations. What truly needs to be examined is: whether the organizer foresaw the risks, whether they allowed animals to suffer unnecessary pain, and whether they handled missing and disqualified individuals after the race.

## Where Do Pigeons That Can't Go Home End Up?

The low return rate in 2024 caused the problem to fly from the loft to the city. Reports recorded injured, dehydrated, and disoriented racing pigeons appearing along the east coast, and noted that some were discarded after their flight feathers were clipped or leg rings were cut off. [^1]

Another investigation pointed out that wild pigeons with leg rings are often lost or abandoned racing pigeons and breeding pigeons. An interviewee from a bird pest control company estimated that about 80% to 90% of Taiwan's wild pigeons originate from the pigeon racing industry. This is an estimate based on the practitioner's experience, not an official nationwide statistic, but it points to a downstream link that is easily ignored: the end of a race does not mean the end of a life issue. [^6]

The pigeon feeding scene in Sanchong, New Taipei City, makes this downstream visible. Some bring water, some bring bread, and some bring nets to find birds suitable for breeding. Feeders view pigeons as neighbors they cannot ignore, while pest control practitioners must handle feces, parasites, and human-bird conflicts. [^6]

From a data perspective, the leg ring is the clue that allows a racing pigeon to be identified even after leaving the competition site. It can tell people that this bird belonged to a certain pigeon club or owner, but it does not necessarily allow rescuers to quickly find the responsible person. When the leg ring is cut off, the pigeon becomes a bird without identity in the city. This is why post-race handling cannot only count the return rate; it also needs to leave records of missing, rescue, abandonment, and resettlement. [^6]

| Post-Race Scenario    | Trackable Data                                         | Institutional Gap                                                    |
| --------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------ | -------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Lost or injured       | Discovery location, leg ring, rescue time              | No unified reporting window                                          |
| Fed by residents      | Flock location, feeding frequency, human-bird conflict | Public health and animal protection responsibilities are dispersed   |
| Captured or resold    | Capture location, contact records, redemption demands  | Formal rules do not cover the intermediate state on offshore islands |
| Abandoned or ring-cut | Injury condition, presumed source, resettlement result | Difficult to trace the original owner                                |

**Curator's Note #3: Post-Race Is the Beginning of Public Issues**
_Once pigeons leave the competition site, they do not automatically become a public issue. They must first become feces on the dock, nests in school buildings, or a rescue post, before people can see the shadows left by the system._

## Another Kind of "Going Home" on Offshore Islands

In Xiaoliuqiu, lost racing pigeons may even form a local business. Residents interviewed by reporters said that some people partnered to catch pigeons, catching 70 to 80 birds in half a day. During the training period, pigeon owners might spend hundreds to thousands of New Taiwan dollars to redeem the pigeons. In the final stages of official races, middlemen may purchase them, with prices potentially higher. [^7]

Even harder to distinguish is the phenomenon of "running pigeons" (pigeons that are caught and returned for a fee). Reports compiled residents' and fanciers' accounts: captors return exhausted racing pigeons to the main island and attach paper strips to their legs, demanding 20% to 30% of the prize money. Some interviewed fanciers did not even consider this cheating, because in their understanding, without these people, the pigeons would not have been able to return anyway. [^7]

This statement reveals another side of the system. When the official referee only acknowledges whether the pigeon returned to the loft within the time limit, offshore residents, pigeon captors, and pigeon owners develop their own "homecoming" transactions outside the formal rules. It does not fit the imagination of fair competition, but it may be viewed by participants as a rescue in reality.

## The Referendum Did Not End the Problem

In 2024, animal protection groups proposed a referendum to ban sea pigeon racing. After the first phase of signature collection was submitted, it was rejected by the Central Election Commission at the end of the year. [^1] The Society for the Protection of Life subsequently explained that the referendum should be viewed as an animal protection advocacy to bring sea pigeon racing into public discussion, and argued for reviewing the racing system, gambling, and government supervision. This is the stance of the advocacy group and should be read separately from the Control Yuan's investigation conclusions. [^8]

The referendum did not make pigeon racing disappear immediately, nor did it render judgment on all controversies. The questions it left behind are actually clearer: If the competition can continue, who must publicly disclose the return rate every season, who must track missing pigeons, who is responsible for post-race abandonment, and who can make pigeon fanciers understand that "fairness" is not only established during the draw?

Pigeon racing deserves to be understood, but that does not mean all racing systems deserve to be preserved. The care technology of pigeon fanciers, the local memories of lofts, and the social networks of the underground economy are all part of Taiwan's life. Questions about animal welfare are also part of Taiwan's life and cannot be brushed aside with "everyone plays like this."

Finally, return to that electronic leg ring. It can prove when a pigeon returned, but it cannot prove what it experienced at sea. A truly mature pigeon racing system should not only calculate the time of the first arrival but also make the last life that did not return visible.

## Three Responsibility Breakpoints

The controversy over pigeon racing is often compressed into "whether to ban racing." But from the moment a pigeon is ringed, responsibility actually divides into three different breakpoints. The first breakpoint is before the release: did the pigeon club and pigeon owner fully assess the season, weather, wind direction, and the young bird's condition? The second breakpoint is during the race: is there a transparent standard for release decisions, and who has the authority to call a halt when encountering rain, low visibility, or headwinds? The third breakpoint is after the race: are there still people tracking missing, disqualified, injured, and abandoned pigeons? [^1][^5]

The 2024 Winter Qualifying Race recorded by reporters brought the first and second breakpoints into focus simultaneously. Nearly 90,000 pigeons were carried to the waters off Keelung, and ultimately only 7,712 pigeons completed the race within the stipulated time. From the pigeon fancier's perspective, this may have been a failure of weather judgment and landing path. From an animal welfare perspective, this also represents a massive loss of life's opportunity to return to the loft after the race began. [^1]

If the system only judges winning and losing at the finish line, it turns mid-course disorientation into blank spaces on a statistical table. However, blank spaces do not mean nothing happened. Lost pigeons may land on fishing boats, the east coast, city docks, or strangers' balconies. Some are rescued, some are fed, some are captured, and some are directly treated as disqualified competitors no longer of value. [^1][^6][^7]

The "running pigeon" phenomenon is unsettling precisely because it stacks rescue and extortion together. Offshore pigeon captors may return exhausted pigeons to the main island, while pigeon owners must choose between prize money, cheating, and saving lives. When formal rules do not handle the time when the bird has already landed on the island, informal transactions will fill the void. This does not mean all offshore residents or pigeon fanciers participate in it, but rather that a competition lacking public management pushes local residents into gray roles they would not otherwise have to bear. [^7]

The value of the official letter interpretation lies in not just asking "is this a competition," but requiring a return to individual cases to see whether animals were frightened, suffered pain, or were harmed, and whether local authorities can conduct investigations. This prevents pigeon racing from obtaining permanent exemption based on tradition, interest, or private autonomy. Competitions can have rules, but rules cannot exclude the _Animal Protection Act_. [^5]

The Control Yuan's early investigation reminds us of another thing. If pigeon clubs, police, local governments, and other agencies wait for each other, the problems of gambling and tax evasion on prize money will become a state that everyone knows but no one truly takes responsibility for. Pigeon racing is therefore not just an issue of animal ethics, but also a question of how administrative responsibility is implemented. [^4]

If "fairness" is to be preserved, we cannot only improve anti-cheating technology and prize transparency; we must also include pigeons that do not go home in the system. This includes publishing the return rate for each stage, establishing reporting and adoption channels for lost pigeons, leaving records of abandonment tracking, and allowing the reasonableness of releases to be subject to post-event inspection. These measures will not eliminate all risks, but they can ensure that risks are no longer borne solely by the birds.

## If Fairness Is to Be Preserved

To retain pigeon racing as a competition, the first step is not to invent a more complex betting game, but to make the basic data of the competition public. Every season should at least allow society to see the number of participants, the release positions for each stage, weather judgments, the return rate, the number of missing pigeons, and post-race rescue records.

These data cannot remain only within the pigeon club. They are the minimum threshold for judging whether a competition is acceptable and the common draft that allows people with different stances to have the opportunity to discuss the same issue. Without data, animal protection groups can only persuade the public with the worst-case scenarios, and pigeon fanciers can only respond with their own care experiences.

| Data That Should Be Seen                            | The Question It Answers                                               |
| --------------------------------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Number of participants and returnees for each stage | Has the risk of this stage exceeded a reasonable range?               |
| Release points and weather records                  | Is disorientation a contingency or a foreseeable decision result?     |
| Handling of disqualified and lost pigeons           | After the competition ends, who is still responsible for the animals? |
| Betting and prize rules                             | Is so-called fairness built on a trackable system?                    |

The second step is to turn "care" from a private virtue into a supervised obligation. Pigeon owners can love their pigeons very much, and pigeon clubs can invest in anti-cheating technology, but as long as there is no reporting channel for missing pigeons and no tracking for abandonment, love only stays when the pigeons are useful.

The third step is to discuss whether the racing system can be adjusted. Reducing sea racing distances, adding suspension conditions, and establishing post-race rescue and adoption mechanisms may not guarantee the safety of every pigeon, but at least they pull "fairness" back from prize rankings to whether animals are treated as lives.

Pigeon racing does not leave behind a black-and-white choice question. It is more like a small laboratory of Taiwan society, allowing people to see how people compose a rule set from luck, technology, trust, and money, and allowing people to see where the lives not covered by the rules will finally appear.
