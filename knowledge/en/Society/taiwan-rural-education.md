> **30-Second Overview:** Taiwan performs strongly in international academic assessments, but Teach For Taiwan (TFT) points out that children from socioeconomically disadvantaged backgrounds may lag behind their more advantaged peers by as much as six years[^1]. Statistics from the Ministry of Education for the 2018–2019 academic year show that there are 1,177 remote-area schools below the senior high level, serving 117,488 students, with an average class size of only 13.8 students—roughly half the national average[^2]. The problem with rural education has never been just "schools are in the mountains"; it is simultaneously a matter of teacher quality, transportation, housing, family support, community connections, and how the entire society defines success.

When A-Wei was young, he often skipped class. It wasn't because he was lazy or didn't want to learn. When his family's foam tea shop got busy, he had to go help. Later, a teacher pulled him back into the classroom; he scored the highest among all the boys in his class and won an award in a rural student painting competition[^3].

Stories like this are easily framed as inspirational tales: if there is just one passionate enough teacher, a rural child's life will be turned around. But real rural education is far more complicated. It is not that certain children are "more problematic," but rather that the entire system supporting a child's growth often unravels layer by layer once the child leaves the classroom.

If we must condense the perspective offered by TFT into a single sentence, it would be this: rural education is not about dealing with "problem students," but about facing students who are surrounded by problems. If the camera lens focuses only on the child, the full picture will never be clear.

## Not in the Mountains, But in the System

When Taiwanese society discusses education, we often first think of scores, admission rates, star high schools, cram schools, or the familiar competitive order within [[education-system-and-admissions-culture]]. This order is not without results. OECD's PISA 2022 Country Notes indicate that Taiwanese students' performance in mathematics, science, and reading is higher than the OECD average, and students generally feel that teachers are willing to provide additional help[^4]. The problem is that averages are very good at obscuring cracks.

TFT's public discourse states this crack plainly: children from more disadvantaged socio-economic backgrounds may lag behind their more advantaged peers by as much as six years in learning outcomes[^1]. This is not just "a certain township is struggling"; it is that educational outcomes remain tightly bound to conditions of origin.

The _Remote Area School Education Development Act_, promulgated in 2017, actually acknowledged that the problems of remote schools are never just about geographical distance. The legal definition of "remote area schools" incorporates transportation, culture, living functions, digital environment, and socio-economic conditions all at once[^5]. In other words, the state itself knows that rural education is not as simple as "the mountains are higher, the roads are longer," but rather a complete combination of living conditions.

The Statistics Division of the Ministry of Education also provided a clear outline in its 2018 academic year bulletin: there are 1,177 schools below the senior high level in remote areas, with a total of 117,488 students. Indigenous students account for 17.5%, far higher than the national average. The average class size in rural schools is 13.8 students, while the national average is 26.4; for rural primary schools, it is even lower at 10.6 students[^2]. Small class sizes do not necessarily mean better care. Often, it simply means population loss, pressure to operate small schools, difficulty in offering a full range of subjects, and teachers having to shoulder more roles alone.

> The hardest part of rural education is not that schools are built farther away; it is that the system supporting a child's growth often unravels layer by layer once they leave the classroom.

## The Three Circles of Pressure Outside the Classroom

One of TFT's important contributions is that it does not reduce educational inequality to just "children have poor grades." In their public framework, quality education includes at least three things: Access, ensuring children can obtain resources; Achievement, ensuring children truly learn key competencies; and Aspiration, enabling children to develop confidence, motivation, and the possibility of self-realization[^6]. To see these three things, one cannot just stare at the child themselves.

TFT uses a concentric circle model on its official pages to understand educational inequality, breaking the problem down into four levels: the child, the school, the community, and society. It specifically reminds us: the causes that trap children in inequality are diverse, intertwined, and mutually influential[^6]. This breakdown is important because it pulls the linear thinking of "blaming one person" back toward a more honest structural analysis.

The first circle is, of course, the child. Some fall behind in learning, some miss school, some go home to care for younger siblings or help with livelihoods, and some are stuck in language, culture, or trauma. These realities exist. But if the lens stops here, we easily fall into telling the story as "this child is not working hard enough."

The second circle is the school. Are teachers stable enough? Does the principal have administrative space? Is a class already multi-aged? Besides teaching, do teachers also handle administration, counseling, and act as the frontline for social work? A whole series of articles in the _Remote Area School Education Development Act_ address this: remote schools can have specially hired teachers, substitute teachers, multi-age teaching,巡回 (circuit) teachers, flexible administration, and residential facilities, because if special systems are not used, the configuration of general schools simply cannot hold up[^7]. The more detailed the law is written, the more it reveals how difficult the on-site reality is.

The third circle is family and community. Article 16 of the Act explicitly states that remote schools must integrate parents, non-profit organizations, universities and colleges, and community resources to provide early warning counseling, remedial teaching, learning activities, and after-school care[^8]. This article is quite candid: it admits that relying solely on schools is not enough; what happens after school is just as important as what is learned during the day. TFT's official statement aligns closely with this logic: only when families and communities cooperate with schools can children have a stable and consistent learning environment[^6].

The fourth circle is the one we usually discuss the least, yet is the deepest: society. That is, how we imagine "success." If the entire society still defines success as getting into a good school, leaving one's hometown, and entering certain respectable professions, then rural children start on a narrower path from the beginning. Because they must not only study, but first cross the uneven distribution of transportation, teacher quality, family support, information, and cultural capital, before finally competing for an endpoint that has already been defined.

## What a Rural School Lacks Is More Than Just a Teacher

Many people first understand rural education starting from "rural areas lack teachers." This is not wrong, but it is only half right.

Article 5 of the _Remote Area School Education Development Act_ stipulates that teachers specially recruited or assigned public funding for remote schools must generally serve for more than six years before applying for transfer to non-remote area schools; Article 7 allows the use of less than one-third of personnel expenses to hire substitute or specially hired teachers when teacher shortages are difficult to fill[^7]. Looking at these two articles together is interesting: on one side, the state tries to keep teachers; on the other, the state admits that even so, it may still be impossible to fill the positions.

Looking further, Article 11 allows remote primary schools with fewer than 50 students to adopt multi-age class grouping, with teacher staffing calculated even at a 5-to-1 student-teacher ratio; Article 12 handles branch schools, transportation subsidies, boarding, and dormitories; Article 18 discusses teacher and student residential facilities, because in some places, it is not just a matter of longer commutes—the daily commute itself does not exist[^7]. Putting these articles together, you do not see "small classes and small schools are happy," but rather a place stretching its institutions wider than urban areas to keep the school running.

Therefore, rural education does not end with sending teachers in. If teachers lack local support, they cannot last long; if children do not have stable care after school, daytime efforts will leak away; if the community continues to lose population, the school will be dragged down by [[taiwan-low-birth-rate-crisis]]; if indigenous schools cannot bring local culture and language back into the curriculum, education may even become a training that detaches from life, which is the difficulty repeatedly encountered in the [[indigenous-language-revitalization-movement]].

## TFT Is Important Because It Pushes the Problem Back into Public View

This is why [[teach-for-taiwan]] is important. Not because it found a panacea, but because it allowed more people to see for the first time that rural education is actually a structural problem.

TFT's official recruitment page clearly states its role: program members must become full-time primary school teachers for two years, systematically understanding the roots of educational inequality on the front lines, and responding to problems together with partners at the school, community, and even societal levels; during the semester and winter/summer vacations, they will continue to receive approximately 500 hours of training[^9]. From organizational partner pages and annual reports, it is visible that as of 2026, TFT has cumulatively sent out more than 465 program members, accompanying more than 7,500 children[^10]. It does not stand outside commenting, but actually sends people into the field.

But TFT itself does not package this as a hero myth. The first paragraph of their "TFT Perspective" page says it hopes to systematically梳理 (sort out) educational challenges in a complete and neutral way, and welcomes different viewpoints for dialogue[^6]. Do not frame rural education as one teacher not working hard enough, one director not caring enough, or certain parents not caring. The truly useful communication is to show the difficulties of each role, letting the public know this is a net, not a single line.

Of course, skepticism has always existed. NPOst raised several sharp questions as early as 2014: Is six weeks of training enough? Sending urban elites to tribes—will this become cultural invasion? If a child's困境 (predicament) involves economy, livelihood, and care, how much can a two-year teacher actually change?[^11] These questions do not equal opposition to rural education; they remind everyone: teachers are a key role, but not the only solution.

In the end, TFT's value may lie precisely here. It lets society see that a teacher can be an entry point, but cannot be the whole. If rural education is reduced to just "sending passion in," then structural problems are still shrunk back to individual goodwill.

## What Truly Needs to Be Rewritten Is the Imagination of Success

Rural education ultimately leads to a harder question to answer: What kind of adults do we want children to become?

If there is only one answer, then rural children's pressure will be heavier than urban children's. Because for many urban middle-class families, academic competition is already hard enough; for rural children, before competing, they must first ensure they can be stably caught. This is why TFT places Aspiration within the 3A framework. Education must not only give children resources and ability, but also let them believe their lives do not have to follow only others' roadmaps[^6].

The most easily overlooked point here is: rural education does not necessarily equal "sending children away from their hometowns." Sometimes, it is more like enabling a child to have the ability to stay, return, or freely choose whether to leave. Being able to speak one's own language, being able to understand the land one grew up on, being able to know that success has more than one shape—these all belong to education.

Liu An-ting's phrase "What do you do with your luck?" is often treated as TFT's representative quote[^3]. But if we pull the perspective back a bit further, this phrase can actually be asked in reverse to the entire society: What do you do with your system, your resources, your imagination of success?

If Taiwan still frames rural education as "sending passion into the mountains," the problem will forever remain at the level of inspirational stories. What truly needs to be sent in is not just teachers, but a more stable support net, a broader definition of success, and a society willing to admit that children do not grow up alone.
