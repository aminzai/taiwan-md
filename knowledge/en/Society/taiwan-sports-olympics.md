# Taiwan's Sports Development and the Olympics: A Team Called "Chinese Taipei"

> **30-Second Overview:** From Montreal in 1976—when Canada demanded a name change to "Taiwan," leading Chiang Ching-kuo to withdraw in disgrace—to Paris in 2024, Lin and Yang achieved the first men's doubles Olympic double. This small island has competed for forty years under an appellation without a nationhood. The same "Chinese Taipei" hat established at the Lausanne Protocol in 1981 required different flags, songs, and emblems for each generation of athletes, with varying weight attached to it. With 12 medals in Tokyo 2020 and 7 in Paris 2024, these are the best and second-best records; institutionally, the National Training Center was formally established in Zuoying in the year of the 1976 withdrawal, the Sports Administration was demoted from the IOC in 2013, and the Golden Plan provided NT$1.2 billion over three years in 2018—gold medals do not appear out of nowhere. The framework has not changed, but NHK began saying "Taiwan desu," and France 2 said "the Taiwan we are familiar with."

## The Unreachable "Taiwan": A Door Closed in Montreal in 1976

On July 16, 1976, on the eve of the Montreal Olympics opening, a delegation of 67 representatives from the Republic of China remained stranded. The Canadian government under Prime Minister Pierre Trudeau had only established diplomatic relations with the People's Republic of China the previous year[^1]. At that time, the IOC issued an instruction: participation in the Olympics was allowed, but compromise was required regarding the name, flag, and anthem—they could choose one to modify. Killanin, the IOC President, mediated, and the final arrangement was entry under "Taiwan": this was the first, and only, time the name "Taiwan" was placed on the table within the Olympic arena[^2].

Chiang Ching-kuo, the Premier of the Executive Yuan, ultimately rejected it. It was deemed a national disgrace, leading to the announcement of withdrawal. The delegation flew directly from Montreal Airport back to Taipei[^3].

Four months after the door labeled "Taiwan" closed, in November 1976, the Zuoying Training Center was formally established. This site was originally intended as an athlete training base for the 1976 Olympics, with preparations starting in 1975. Instead, it welcomed athletes who did not participate in the Games[^4]. The beginning of the National Sports Training Center is thus marked by this ironic temporal knot: a facility built for participation operated from the year that never materialized.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: A common online narrative claims that "the IOC forced the Republic of China to change its name." This narrative is convenient but inaccurate—the naming requirement in 1976 was put forth by the host country, Canada, and the IOC acted as a mediator. Blaming only the IOC misses a sharper fact: it was the first and last opportunity for the name "Taiwan" to enter the Olympic arena, and the decision to refuse belonged to Taipei, not Geneva.

The withdrawal was not an isolated event. The Republic of China withdrew from the United Nations in 1971; diplomatic relations between Taiwan and Japan were severed in 1972; and relations between the US and the PRC were cut off in 1979—the international stage was closing door after door. The Olympics was just one instance. On October 25, 1979, the IOC made a resolution via a vote in Nagoya: 62 votes in favor, 17 against, and 2 abstentions[^5]. Beijing joined under the name "People's Republic of China," using the PRC flag and anthem. Taipei remained, but its flag, anthem, and emblem all had to be different from the existing ROC versions.

The word "different" planted forty years of micro-politics.

## The Cost of Rewriting a Song: The Lausanne Protocol of 1981

![Olympic Flag of Chinese Taipei](/article-images/society/chinese-taipei-olympic-flag.svg)

_The Olympic Flag of Chinese Taipei, designed by Weng Ming-yi in 1980 and approved by Jiang Ching-kuo, used after the Lausanne Protocol in 1981 — Public Domain [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Flag_of_Chinese_Taipei_for_Olympic_games.svg)_

On March 23, 1981, in Lausanne, Switzerland, Shen Chia-ming, President of the Chinese Olympic Committee (COC), signed an agreement with Juan Antonio Samaranch, President of the IOC (who was newly appointed at the Moscow Congress in July 1980) [^6]. This agreement established "Chinese Taipei" as the official name, and three items were put on the table: the flag, the anthem, and the emblem.

The designer of the flag was Weng Ming-yi [^7]. He himself was a former member of the modern pentathlon team and served at the COC. In 1980, he submitted three designs, one of which was selected by Jiang Ching-kuo: white background with a single plum blossom in the center, and the five rings below. It later became known as the "Plum Blossom Flag" [^8]. It is not the national flag of the Republic of China, but it retains the outline of the Blue Sky with a White Sun. It was a single-use flag designated solely for international competitions and was never seen domestically.

The anthem's history is more convoluted. In June 1983, the COC approved retaining the melody that Huang Zi had composed for "National Flag Song" in 1937, and asked Vice President Zhang Bide to rewrite the lyrics [^9]. The original national flag lyrics were written by Dai Chuan-hsien in 1937: "Majestic mountains and rivers, abundant resources, descendants of the Yellow River, supreme in East Asia... Blue sky covers all land in red." — this version was clearly unusable. Zhang Bide's new lyrics were: "Olympics, Olympics, without distinguishing religion, regardless of race; to promote friendship, for world peace, youth from five continents gather at the Olympics..." [^10]

The tune is the same, but the words are different. The island entered the stage for the first time under the name "Chinese Taipei" during the 1984 Winter Olympics in Sarajevo in February and the Summer Olympics in Los Angeles that year [^11]. On the day Tsai Wen-yi won a bronze medal in the 60 kg category of men's weightlifting in Los Angeles—lifting 125 kilograms, 147.5 kilograms in the clean and jerk, for a total of 272.5 kilograms [^12]—the Plum Blossom Flag was flown on the podium while the melody of the National Flag Song was played, but the lyrics had been changed.

This gap between "same tune, different words" has been debated for forty years. On August 4, 2024, in Paris, Lin Yang and his partner won consecutive men's doubles gold medals. The Plum Blossom flag was raised, and the anthem melody was played on stage, and the Taiwanese audience sang along: they were singing the national flag lyrics, not the official song lyrics [^13]. Wang Qilin joked after the competition, "The Tokyo Olympics before didn't have an audience; it was just us two singing to ourselves. This time, the whole stadium sang along." [^14] Most citizens do not even remember what the official anthem lyrics were: this is both a gap left by the rewriting in 1983 and a way that the public has stitched it together through daily life.

On November 24, 2018, the "Tokyo Olympics Name Referendum" was held to propose applying for participation in the 2020 Tokyo Olympics under the name "Taiwan." The referendum did not pass [^15]. Before voting, the IOC sent three letters of warning, stating that if the proposal passed, the COC's membership could be revoked: meaning athletes might not even be able to wear the "Chinese Taipei" hat. From the memory of withdrawing in 1976 to the referendum in 2018, Taiwanese society learned one thing: this hat allows athletes to participate; take it away, and the door might close.

The debate during that year's referendum can be read as the tension without a definitive conclusion on the island: on one side is the memory of the door closing in 1976: since the name "Taiwan" was rejected by its own government at the time, it is reasonable to fight for it again now. On the other side is the reality of the competition: athletes cannot lose their chance to compete because of decisions made by adults. Kuo Hsing-chun, Lin Yun-ru, and Dai Zi-ying all expressed concern before the referendum, worried about losing qualification. Both positions lived within this hat; the result of the referendum was a temporary balance of two weights in that year.

## The Long Night of the 60s and the Double Gold in 15 Minutes: 1968–2004

![Yang Chuan-kwang winning a silver medal at the Rome Olympics in 1960](/article-images/society/yang-chuan-kwang-1960.webp)

_Yang Chuan-kwang, who won a silver medal in the decathlon at the 1960 Rome Olympics; this silver medal was designated as the first national treasure in sports by the Ministry of Culture in 2025 — public domain [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Yang_Chuan-kwang_1960.jpg)_

On September 6 and 7, 1960, at the Olympic Stadium in Rome, Yang Chuan-kwang (born 1933; Amis ethnicity, from Malan Village in Taitung) competed against Rafer Johnson, a UCLA alumnus and roommate, in the decathlon. Wearing athletic wear bearing the flag of the Republic of China[^16], he finished with a score of 8334 points, losing to Johnson by 58 points for the silver medal[^17].

That competition established the nickname "Asian Man" (or "Iron Asian"). It was Taiwan's first individual Olympic medal. On April 29, 2025, the Ministry of Culture announced that this silver medal would be designated as an "Important Cultural Property," making it the first national treasure in sports[^18].

However, after the medal from 1960, there was a long wait. In Mexico City in 1968, Chi Chung-jen won a bronze medal in the women's 80-meter hurdles with a time of 10.51 seconds[^19]; this was Taiwan's first Olympic medal for a female athlete and the starting point for the nickname "Flying Antelope." Then came the vacuum of 16 years. There were three consecutive gaps: Munich in 1972, Montreal (withdrawal) in 1976, and Moscow (boycott with the Western bloc) in 1980.

In Los Angeles in 1984, Tsai Wen-yi won a bronze medal in the men's 60 kg weightlifting competition under the name "Chinese Taipei," ending the 16-year drought[^20]. The era of "Free China" also came to an end after this bronze medal; the next Olympic medal would not come until Athens 20 years later.

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: The four phases of Olympic medal history are not a story of linear evolution—Yang Chuan-kwang in 1960, Chi Chung-jen in 1968, and Tsai Wen-yi in 1984; each was an isolated peak supported by individual talent, with systemic structures being almost absent. The real turning point is only visible after the 2000s, when the National Training Center was upgraded, the Sports Administration was established, and the Golden Plan was implemented, showing a shape of "systemic output." Condensing these forty-plus years into one timeline misses the long night of those 16 years: it was an athletic arena that could only rely on genius descending from above.

On August 27, 2004 (evening of August 26 in Athens time). Chen Shih-hsin competed against Diaz from Cuba in the women's 49 kg Taekwondo gold medal match with a score of 6:4[^21]. Fifteen minutes later, Chu Mu-yan competed against Salazar from Mexico in the men's 58 kg gold medal match with a score of 6:1[^22]. Within 15 minutes, Taiwan won two Olympic gold medals: and these were Taiwan's first and second gold medals.

This ended 72 years without a gold. The starting point for zero golds was not Yang Chuan-kwang in 1960, but Liu Chang-chun in Los Angeles in 1932; he competed representing the Republic of China to prevent Japan from registering under the name "Manchukuo"[^23]. The long night of 72 years was concluded by two athletes in 15 minutes.

Taekwondo was only officially added as an Olympic event in Sydney in 2000, making Athens 2004 the second official competition. In other words, Taiwan's first gold came from a discipline that had only been established for four years: this reflects the structural reality where individual events were relatively more favorable to small delegations like "Chinese Taipei." Team sports (like baseball, which was sometimes included and sometimes excluded, or basketball, where tickets were hard to get) could not reach the Olympic stage; conversely, the path to gold in individual combat sports was a comparatively low systemic cost.

## What is raised is not the flag, what is sung is not the national anthem, but who cries are we: The Gold Medal Project after 2013

![Logo of Taipei Olympics](/article-images/society/chinese-taipei-olympic-emblem.svg)

_Taipei Olympics Logo — Public Domain [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Chinese_Taipei_Olympic_Emblem.svg)_

On January 1, 2013, the Sports Committee was downgraded to the Sports Administration under the Ministry of Education[^24]. This is a critical and often misstated turning point: common parlance often refers to it as an "upgrade," but in fact, it was a demotion from a ministerial-level committee to an agency subordinate to the Ministry of Education, representing a decline in administrative level. The downgrade was the result of organizational restructuring, with the underlying judgment being that sports would be governed within the education system.

On January 1, 2015, the National Sports Training Center was upgraded to an administrative corporation, separating it from direct supervision by the Ministry of Education and adopting a corporate operational model[^25]. After 40 years—from its preparation in Zuoying starting in 1975, formal establishment in 1976, and renaming to "National National Athlete Training Center" under the direct management of the Sports Committee in 2001—it gained autonomous governance rights.

In 2018, the Sports Administration launched "Project Gold": a three-year program with a total budget of NT$1.2 billion, focusing on customized training for key athletes, scientific support, international training exchanges, and coach allocation[^26]. The first phase had clear goals: to enable athletes to qualify for the Tokyo 2020 Olympics. Of the 38 trained athletes, all qualified, 18 reached the top 8, ultimately winning 2 gold, 4 silver, and 1 bronze medals[^27]. Lin Yun-ru's training support in one year approached NT$8 million, a figure often cited in individual cases[^28].

> 📝 **Curator's Note**: Gold medals are the product of national governance projects, not nationalist slogans like "reflection of national power." Looking at the 12 medals from Tokyo 2020 and the 7 from Paris 2024, the thread runs back to the preparation in 1975, the corporatization in 2015, and the launch of Project Gold in 2018. During the same period, grassroots sports were squeezed by academic pressure, CPBL attendance fluctuated between high and low points, and the women's professional leagues never established a stable profile—the cost of concentrating resources at the top is the deficit at the bottom. This article writes about Olympic gold medals; what it does not write is how much time children have left to play in the same island.

The Tokyo Olympics, postponed due to the pandemic, were held in July-August 2021. The Taipei delegation won 2 gold, 4 silver, and 6 bronze medals, totaling 12[^29]: this was the best in the team's history. Kuo Hsing-chun won a gold medal in women's 59 kg weightlifting with a three-lift Olympic record (snatch 103 kg, clean & jerk 133 kg, total 236 kg)[^30]; Lee Yang and Wang Zhiqin ("LinYang pairing") secured Taiwan's first-ever badminton gold medal in the Olympics[^31]. An NHK reporter and Mayuko Kuda said during the opening ceremony: "Taiwan desu"[^32]: The Japanese deliberately placed the delegation in the 'Ta' group and not the 'Chi' group, which is an alphabetical sorting operation within IOC rules, being the first time Taiwan was listed before China.

In Paris 2024, there were 2 gold and 5 bronze medals, totaling 7, the best in the team's history[^33]. The LinYang pairing successfully defended their men's doubles title on August 4th: this is the first time a same-combination men's doubles pair has won back-to-back titles in Olympic history[^34]. Lin Yu-ting won gold in women's 57 kg boxing on August 9th, while being entangled in gender disputes by the IBA and supported by the IOC during the competition[^35]. Kuo Hsing-chun won a bronze medal in women's 59 kg weightlifting with a total of 235 kg, becoming **the first athlete from Taiwan to win medals in three consecutive Olympics**[^36]. Other bronze medals included Li Meng-yuan in men's trap shooting (trap): this was the first Olympic medal in Taiwanese shooting history[^37], Wu Shi-yi in women's 60 kg boxing, Chen Nianqin in women's 66 kg boxing, and Tang Jiahong in men's high bar gymnastics[^38].

Yang Yongwei failed to win a medal at the Paris 2024 Olympics after winning a silver medal in men's 60 kg judo at Tokyo 2020, stopping at the top 8[^39].

The French television channel France 2 narrated the entrance of the Taipei delegation in Paris: "Taipei, which is Taiwan as we know it."[^40] The same melody, different words, and a different flag: but the discourse power outside the stadium quietly flipped the label.

## A Story Not Included in the Five Rings: Baseball and the Gap of "Chinese Taipei"

On August 21, 1931, at Koshien Stadium in Hyogo Prefecture, Japan, Chiyaga Agricultural School (Chianong) advanced to the finals of the National High School Baseball Tournament (Summer Koshien), marking the first time a school from Taiwan had reached that stage[^41]. The final was lost 0-4 to Chukyo Commercial School, resulting in second place (runner-up)[^42]. Pitcher Wu Ming-chieh (1911–1983, Hakka from Miaoli, nicknamed "Qilinzi" or "Weird Arm") pitched all six games[^43].

Among the 14 starting players for Chianong were Japanese, Han Chinese, and indigenous people: the concept of a "three-race republic" was later repeatedly referenced[^44]. Coach Kondo Heitaro was a professional baseball instructor from Ehime Matsuyama Commercial School who transplanted the Japanese baseball training system to Chiyaga[^45]. When the team toured the streets of Taipei after the 1931 game, this scene was preserved in the National Cultural Memory Archives by the Ministry of Culture[^46]; and the film _KANO_ brought this history back into public view in 2014.

From the Japanese colonial period onward, baseball was Taiwan's most popular sport, continuing post-war as "Third Tier Baseball": In 1969, the Jinlong Little League defeated the California Santa Clara North County Briarwood team 5-0 in the William Porter finals, winning Taiwan's first World Little League Championship[^47]. For generations born in the 1950s and 1960s, waiting for broadcasts before their televisions at dawn was a collective memory.

But this collective memory has a hidden dark side. In 1968, the Hongye Little League defeated the visiting Japanese "Kansai Little League All-Star Team" 7-0—which was often mistakenly reported as the "Wakayama World Champion Team," but was actually an all-star team selected regionally. A post-game judicial investigation uncovered identity fraud: among the 13 players, 5 were found to have lied about their age (overage), and 9 used another person's school credentials to play (only 4 were genuine students); Vice Captain Hu Hsu-li and others received suspended sentences in 1969 for forging official documents[^48]. The media later dubbed it the "Great Scam of the Century." The beginning of Third Tier Baseball was half real talent, and half a patch sewn up by the system.

On October 23, 1989, the Chinese Professional Baseball League (CPBL) was officially established: the third professional baseball league in Asia after Japan and South Korea. On March 17, 1990, at Taipei City Stadium, the inaugural game featured Brothers vs. Uni Lions, with a final score of **Brothers 3:4 Uni**[^49]: Brothers lost. Chang Yung-chang was the first losing pitcher in history, and Tu Fo-ming was the first winning pitcher[^50]. The four founding teams (Brothers, Uni, Wei-Chen, San-Song) started play, establishing a firm foothold for professional baseball in Taiwan. Although the CPBL went through several match-fixing storms—the "Black Eagle Incident" in 1997, the "Black Rice Incident" in 2005, and the "Black Elephant Incident" in 2008—leading to severe fan attrition at its lowest point, total attendance surpassed 2.9 million in 2024[^51], marking a new period of revival.

Here is the question: Why is Taiwan's most popular sport scarce on the Olympic stage?

The history of baseball's presence and absence from the Olympics is as follows: The 2008 Beijing Olympics was the last time baseball was an official event (Taiwan competed under the name "Chinese Taipei," finishing 5th)[^52]; neither the 2012 London nor the 2016 Rio Games featured baseball; while baseball returned to the 2020 Tokyo Games, Chinese Taipei withdrew from the qualifiers: pandemic risks and several CPBL players were unwilling to travel during the event schedule[^53]; and there was no baseball in Paris 2024; **baseball will return for the 2028 Los Angeles Olympics with a contingent of 6 teams**[^54].

The ebb and flow of Olympic baseball is related to whether the IOC allows players from Major League Baseball (MLB) in the United States, and whether the host country "needs baseball to fill seats": this is internal politics of the Olympics, not an issue for baseball. For Taiwan, this means a structural matter: the anchor sport of national identity—baseball—simply does not complete its journey under the Five Rings banner. Our gold medal narrative lies in Taekwondo, badminton, weightlifting, and boxing, but the collective ritual of "watching games together" is found in the CPBL, Asian Championships, and the World Baseball Classic (WBC): the intersection of these two systems is disproportionately small.

On November 19, 2023, during the WBC qualifiers, Taiwan defeated Japan and the United States to win its first Premier12 championship at the 2024 World Baseball Classic: on that night, 26,000 people sang in unison at the Great Dome in Taipei, with Taipei 101 displaying the word "Champion": the influence of this victory far exceeded any Olympic medal from the same year[^55]. The island is running two separate sporting narratives: one is the Olympic framework of "Chinese Taipei," held once every four years, consisting of scattered events and female-dominated gold medals; the other is the "Taiwanese Team" in baseball, which plays every year, is male-dominated, and has a deep social base.

## Conclusion: The Hat, and How the World Began Calling It Taiwan

Wang Qilin said, "The previous Tokyo Olympics had no audience; it was just us singing for ourselves. This time, everyone sang along." He was referring to the men's doubles double gold in Paris on August 4, 2024—where the Japanese flag was raised, the anthem melody was played, and the lyrics of the national anthem were sung by those present. The framework was "Chinese Taipei," but the content had already been rewritten by the people there.

NHK said "Taiwan desu" (it is Taiwan), France 2 said "just the Taiwan we know," and while IOC official documents still use Chinese Taipei, Japan deliberately placed the delegation in the "Ta" group, and reporters around the world referred to this team as Taiwan. The flag, anthem, and emblem established by the Lausanne Agreement in 1981 remain in place; the hat has not been removed from their heads, but its flexibility—the different weight worn by different people wearing the same hat—has been stretched layer by layer through forty years of gold medals, withdrawals, referendums, and consecutive victories.

From the closed "Taiwan" door in Montreal in 1976 to the world calling this delegation Taiwan in Paris in 2024—what happened in between was a small island with a training ground spanning thirty-six thousand square kilometers, iterations of four governing bodies, a NT$1.2 billion plan, 52 athletes, and a song whose lyrics have been re-written to slowly stitch their name onto the seating charts of international competitions. For the next Games in Los Angeles in 2028, baseball will return, the list of gold medalists will be updated, and the hat might still be on their heads. But after reading forty years of competition, what is most worth remembering is this: when the flag, anthem, and emblem are constrained, all that can be changed is the content—that is what the people on this island have never stopped doing.
