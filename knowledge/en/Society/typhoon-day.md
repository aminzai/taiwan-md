# Typhoon Holidays: Whose Day Off, Whose Work?

> **30-Second Summary:** In 2001, Teacher Hsu Bi-lan (許碧蘭) of Qingshan Elementary School in Changhua County died during a typhoon, shaking Taiwanese society and prompting the government to establish a clearer system for typhoon holidays. But over twenty years later, a survey by yes123 found that 81% of workers had worked on a typhoon day, and data from 1111 Job Bank showed that 37.7% received absolutely no pay. Every summer, the moment a county or city mayor announces a shutdown, this paper conceals a deeper issue: this holiday has never been for everyone.

## The Ditch the Teacher Couldn't Cross

On the day Typhoon Taozhi (桃芝) arrived in late July 2001, Teacher Hsu Bi-lan was at Qingshan Elementary School in Changhua County.[^1]

The medium-strength typhoon "Taozhi" landed in Hualien and advanced westward. The rain in Changhua County was heavy. While moving around the campus to ensure student safety, Hsu Bi-lan accidentally fell into a drainage ditch and was swept away by the strong floodwaters.

She died. President Chen Shui-bian (陳水扁) attended her wake to pay his respects. This accident shook Taiwanese society and forced the government to confront: should there be a holiday on a typhoon day, what are the standards for granting one, and who decides?

But before asking "who decides," Taiwan already had a regulatory answer. As early as **July 1974**, the Executive Yuan's Personnel Administration Bureau issued the _Guidelines for Suspension of Office and School Operations During Natural Disasters_. The regulations were in place, but they were not taken seriously until one teacher failed to cross a drainage ditch.[^2]

---

## Mayors Gamble Every Time They Announce a Shutdown

After the Hsu Bi-lan incident, the government gradually established clearer decision standards. On **January 22, 2013**, the Executive Yuan officially renamed the old guidelines as the _Regulations for Suspension of Work and School Operations Due to Natural Disasters_, incorporating typhoon holidays into a unified framework for various natural disasters.[^3]

The current triggering thresholds are twofold: wind speeds reaching Level 7 or higher, or gusts reaching Level 10 or higher; or accumulated rainfall exceeding 200 mm in mountainous areas or 350 mm in plains. However, the people who truly drive the decisions are the mayors of various municipalities and counties—not the central government.

This design has taken unexpected shapes in practical operation.

A quantitative political study covering 35 typhoon events from 2005 to 2015 placed typhoon data and election results into a regression model, controlling for unemployment rates, crime rates, education levels, and population structure, concluding that for every extra day of typhoon holiday granted, the average vote share in re-election increased by **2%**.[^4]

The policy decision immediately felt by voters is politics. The typhoon holiday is one of the few issues in Taiwan where everyone experiences the policy's effect simultaneously—if granted correctly, everyone breathes a sigh of relief; if granted incorrectly, no one is happy. However, the study also found that incorrect decisions (granting a holiday when unnecessary or failing to grant one when needed) had no significant electoral penalty for incumbents. What does this indicate? It shows that voters look at "getting it right," not "accountability." Therefore, the optimal strategy for politicians is: wait for neighboring counties and cities to announce theirs.

This game reached a peak during Typhoon Kaimi (凱米) in 2024—setting a record of two consecutive days of island-wide holidays since 2001. Yilan County had the most accumulated typhoon holiday days due to its location and terrain, reaching **47 days**; Miaoli County had the fewest on the main island, only **31 days**.[^5]

---

## But This Arithmetic Doesn't See a Single Person

A survey by yes123 Job Bank of 1,330 workers found that 81% had worked on a typhoon day, with **65% being required by their supervisors**.[^6]

The version from 1111 Job Bank was more specific: 76% of workers reported working on a typhoon day, of whom 53.5% were paid full salary, but **37.7% received no pay at all**. 43.1% had no protective measures, and 12.2% only received verbal rewards.[^7]

These two surveys tell the story of daily life during typhoon holidays in Taiwan. At the moment a county or city mayor announces a shutdown, over one-third of workers are going out into the storm without receiving a single cent in compensation.

The top five industries with the highest attendance rates were: large PR agencies (**90%**), wholesale and retail (**88.6%**), medical/healthcare/technology (**86.7%**), catering/travel (**85.7%**), and agriculture/fishing/livestock (**83.3%**).[^8] This list is almost a directory of Taiwan's blue-collar and grassroots service industries.[^8]

> **📝 Curator's Note**
>
> The common narrative is that "typhoon holidays let Taiwanese rest at home." This narrative slices along a class line: white-collar office workers wait at home for the holiday notice, while workers in wholesale/retail, agriculture, fishing, and catering continue to go out during the same typhoon. The system's design is not flawed, but its assumption is: those who get "time off" have an office they can stay away from. People in wholesalers, fish farms, and restaurants are never part of this assumption.

---

## Migrant Workers: The Most Invisible Layer in the Structure

Nearly **800,000 foreign migrant workers** in Taiwan are mostly concentrated in manufacturing, construction, and agriculture/fishing/livestock—precisely the industries with the highest typhoon attendance rates.[^9]

The situation they face is more complex than the data shows. Most migrants are on a monthly salary system; being docked pay due to a shutdown directly impacts the amount they remit home each month. Coupled with language and regulatory barriers, it is almost impossible for them to proactively exercise their "right to retreat" or apply for typhoon holiday allowances. This does not even account for the fact that their work is often site-based—there is no option for remote work. Furthermore, typhoon holiday decisions are made at the local level, and migrants sometimes do not even receive notification.

The Ministry of Labor's regulations state in black and white: working on a typhoon day "should not result in deductions from wages, nor should it be considered an absence subject to disciplinary action such as withholding performance bonuses," with penalties ranging from NT$20,000 to NT$1 million for violations.[^10] However, the word "should" (宜) is a suggestion, not a mandate. Enforcement is limited.[^10]

96% of workers support making typhoon holidays paid leave, and 64% of companies also support it. But the progress in amending the law is extremely slow because "potentially severely affecting necessary services" is used as a universal administrative excuse.

> **✦** "For every extra day granted, the re-election vote share increases by 2%." This finding accurately describes the political grammar of typhoon holidays, but what it doesn't say is: were those 81% who showed up to work counted in that vote?

## How Much Is Lost in One Day? Different Algorithms Yield Different Answers

Before and after every typhoon holiday, various media outlets consistently cite the figure of "NT$31.0 billion to NT$31.5 billion lost per day." Where does this number come from?

The calculation logic is basically: using 2023's GDP of NT$23 trillion as a base, dividing by 365 days yields an approximate daily output value of NT$63 billion. This is then minus the portion that continues to operate on typhoon days (semiconductor fabs operating continuously, some domestic consumption), resulting in a net loss of about NT$31.5 billion.[^11]

Veteran media figure Huang Yang-ming (黃揚明) calculated it differently from an export perspective: total exports exceeding $430 billion USD in 2023, with daily exports over $1 billion USD, accounting for over NT$31 billion just from this item. The two algorithms yield similar figures but have opposing standpoints—the former aims to show "don't grant holidays lightly," while the latter aims to show "a holiday comes at a high cost."[^12]

The problem with this arithmetic is not the number itself, but the column it lacks: the human cost of those who worked on typhoon days. Does Teacher Hsu Bi-lan’s drainage ditch in 2001 count as part of NT$31.5 billion?

## The History of Typhoon Holidays is a Lesson in Human Life

The complete statistics of Taiwan's typhoon holidays tell another story.

Since 2001, there have only been **14 instances** of island-wide synchronized typhoon holidays (as of July 2024). The longest continuous holiday was during Typhoon Morakot (in 2009), with 10 counties and cities for 3 consecutive days. Typhoons holidays usually fall on weekdays, not the "multiple weekends" circulated online.[^13]

| Longest Consecutive Holiday                     | 3 days                 | Typhoon Morakot, 10 counties/cities |
| First Instance of Two Consecutive Days (Island) | 2024                   | Typhoon Kaimi                       |
| County with Most Holidays                       | Yilan County: 47 days  | Due to geography and location       |
| County with Fewest Holidays (Mainland)          | Miaoli County: 31 days | Geographic barrier effect           |

The difficulty in coordinating across counties remains unresolved. Administrative divisions do not align with commuting circles; the holiday standards for residential areas, workplaces, and schools may all be different. Scenarios like "parents go to work while children have a holiday at home" spark discussions on social media every few years. Various counties have attempted regional defense coordination, but typhoon paths and terrain make synchronization nearly impossible.[^14]

The system operates according to 20th-century administrative boundaries, but typhoons do not follow administrative boundaries.

## The Wounds of Agriculture: Typhoons Don't Follow Administrative Lines, But Losses Are Calculated by County

The discussion about typhoon holidays focuses on urban white-collar workers and offices, but the sector most directly battered by typhoons every year is agriculture.

According to Ministry of Agriculture statistics, the total agricultural losses in Taiwan in 2023 were **NT$24.276 billion**, with typhoons accounting for **77.16%**, or about NT$18.7 billion; in 2024, it was even more extreme, with total agricultural losses of **NT$52.651 billion**, and typhoons making up **98.88%**, almost entirely caused by the "Three Typhoons" (Kaimi, Santuaro, Kangrui).[^13a]

In 2025, Typhoon Danas (丹娜絲) caused agricultural losses of **NT$2.5 billion**: Tainan had the most (NT$929 million), with banana losses at NT$229 million, 445,000 chickens killed, and **2,454 utility poles damaged**, setting a record.[^13b]

Bananas, melons, citrus, and bamboo shoots are perennial victims of typhoons. Pingtung, Chiayi, Yunlin, and Tainan are consistently listed as disaster-stricken areas. Farmers in these counties knew the outcome before the typhoon holiday was announced. At the same moment the shutdown notice is issued, they are bundling banana stems with agricultural tape, hauling oyster cages ashore, or standing in rice paddies doing nothing.

This loss does not appear in the conspicuous daily GDP calculation, but it is the most overlooked face in the discussion of typhoon holidays.

## Typhoon Noodles and Stockpiling Cabbage: A Different System

Beyond this official system, Taiwanese people operate another civilian agreement before a typhoon.

Media worker Li You-zong (李又宗) said that thirty years ago, typhoons almost always came with water and power outages. The night before the typhoon, mothers would fill bathtubs, fill up drinking water dispensers, and go to convenience stores to buy batteries and candles; grandmothers would stock up on groceries at the wet market, preparing cookies, canned goods, and instant noodles. After the power went out, rice cookers couldn't be used, so the whole family cooked a bowl of tomato-sauce mackerel canned soup with an egg, neighbors exchanged different flavors of instant noodles—this is how "typhoon noodles" came to be. He said this habit is "etched into our bones, it is a symbol of having lived."[^15]

According to United News Agency's _Online Temperature Meter_ analysis over the past two years (2023-2025), the most frequently bought items before a typhoon are rice and noodles, followed by instant noodles in fourth place, and drinking water in sixth place. The phrase "a full rice bin equals full security" accurately captures an older logic: not trusting any system to protect you, so protecting yourself first.[^16]

As power and water infrastructure improves, students in grades eight and nine rarely experience typhoons causing blackouts, and this collective memory is rapidly experiencing generational gaps. However, the phenomenon of supermarkets being cleared out two days before a typhoon never disappears. People may debate the effectiveness of the typhoon holiday system, but "typhoon noodles" are a language everyone understands.

> **📝 Curator's Note**
>
> The official typhoon holiday system and the civilian culture of typhoon noodles are two answers to the same problem. The former attempts to manage the impact of typhoons through law and administrative decisions; the latter bypasses any system directly, exchanging food stockpiling for a sense of security. The coexistence of these two answers shows that Taiwanese memory of typhoons is deeper than its trust in any system.

## The Flip Side of Typhoon Holidays: Why Some Say "Don't Grant Them"

The argument that "Chairman Lin Bo-feng (林伯豐) from the San San Association said a typhoon holiday costs NT$31.5 billion" is brought up before and after every typhoon season, backed by an industry perspective: a typhoon holiday is a luxury; Taiwan has an export-oriented economy, and factories losing a day means losses, customers cannot wait, and orders will be lost.

This viewpoint is not wrong, but it chooses arithmetic that benefits them. This same arithmetic omits the 81% of workers who showed up to work on typhoon days, the 37.7% who received no pay, and the vendors walking through puddles with umbrellas to go to wholesale markets.

Japan has taken a different path: in some regions, typhoon holidays are automatically triggered after the Central Weather Administration issues a special warning, which local governments execute according to standards, not individual judgments by politicians. Hong Kong's mechanism is more direct: when Signal No. 8 is hoisted, various organizations automatically initiate shutdown and school closure procedures without requiring an individual announcement from superiors.

Taiwan's system is mixed: it has legal triggering conditions, but the final decision rests with the county or city mayors. This design retains local flexibility while also retaining room for political judgment. Which people benefit, and which people continue to work—this is quietly decided within this space.[^14a]

## Changhua in 2001 vs. Regulations in 2026

After Teacher Hsu Bi-lan's death, the government did act. But there is a silent distance between "acting" and "changing."

The regulatory framework was established, decision standards were clarified, and the procedure for mayoral announcements became more public. Yet today, in 2026, when a typhoon arrives, wholesale workers are still going out into the storm, migrant workers are still being required to work without notification, and 37.7% of workers still receive no pay.

The starting point of the system was the death of a teacher. But the boundary of systemic change has always stopped at the border of public agencies and formal offices.

Teacher Hsu Bi-lan was a teacher in a public school, one of the groups ultimately protected by the system—it was because of her death that subsequent teachers had clearer legal protection on typhoon days. Her story was later included in policy explanations, written into media reports, and put into disaster prevention textbooks.

But every summer, when opening your phone to see the shutdown notice, those 81% are not waiting for this notification.[^17]
