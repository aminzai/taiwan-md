> **30-second overview:** In 1966, Master Cheng Yueh established the Buddhist Compassion Relief Tzu Chi Foundation in Hualien, with the earliest funding coming from housewives saving five cents a day.[^1] Starting from poverty relief, the organization later expanded its services to hospitals, schools, international disaster relief, and environmental protection; research on the 2024 Hualien earthquake revealed how its everyday volunteer network was transformed into disaster response capabilities.[^2] [^8]
>
> The most counterintuitive aspect of Tzu Chi may not be how large it eventually became, but how it turned "doing a little each day" into an organizational method that can be replicated across cities and nations.

In 1966, Hualien, Master Cheng Yueh and 30 housewives established the Buddhist Compassion Relief Tzu Chi Foundation with daily five-cent coin donations in bamboo tubes.[^1] Six years later, she saw how illness and poverty entangled the same families, and Tzu Chi began expanding from relief to healthcare.[^3] Groundbreaking of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital in 1983, opened in 1987; after the 2024 Hualien earthquake, academic research placed Tzu Chi within the contexts of "public-private partnership" and "refuge sheltering."[^5] [^8]

This timeline appears to be a story of an organization growing year by year, but what's truly worth questioning is: how does a five-cent coin become a system that can operate in disaster zones? The answer lies not only in donation amounts, but in how Tzu Chi broke down faith into visits, documentation, training, division of labor, and repetitive actions.

## The Five Cents in the Grocery Basket

Master Cheng Yueh's secular name is Wang Chun-yuan, born in 1935 in Taichung's Qingshui. After her adoptive father passed away, she began engaging with Buddhism, later practicing at Pingming Temple in Hsinchu's Xiulin, and became a disciple of Master Xingyun. This life transition made "becoming a monk" not just a personal cultivation, but gradually connected to a question: how can Buddhism care for real people in society?[^1]

Materials compiled by the National Taiwan History Museum mention that a Catholic nun from Hualien's Haixing Middle School once asked her: does Buddhism have similar social service arrangements as churches, hospitals, and nursing homes? This question did not immediately form a large-scale plan, but instead first fell upon housewives' grocery baskets and pocket change.[^1]

Saving five cents before shopping, fifteen yuan a month, became emergency relief funds after a year. The power of "five cents can save lives" lies in rewriting charity from the generosity of a few to an action that many can participate in every day.[^1]

📝 Curator's note: Tzu Chi's first important artifact was not a grand building, but coins placed in daily life that are invested into every day.

Tzu Chi's global official history page also describes early fundraising as "bamboo tube years": 30 housewives saved fifty cents daily before shopping, while six nuns produced and sold surplus baby shoes. This is the organization's own historical narrative, with details carrying clear symbolism; yet it still points to an observable organizational design: turning goodwill into fixed frequency, and fixed frequency into predictable resources.[^2]

## From Visit Registers to a Hospital Without Deposit Requirements

Tzu Chi's early work was not just delivering supplies to doorsteps. Master Cheng Yueh visited poor households, established records, continued to provide comfort, and attempted to help recipients regain self-reliance. This visit system made "poverty" no longer just a label to be rescued, but a living state to be understood, tracked, and accompanied.[^1]

After six years of engaging with Taiwan's poor families, Master Cheng Yueh shifted her attention to another cycle: illness caused families to lose income, and lack of medical care made diseases harder to treat. Tzu Chi Medical Foundation's English introduction summarizes this transition as the observation of "falling into poverty due to illness," and treats medical care as an extension of charitable work.[^3]

Groundbreaking of Hualien Tzu Chi Hospital in 1983; after opening in 1987, the hospital adopted a system of not requiring hospital deposits. The National Taiwan History Museum's materials indicate that this practice later led the then health authorities to order all major hospitals to abolish the deposit system. This was a greater turning point than "a charity organization built a hospital": relief began to confront the system itself.[^5]

Tzu Chi Medical Foundation currently states it operates six hospitals in Taiwan, and arranges medical professionals to participate in overseas services through the International Medical Association. This type of organizational self-description cannot directly replace government statistics, but it shows how Tzu Chi describes its role: volunteers, doctors, and international medical actions are placed in the same service chain.[^3]

📝 Curator's note: When charity enters hospitals, the questions change. It's not just asking "whether to help," but also "who is responsible, how to train, and how to turn one-time aid into long-term care."

## Disaster Zones Need More Than Supplies

In 1991, Tzu Chi launched overseas emergency relief after a cyclone in Bangladesh. Brookings' review of Taiwan's humanitarian aid also mentions Tzu Chi's participation in the 1999 Central American hurricane Mitch, the Kosovo refugee crisis, and the 2001 earthquakes in El Salvador, Peru, and India. These events gradually positioned Tzu Chi from a Hualien local organization to being seen as a Taiwan humanitarian aid actor.[^7]

But "going international" does not mean applying the same process unchanged overseas. A 2020 study published in Voluntas pointed out that Tzu Chi's experience in the United States brought two changes: new cognitive frameworks and learning about organizational forms. Researchers thus viewed Tzu Chi's internationalization as a process of co-evolution, rather than a one-way output path.[^6]

This observation is important. When Tzu Chi encounters different laws, religions, ethnic groups, and NGO ecosystems overseas, the volunteer culture formed in Taiwan must be re-translated. The translation target is not just language, but also practical issues such as "who can join," "who can represent the organization," and "how rescue operations collaborate with local governments."[^6]

Brookings' analysis also reminds us that Taiwan's humanitarian aid has always overlapped with diplomatic space, international visibility, and resource limitations. Tzu Chi's ability to enter disaster zones often relies on private networks and volunteer speed; but an organization based in Taiwan also faces limitations in cross-border access, political pressure, and logistics capabilities.[^7]

## 2024 Hualien Earthquake: How Everyday Volunteers Become Response Capabilities

The 2024 Hualien earthquake provided a closer observation window to today. A research team from Taipei Medical University interviewed personnel from Tzu Chi's Central Emergency Response Center and field volunteers, then used thematic analysis to identify five factors: disaster management, organizational capabilities, government multi-level collaboration, transformation of daily activities, and psychological support for victims.[^8]

This study's sample size is small, with only two and a half semi-structured group interviews, so it cannot directly apply every Tzu Chi rescue to the same result. Its value lies in pointing out something easily overlooked: disaster response does not start after a disaster occurs; everyday volunteer activities, material preparation, and cooperative relationships have long been accumulating adjustable capabilities.[^8]

The study also found that Tzu Chi considers workload and mental health when deploying volunteers, and incorporates psychological support into refuge sheltering services. Here, "care" refers not only to distributing supplies, but also to having someone to talk to and knowing where to go next in unfamiliar and chaotic spaces.[^8]

This gives new resonance to the five-cent coin story. Five-cent coins alone cannot handle a major earthquake, but daily repeated participation may form long-term familiarity with people, materials, information, and trust. Tzu Chi's scale ultimately manifests as whether the field can quickly find people, assign tasks, and connect with government and communities, rather than just donation totals.

## How a Religious Organization Becomes an International NGO

A study on volunteer networks included in Airiti Library, using participant observation and in-depth interviews, analyzes how Tzu Chi mobilizes volunteers. The study found that Tzu Chi transformed "serving the public" into activities that ordinary people can participate in, and maintained a tight volunteer network through information transmission, organizational structure, humanistic management, and organizational culture.[^4]

Another study on volunteer mobilization from a resource mobilization perspective breaks recruitment and management into eight aspects: activities, organizational vision, media, leadership, community volunteers, training assessment, professional division of labor, and hierarchy. These terms sound like management studies, but they actually answer how Tzu Chi turns "wanting to do good" into practical procedures for multi-person collaboration.[^9]

This is also a tension easily overlooked in Tzu Chi's story: its language comes from Buddhist compassion, but its operations must face modern organizational training, professionalism, information, and responsibility allocation. As the organization grows larger, the founder's original aspirations cannot single-handedly complete every medical, rescue, or educational decision; aspirations must enter institutions, and institutions must also accept external scrutiny.

Cambridge's research describes Tzu Chi's development in the United States as a case study of transitioning from a Taiwan NGO to an international NGO. This "becoming" is more accurate than "expansion": it implies that internationalization is not just adding a few more branches on the map, but that the organization must relearn how to be understood, how to cooperate, and how to prove its reliability in different societies.[^6]

## After Charity Becomes Institution, Who Writes History?

An organization active for over half a century will leave behind a large amount of its own archives, publications, images, and oral histories. Activities introduced by the National Palace Museum and the Taiwan Oral History Association mention that Tzu Chi began larger-scale oral history work about 20 years ago, and organized materials under the "Tzu Chi Collection" including sutras, history, essays, discussions, and legal codes. These records are important internal memories, and also remind readers: how an organization remembers itself is itself a social phenomenon worth studying.[^10]

Therefore, reading Tzu Chi cannot be limited to success stories, nor can it be concluded with a single label of "religious charity group." The founder's life, housewives' pocket change, hospital systems, overseas access, volunteer training, and government collaboration each require different types of sources. Tzu Chi's official history page is suitable for explaining how the organization understands its origins, while museums and academic research help readers place this self-narrative within the context of Taiwan society and international NGOs.[^1] [^2] [^6]

There is also a continuing challenge here: the moral sense of charity easily makes organizations appear beyond questioning, but the larger, cross-national, and religiously mission-driven an organization is, the more it needs to clearly explain how decisions, resources, and responsibilities operate. Tzu Chi's future is not only about whether aid can be delivered to the next disaster zone, but also about whether goodwill can remain transparent, learnable, and trustworthy within institutions.

## The Resonance Left by Five Cents

Returning to 1966 Hualien, the most memorable thing is not how much the five cents eventually accumulated, but how it changed the question of "who can do charity." When a housewife puts coins into a bamboo tube, she simultaneously gains a position: she can observe the community, participate in visits, and give her time to a larger action than herself.

Sixty years later, Tzu Chi's work already includes medicine, education, international disaster relief, environment, and humanities. Scale forces it to face professionalization, international collaboration, and public responsibility, but the original organizational imagination remains clear: turning abstract compassion into something the next person can do today.[^2] [^3]

This may be Tzu Chi's most interesting reminder to Taiwan. Public action does not necessarily start with a large sum of money; it may start with a fixed, tiny, repeatable habit; and if a habit can be properly taken over by an organization, it may go from Hualien's market all the way to global disaster zones.
