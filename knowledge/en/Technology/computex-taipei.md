# Computex: Two of the Big Three International Computer Shows Are Gone — the Last One Grew in Taipei

> **30-second overview:** Computex Taipei turns 45 this year. It grew from a 1981 export exhibition for small and medium businesses beside Songshan Airport into the stage that the world's AI giants must attend every late May and early June. Its two contemporaries — Germany's Hannover-based CeBIT and America's Las Vegas COMDEX — have both folded. The 2026 theme is "AI Together." Jensen Huang stepped onto the Taipei stage and opened with "It's great to be back home." That week, three CEOs — NVIDIA, Qualcomm, and Intel — took the stage in relay, and Taichung's HIWIN made its cross-industry debut, connecting harmonic reducers to the joints of humanoid robots. Forty-five years — from selling capacitors to selling joints — and this island has been doing the same one thing all along: taking the "manufacturing" that developed countries outsourced away, and doing it so well the world cannot route around it.

## It's Great to Be Back Home

On the morning of June 1, 2026, in Taipei. Jensen Huang stepped onto the GTC Taipei stage and opened with "It is great to be back home."[^1][^24]

The massive presentation backdrop behind him listed the shop names of Taiwanese street snacks — Wang Ji Fu-Cheng rice dumplings, Fu Ba Wang pork knuckles — with not a single chip model number in sight[^1]. A Tainan boy who moved to America at age nine and later founded the world's most valuable company in California[^2], returning to Taipei, called this place "home," called it "the origin of it all"[^1].

In that keynote he unveiled a next-generation compute chip codenamed Vera Rubin and declared it "in full production," and he showed off a chip called N1X, describing it as "the world's most amazing chip"; he talked about "useful AI has arrived," and about "agentic AI has arrived"[^1][^25]. He said one line that would be quoted over and over: "AI agents will be the largest users of computing."[^24] But what the room remembered most firmly may still be that backdrop with the rice dumplings and pork knuckles on it.

The exhibition he was standing at is called COMPUTEX — Taipei International Computer Show — 45 years old this year. And he wasn't the only one flooding into Taipei that week. Earlier that same morning, Qualcomm's Cristiano Amon delivered the opening keynote at Nangang Exhibition Hall 2, framing this year as the "Year of Agents" and announcing Dragonfly, the new data center brand[^26]. A few hours later, Lip-Bu Tan (陳立武), just three months into his tenure as Intel CEO, took the Computex stage for the first time as head of Intel, opening with "x86 has powered data centers for more than five decades"[^27]. Three companies, three keynotes, a combined market cap exceeding ten trillion USD[^3].

AMD's Lisa Su did not give a keynote this year — her second consecutive year of skipping this slot. But she spent five days in Taiwan in late May, announcing an additional ten-billion-USD investment plan for Taiwan[^28].

> **📝 Curator's note**
> A question worth pausing on: why do a handful of the world's most valuable tech companies choose Taipei — a computer show that began in 1981 beside Songshan Airport — to announce their most critical products? The going explanation is "the Jensen Huang whirlwind," a Taiwanese-American celebrity CEO who brings the world's attention to Taiwan. But that explanation gets cause and effect backwards. What Jensen Huang came back to is a place that could not be routed around in the first place. To see this clearly, you have to start with that small, unpromising exhibition from 45 years ago.

## The Export Exhibition Beside Songshan Airport

In 1981, Taiwan's personal computer industry was only just budding. The first edition of the exhibition that year had a very plain name — the "Taipei Computer Show" — held at the TAITRA exhibition hall beside Songshan Airport, organized by the Taipei Computer Association[^4][^5].

Its function at the start was very practical: give Taiwan's newly emerging small computer businesses a booth to lay out their motherboards and components, and sell to foreign buyers who came to Taiwan to source[^5]. It was the "money-flooded-Taiwan" era, and the electronics-parts culture of Guanghua Market was about to spill over into an export business — and this exhibition was that business's annual gathering point.

By the second edition, there were only 40 exhibitors[^4]. No one imagined that, more than four decades later, that number would become 1,500 companies and 6,000 booths[^6].

## The Year Stan Shih Changed the Name

![Stan Shih at the 2014 Taipei IT Month press event, in a dark suit, surrounded by a row of microphones and cameras, with the IT Month exhibition floor in the background](/article-images/technology/stan-shih-taipei-it-month-2014.webp)
_Stan Shih, founder of Acer. In 1984 he officially renamed the "Taipei Computer Show" to COMPUTEX TAIPEI, hanging a world-facing sign on this exhibition; the photo is from his 2014 press interview at Taipei IT Month. Photo: Tony Tseng, 2014-12-05. [License via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Stan_Shih_at_Taipei_IT_Month_20141205a.jpg)._

1984 was a turning point. According to Wikipedia and multiple reports, the then-chairman of the Taipei Computer Association, Stan Shih (施振榮) — the person who would later found Acer — approved renaming the exhibition's English name to "COMPUTEX TAIPEI"[^4][^5]. A local engineer's naming decision hung a world-facing sign on an exhibition that had until then only served Taiwan's own manufacturers.

The following year, starting with the 5th edition in 1985, the semi-official Taiwan External Trade Development Council (TAITRA) joined as co-organizer, and the Chinese name was updated to "Taipei International Computer Show"[^4]. From then on it had two engines: one, a private industry association that understood the sector and stayed close to manufacturers' needs; the other, a trade promotion body with the resources to push the exhibition internationally. With both engines turning together, the show began to climb.

By 1989, the industry generally agreed it had become Asia's largest computer exhibition, ranked worldwide only behind Germany's Hannover CeBIT and America's COMDEX[^4]. Those were the golden years of Taiwan's PC OEM boom, and future global brands like ASUS, Gigabyte, and MSI all first walked out into the world through booths at this show.

> **💡 Did you know?**
> Computex's venues have "grown" outward piece by piece. Beside Songshan Airport in 1981, then settled at Taipei World Trade Center (TWTC) Hall 1 on Xinyi Road in 1986; in 2003 delayed to September because of SARS and folded TWTC Hall 3 in for the first time; four halls opened together in 2004; from 2008 onwards, Nangang Exhibition Hall 1 (total investment about USD 110 million) was folded in as well[^4][^29]. It's like a tree that keeps sprouting new branches — the pot has never been changed. In 2026 the show "returns to TWTC Hall 1" for the first time to set up an AI robotics zone, with both venues open in parallel[^14].

## Three Big Computer Shows — Two of Them Died

Entering the 2000s, the balance among the world's three big computer shows began to tip.

The first to fall was America's COMDEX. It began in 1979 in Las Vegas and peaked in 1996: 225,000 visitors, 2,337 exhibitors[^30]. But after the dot-com bust and 9/11, IBM, Apple, Compaq and other majors shifted to private invitation-only launch events one after another, and the demand-side purpose — reaching resellers through this show — collapsed entirely. The 2003 edition had only 500 exhibitors and 40,000 visitors, an 80% collapse from the peak[^30][^31]. The following June, organizer MediaLive announced a "postponement," and the show has never been held again since[^32].

![CeBIT 2005 in Hanover, Germany: crowds inside the exhibition hall, with wide aisles lined by IT vendors on both sides, high ceilings, and banners everywhere — the scale of what was then the world's number-one computer show](/article-images/technology/cebit-hannover-2005.webp)
_CeBIT 2005 in Hanover, Germany. At the height of the dot-com boom it once reached 850,000 attendees — undisputed world number one; in 2018 it announced it was going into the history books. Photo: Florian K, 2005-03-16. [License via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Cebit_Hannover_2004.JPG)._

CeBIT died more slowly, but ended the same way. It spun off from the Hannover Messe industrial fair to become independent in 1986, and was once the world's number one[^33]. Thirty-two years later, on November 28, 2018, organizer Deutsche Messe announced: "due to declining visitor and exhibitioner attendance, CeBIT would be canceled for the foreseeable future"[^7][^34].

Two shows, different causes of death, but structurally the same position: both were "demand-side" shows. COMDEX sold to American buyers, media, resellers; CeBIT sold to Europe. When the manufacturing center of gravity for semiconductors and PCs shifted inch by inch to Asia, it made more sense for European buyers to fly straight to Taipei than to fly to Hannover, and American journalists writing up product launches no longer needed to cram into Las Vegas. The demand-side shows emptied out first.

Computex didn't die, because it grew on the supply side. Behind the city of Taipei sit 1,500 exhibitors, 6,000 booths, and the full chain from wafer, packaging, PCB, connectors, all the way to assembly OEM[^6]. If buyers don't come here, they don't meet the engineers, don't see next-generation cooling solutions, don't get to touch the OEMs that can turn samples around in three months. Of everything called a "world-class computer show," the one that survived happens to have grown on the manufacturing end.

One qualification: when we say "of the big three computer shows, only Taipei is left," it needs to be precise — America's CES (in Las Vegas) still opens every January, but it long ago pivoted from a computer show to a broader consumer electronics event; Barcelona's MWC took over as the main stage of the mobile communications era; the data center world still has OCP Global Summit, and the supercomputing world still has SC. The reason Computex is "the one that's left" is limited to the subcategory of "general-purpose computer shows tied to manufacturing" — not to say every other big tech show on the planet has folded.

## 2016 — "Your Moment of Zen"

Looking back, Computex was never a straight upward line. In 2012, global PC shipments dropped year-over-year for the first time, and smartphones scooped up the consumer end's attention in one bowl[^35]. Intel's 14nm Broadwell process slipped from a planned Q2 2014 to a Q4 2014 shipping date, and the "Tick-Tock" rhythm was rewritten into a three-year cycle from that point on[^36][^37]. Those years, the Computex booths were shrouded in a "PC is dead" shadow.

And in the very deepest year of the PC winter — on Wednesday morning, June 1, 2016 — AMD's CEO Lisa Su walked onto the main Computex stage.

She wasn't there to put out the fire. She was there to swap the engine.

On stage she uncapped an 8-core processor, codenamed Summit Ridge, meant for desktop PCs, with an architecture called Zen. Then she said the line that PC enthusiast circles would later treat as a coming-of-age declaration: "Zen is delivering 40 percent more IPC than our previous processor." Zen's instructions per clock — 40% more than the previous generation[^38]. She added: "Zen is a from scratch architectural design, for those of us who do those you know it takes a lot of work." Zen is a from-scratch new architecture, and anyone who's built one knows how much work that takes[^39][^38].

The media didn't quite believe it in the moment. AMD had been thrashed by Intel through the Bulldozer generation; the last few years had left it only the low end, with brand value at rock bottom. "From scratch," "40 percent IPC" — those promises had been heard too many times in the past decade. Wccftech, PC Perspective, TweakTown — their headlines that same day read like a gamble: "Here It Is! Your Moment of Zen!"[^38][^39][^40]

Then Zen actually delivered. On March 2 the following year, the Ryzen 7 1700X shipped, and Intel's high-end desktop market started bleeding. AMD's market cap, which was under USD 3 billion when Lisa Su took over in 2014, climbed all the way to the hundred-billion tier by 2020. At the Computex booths, motherboard makers had things to sell again, cooling vendors had orders to take again, and PC DIY enthusiasts had a reason to fly to Taipei every May again.

Lisa Su was born in Tainan (in 1969) and moved with her family to Queens, New York at age three; her father was a statistician, her mother an accountant[^41]. Incidentally, Jensen Huang is her first cousin once removed — Jensen Huang's mother is Lisa Su's grandfather's younger sister[^42]. The two CEOs who rewrote the geography of the computing industry share a family origin in Tainan.

That 2016 keynote was the key foreshadowing of Computex's escape from the PC winter. But it would be seven more years — 2023 — before Jensen Huang would walk onto the Computex stage and say "We're back"[^9] — the year he unveiled the Grace Hopper superchip, and used NVLink to string together 256 H100s into a 1-exaflop DGX GH200, bringing generative AI into every data center. Computex was thereby fished out of the PC winter and became the AI main stage.

> **💡 Did you know?**
> Computex also began hosting the InnoVEX startup exhibition in 2016. The first edition had 217 startups from 22 countries, and TAITRA EVP Walter Yeh said at the time it "completely surpassed our expectations"[^43]. It expanded the show's scope from mature PC hardware out to AI, IoT, and startups. By 2026, InnoVEX had 23 countries and nearly 500 startups — an 11-year record[^6]. Computex's next body was quietly growing at the booth next door back then.

## The Mirror of Info Month: Same Island, Two Shows

This island had another computer show — one that didn't survive.

Info Month (資訊月). Launched in 1980 by the government as "Info Week," renamed "Info Month" in 1985, held every December at TWTC — selling computers to ordinary households. A PC, a printer, a boxed software bundle, timed to year-end paychecks and Lunar New Year red envelopes. That was the show that seventh, eighth, and ninth graders remember tagging along to with their dads[^44].

In 2001, the Taipei Computer Association handed the hosting duties of Info Month over to a coalition of eight private trade associations[^44]. Then its curve started bending down. After the 2010s — with e-commerce rising, phones replacing home PCs, the consumer base fragmenting — Info Month's role shifted from "annual big worship" to just one more slot squeezed in between camera-gear fairs, auto shows, and e-commerce promotions.

Two shows, both held on the same island. One in May, one in December; one B2B, one B2C; one selling to foreign buyers who came to Taiwan, one selling to local household consumers. Computex kept growing bigger, and Info Month slowly bowed out.

The supply-side show survived; the demand-side shows are winding down. COMDEX, CeBIT, and Info Month are telling the same story in three different versions — buyers get old, buyers move, buyers get scooped up by Amazon. But wherever the manufacturing stays, the next generation of engineers goes there. Taipei has never let the chips leave.

## The Six Thousand Booths Nobody Films

![Exterior of Nangang Exhibition Hall 1: glass curtain wall with a large COMPUTEX TAIPEI banner up top; crowds with badges gather at the entrance ready to go in. This has been the primary venue of Taipei International Computer Show in recent years](/article-images/technology/computex-nangang-exterior-2016.webp)
_Nangang Exhibition Hall 1's exterior during Computex 2016. From 2008 onwards, COMPUTEX folded Nangang in as its primary venue; in 2026 the "dual venue" model had one wing at Nangang and the other returning to TWTC Hall 1. Photo: NVIDIA Taiwan, 2016-05-31. [License via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Computex_Taipei_at_Taipei_Nangang_Exhibition_Center_20160531.jpg)._

The cameras at every Computex point at the main stage. But walk into the actual show floor, and among those six thousand booths, the vast majority of the exhibitors' names you can't even recall.

They make cooling modules, connectors, chassis, power supplies, mechanical parts, passive components, test fixtures. Their booths aren't big — sales and procurement stand and chat, no show girls, no photo wall. Their customers are Quanta, Wistron, Foxconn — and one layer up, NVIDIA, AMD, Intel. Their competitors are scattered across Xinzhuang, Taoyuan, Taichung, Tainan — mostly sister factories, or same-trade shops started by a foreman who went out on his own.

These booths are Computex's body. Taiwan industry estimates that nearly ninety percent of the world's AI servers are assembled and shipped by these companies[^18]. But it isn't upstream money they earn. There's a self-mocking phrase in the OEM industry — "gross three to four" (毛三到四) — where gross margins oscillate between 3% and 4%, jokingly nicknamed the "Maoshan Taoists" (茅山道士, a homophone)[^19]. By contrast, TSMC's gross margin sits around 50%, and NVIDIA's around 70%.

And the more you do, the harder it gets. AI rack unit prices are high, memory costs keep rising, and assembly fees can't be raised proportionally — so gross margins actually get squeezed. Wistron's Q4 fiscal 2025 was 5.88%; Quanta's Q3 the same year was 6.85% — and this structure was repeated over and over on the 2026 earnings calls[^15].

The star lights on the main stage burn very brightly, but the booths behind the stage are still the skeleton of this show. Without them, no amount of keynotes can match the smell of engineering samples.

## HIWIN Bolts Reducers Onto the Joints of Humanoid Robots

One of the booths most worth pausing at in 2026 is from Taichung.

HIWIN Technologies (2049) has been making precision motion parts for over forty years. It started with ball screws, then grew harmonic reducers, linear actuator modules, and electric grippers one after another — its original customers were machine-tool makers and semiconductor equipment makers. On June 3, 2026, the Commercial Times headline read: "Precision motion powerhouse HIWIN Technologies (2049) makes its cross-industry debut at COMPUTEX 2026"[^22].

The phrase "cross-industry debut" is worth pausing on. A forty-plus-year-old Taichung motion parts maker, walking into a computer show for the very first time in 2026. What it brought out were three integrated product lines: a dual-arm logistics robot in partnership with an American startup; core modules for humanoid robots (the same ball screws, harmonic reducers, motors, drivers, and linear-and-rotary actuator modules); and smart grippers together with semiconductor smart equipment solutions[^22].

It's taking everything it had built over the past forty years for machine tools, and bolting the full set onto the body of that "physical AI" that Jensen Huang describes. At the first edition of Computex in 1981, the booths beside Songshan Airport were selling capacitors, resistors, motherboards — for Hong Kong buyers to haul back and assemble computers. At the TWTC Hall 1 AI robotics zone in 2026, over 180 companies used live hardware demos on site to show "the complete industrial chain from sensors, motors, reducers, all the way to system integration"[^23]. The same 45-year curve, with the definition of "components" swapping from capacitors to reducers.

TAITRA chairman James Huang (黃志芳) said he hopes Taiwan will "leap from being a global tech manufacturing powerhouse to being a 'global AI solutions center'"[^23]. In 2026, Foxconn, Pegatron, and other OEMs that originally assembled AI servers were also showing robots on the floor[^16].

## Saying Farewell to Taipei: the Players Left Behind

But at this year's Computex, some people felt left behind.

Tom's Hardware, in its 2026-06-05 Day 4 report, wrote the headline straight: "Computex 2026, Day 4 — the B2B shift, and we say farewell to Taipei"[^45]. The piece summed up the week's observation in one line: "Computex 2026 had far more B2B-focused companies in attendance, whereas in the past, the show typically featured strictly consumer products and companies that feed into that ecosystem."[^45]

On opening day, Tom's Hardware's own Jeffrey Kampman had already inoculated readers in advance: "In a world where nobody is launching truly new graphics cards, my Computex week will almost certainly be defined by data center products, integrated graphics processors, and AI accelerators of various shapes and sizes."[^46]

PC Gamer's observation went in a different direction: "Computex 2026 has really highlighted the fact that PC gaming tech isn't all about gaming anymore."[^47]

The evidence isn't just floor vibes. In Q1 2026, consumer DRAM rose 110% and consumer SSDs rose 147%; a 32GB DDR5 memory kit that was just over USD 100 a year ago was pushed to more than USD 350[^48]. AMD's RDNA 5 gaming GPUs were quietly told by their AIB partners to expect a slip to late 2027 or even early 2028; NVIDIA's next-gen consumer GPUs are lined up in the same window[^49]. On the show floor, the cooling booths were all doing liquid-cooling plates instead of RGB fans — and those players who used to come to buy high-end gaming rig parts DIY-style could sense there wouldn't be any real new GPU worth buying this year or next.

The main stage gave us physical AI, Vera Rubin, Year of Agents. The booths gave us liquid-cooling plates, reducers, humanoid robot modules. Those players who a few years ago were on the main floor hunting for new GPUs and new RGB chassis got pushed to the last two rows at the outermost edge of the show — or simply didn't come. Computex was fished out of the PC winter by AI, and survived. But this time, surviving doesn't necessarily mean every original customer segment is still inside.

## What Really Holds Up the AI Main Stage Isn't Data Centers — It's the Fabs Carving the Chips

The hotter the main stage, the heavier the energy bill.

![Exterior of TSMC's Hsinchu Science Park fab: a cluster of large, cream-colored clean-room buildings — the primary production base for the world's most advanced chips](/article-images/technology/tsmc-fab-hsinchu-2009.webp)
_TSMC's Hsinchu Science Park fab. What actually eats electricity on the chain that holds up Computex's main stage is the upstream fab, not the downstream data center. Photo: Arusanov, 2009, Public Domain. [License via Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Tsmc_factory_hsinchu.JPG)._

The Reporter's 2026-04-20 energy investigation delivered a set of counterintuitive comparisons: data center electricity in 2025 "compared with Taiwan's national 2025 electricity consumption of 282.8 billion kWh, still accounted for only about 0.5%"; while in the same year, "semiconductor manufacturing" consumed 42.38 billion kWh — nearly 15% of the national total[^21].

In other words, the chain that holds up the GB300s, Vera Rubins, and Dragonflys on the Computex main stage — what really eats the electricity isn't at the tail end. It's TSMC's upstream fabs carving out the chips. S&P Global estimates that in 2024, TSMC used about 8% of Taiwan's national electricity and 16% of the industrial sector's; by 2030, with wafer capacity 90% higher than in 2023, it could climb toward 24%[^50].

The AI end is climbing just as steeply. In its 2023 "National Power Supply Situation Report," the Bureau of Energy of the Ministry of Economic Affairs estimated that Taiwan's AI tech electricity demand "will increase from 240,000 kW in 2023 to 2,240,000 kW in 2028, a full 2,000,000 kW increase — an 8-fold rise"[^51].

The signals that the power grid can't hold up have been flashing for a while. In early August 2024, Taipower dropped a bombshell: no more new applications from data centers of 5MW or larger north of Taoyuan[^21][^52]. Behind that was a 20-billion-kWh supply-demand gap — northern Taiwan consuming 93 billion kWh a year against just 75 billion kWh of local generation[^52]. Deputy Minister of Economic Affairs Tseng Wen-sheng added: this policy "only targets data center facilities" — R&D centers and supercomputers are not affected[^52]. Taipei's computer show lasts four bustling days every year, but the annual electricity ledger of the island holding it up gets harder to close year by year.

## The Same Irreplaceability — Swapped from Shield to Target

For the past decade, the concept most often quoted about Taiwan has been the "silicon shield." Meaning: because 92% of the world's most advanced chips are produced in Taiwan, any armed conflict would blow up the entire global tech industry — so this island, in theory, wouldn't be attacked.

Since 2025 that concept has started flipping.

A joint analysis published on August 21, 2025 by CSET (Center for Security and Emerging Technology) and Lawfare wrote: "The more both sides treat advanced AI as a decisive strategic imperative, the greater the risk that Taiwan's once-vaunted 'silicon shield' could become a target."[^20][^53]

The piece went on to reason about Beijing's motivations: "Beijing may view going on the offensive as its best shot at accomplishing two strategic objectives at once: achieving 'reunification' and leveling the AI playing field."[^20]

Newsweek's January 22 book excerpt from Kerry Brown the same year also said: "In the current scenario, TSMC's position makes China covet Taiwan even more." "For all the talk by Taiwanese officials of the 'silicon shield' protecting them, reliance on it as an absolute guarantee of the island's safety and security would be foolhardy."[^54]

The same slab of irreplaceability — its symbol swapped within a year.

The Taiwanese response has been "China Plus N." Wiwynn already has 70% of its capacity in Mexico; Compal's factory footprint has stretched from China all the way to Vietnam, India, Brazil, Poland, Mexico, and — added in 2024 — the United States[^15][^55]. In August 2024, Foxconn added USD 241 million to expand AI server production in Chihuahua, Mexico; that October, Foxconn senior VP Benjamin Ting announced at Foxconn Tech Day that they were "building the largest GB200 production facility on the planet" in Guadalajara, Mexico — with a monthly capacity of 20,000 units and an annual output of 240,000 Blackwell-class servers[^56][^57]. Quanta likewise expanded AI server and EV parts production in Monterrey, Mexico[^58].

The supply chain research firm Z2Data titled its March 2025 report: "Why 'China Plus One' Has Become 'Anywhere but China,'" explaining: "What began as the cautious 'China Plus One' strategy — where companies maintained China operations while establishing backup manufacturing elsewhere — has evolved into something far more decisive: 'Anywhere but China.'"[^59]

The Federal Reserve Bank of Dallas's 2025 trade report added one more line: Mexico's automatic data processing machine exports have more than doubled in the past year, breaking through USD 85 billion in 2025; announced Taiwanese FDI to Texas in 2025 is estimated at USD 5.3 billion[^60].

Silicon shield and distributed footprint — this island is holding both ends at once. On one side, making itself harder to attack; on the other, making sure that if something does happen, the world can still be supplied. The prettier the CEOs' words on the Computex stage, the heavier this double burden weighs on the shoulders of the engineers at the booths.

## Taipei Is the Origin of It All

Behind that backdrop of rice dumplings and pork knuckles on June 1, 2026, Jensen Huang said one more line: "Taiwan possesses the world's best supply chain ecosystem."[^24]

When he said that, in the seats below him weren't only the media — there were also engineers, salespeople, and chairpersons from HIWIN, Quanta, Wistron, Foxconn, Auras, Sunonwealth, Acer, Pegatron, Delta Electronics. One layer further behind: the master craftsmen making PCBs in Xinzhuang metal-sheet workshops, the women workers soldering connectors on Taoyuan production lines, the next-generation successors of the 700-plus machine tool and precision motion parts factories within a half-hour drive of Taichung, the 30-year-old PhDs running processes in Tainan Science Park.

In 1981, the booths beside Songshan Airport were selling motherboards for Hong Kong buyers to haul back. In 2026, at the TWTC Hall 1 AI robotics zone, 180 companies used live hardware on site to demo reducers, motors, and grippers. Across those 45 years in the middle, this island has been doing the same one thing: taking the "manufacturing" that developed countries outsourced away, and doing it to the point that the world can't route around it.

COMDEX died because it was held in Las Vegas — the demand side of buyers, media, product launches. CeBIT died because it was held in Hannover — European buyers, European media. Info Month withered too, because it was held in Taipei but faced Taiwan's household retail end — and that retail end got sliced away by e-commerce and phones.

Computex survived, because it's held in Taipei — the supply side of this island. Next time you see the news "Jensen Huang visits Taiwan" or "three CEOs on the same stage," you can read it this way: it's the world's compute infrastructure returning every year to the place where it is actually assembled, to make its pilgrimage.
