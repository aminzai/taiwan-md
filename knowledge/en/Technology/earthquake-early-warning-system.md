> **30-Second Overview:** After the 1999 Chi-Chi earthquake, Taiwan gradually connected seismic observation, real-time communication, and campus disaster prevention. Today's strong earthquake alerts are not predictions, but notifications issued after an earthquake has occurred, utilizing the leading P-waves before the more destructive S-waves; the further away from the epicenter, the more seconds to tens of seconds might be gained, while those closest may receive nothing in time. The 2024 Hualien earthquake showed that what is truly difficult is not just "speed," but also accurately estimating intensity when the rupture direction changes.

# Wu Yi-min's Low-Cost Sensors Started with a Problem

At 1:47 AM on September 21, 1999, the Chi-Chi earthquake thrust Taiwan's seismic disaster prevention issues from textbooks to every household doorstep. This magnitude 7.6 earthquake caused over 2,400 deaths and over 11,000 injuries. Subsequent research and engineering reforms also turned "whether one more thing can be done before a strong quake arrives" into a national issue.[^1]

The research team led by Professor Wu Yi-min from the Department of Earth Sciences at National Taiwan University later developed low-cost P-wave alarms. It does not wait for the entire earthquake to end, but rather reads the earliest arriving P-waves first, then estimates how large subsequent vibrations might be. The key idea is not to make instruments guess "where an earthquake will happen tomorrow," but to transform an already happened earthquake into usable information in time.[^2]

📝 Curator's Note: What is most counterintuitive about Taiwan's seismic warning system is that the less it resembles a "prophecy," the closer it gets to truly life-saving technology.

Starting in 1991, Taiwan gradually built a free-field strong motion station network, accumulating strong motion data. The Chi-Chi earthquake left extensive near-fault records, allowing researchers to place seismic waves, ground response, and building responses on the same map. A review by National Tsing Hua University pointed out that this observational data later supported earthquake early warning, seismic design, and earthquake engineering research.[^3]

P-Alert, which began operating in 2013, formed a high-density network using low-cost MEMS sensors. The project page at the Institute of Earth Sciences, Academia Sinica, currently lists 786 stations. They not only issue local alerts but also provide real-time seismic information and waveform data, allowing researchers to quickly generate intensity maps.[^4]

The "low cost" here is not a synonym for cheapness, but a strategy of density. If every station required expensive instruments, the network would be difficult to deploy densely enough. When sensor costs decrease, more stations can be placed in campuses, research institutions, and different areas, allowing the system to see earlier where an earthquake started and where it was moving.

This density also changed the use of post-event information. P-Alert not only answers "where shook first," but can stitch together real-time signals from various stations into an intensity map, allowing disaster relief units to see potential affected directions first. Research on the 2025 Chiayi Dapu earthquake showed that P-Alert generated a detailed intensity map 36 seconds after the earthquake. Local sensors within 30 kilometers of some epicenters may also provide notification earlier than regional warnings.[^9]

# Warning is Not Prediction: P-waves Arrive First, S-waves Are More Destructive

After an earthquake, the P-wave arrives first. It travels faster and usually has a smaller amplitude. The subsequently arriving S-waves and surface waves often bring stronger shaking. Strong earthquake early warnings utilize this physical gap: stations receive the P-wave first, the system rapidly calculates location and magnitude, and then notifies potentially affected areas before the S-wave arrives.[^5]

The Central Weather Administration clearly separates this technology from earthquake prediction. Warning is a rapid notification _after_ an earthquake has occurred. Prediction attempts to estimate time, location, and magnitude _before_ an earthquake occurs, which is not yet something that can be accurately achieved in the near term.[^5]

For Taiwan, these seconds are not abstract scientific units. The Central Weather Administration states that current operations provide preliminary information and estimated intensity for moderate-to-large earthquakes within 10 kilometers of the island on average within 20 to 30 seconds after occurrence. For areas beyond 100 kilometers from the epicenter, a warning time of over 10 seconds may be obtained.[^5]

The distance thus determines both the value and limitations of the warning. Areas very close to the epicenter might receive the S-wave before the system finishes processing, creating a warning blind spot. For areas further away, although the message arrives later, it can still play a role in slowing down trains, shutting down factories, cutting off gas, or allowing people to take shelter in place.[^5]

📝 Curator's Note: The unit of warning is not just about "accuracy," but "what this ten seconds allows someone to do first."

Local warnings directly read the P-wave near the user and estimate subsequent shaking. Regional warnings integrate multiple stations around the source to locate and estimate magnitude, then extrapolate intensity for other areas. Both can form a composite system, prioritizing results that meet standards faster.[^3]

The mobile earthquake warning service from the Central Weather Administration also clearly indicates that notifications are sent approximately 10 to 15 seconds after the earthquake, and only uses data from some stations near the epicenter to gain speed, so the estimated intensity may differ from the actual experience.[^10]

This also explains why an earthquake alert cannot rely on just one mobile app. The value of the warning comes from the connection between stations, algorithms, communication, receiving end, and response procedures. Without any link, what remains might just be a sound on a screen.

# Hualien Earthquake Introduced Directional Issues to "Speed"

The 2018 Hualien earthquake became an important case for testing P-Alert. The research team analyzed the performance of the low-cost strong motion network during this magnitude 6.4 earthquake and demonstrated how high-density stations can rapidly produce intensity maps and local warning information after an earthquake.[^6] Academic reviews pointed out that in the 2018 Hualien earthquake, even after the regional system issued a warning, the P-Alert local network could still provide about 2 to 8 seconds of warning in some blind spots.[^6]

On April 3, 2024, a magnitude 7.4 earthquake occurred off the coast of Hualien. Subsequent research indicated that the Central Weather Administration system estimated a magnitude of 6.8 within 15 seconds. Because the estimation was low, the Taipei metropolitan area did not receive an alert under the original threshold, yet the actual intensity felt in Taipei reached 5-minus.[^7]

This is not a story of "complete system failure," but rather a harder question: when an earthquake rapidly extends in a certain direction, if the system only sees partial stations early on, the station distribution and data direction might cause the magnitude to be overestimated or underestimated. Research found that using only northern stations led to overestimation, while using only southern stations might lead to underestimation. How the stations are distributed directly affects warning interpretation.[^7]

The 2024 Hualien earthquake also reminded people that magnitude does not solely determine damage. A study comparing the 2018 and 2024 Hualien earthquakes noted that the 2018 quake was shallower, and the Milun fault rupture passed through Hualien City, causing very strong localized ground motion. The 2024 earthquake was larger in magnitude but deeper in origin and lacked similar surface ruptures, resulting in different damage patterns for both events.[^8]

📝 Curator's Note: A bigger number does not necessarily mean a greater urban wound. What truly determines how people are injured is also depth, direction, site, and building structure.

Research after the Hualien earthquake also pointed out that real-time warning and seismic data sharing networks help facilitate evacuation and response, but they are only part of resilience. Building seismic resistance, school reinforcement, local government decision-making processes, civil organizations, and evacuation experiences all determine whether the message can be turned into action after the alarm sounds.[^8]

# From Stations to Factories: Who Gets Those Ten Seconds First?

## High School Students Turn Warning Blind Spots into an App

Lin Rui began thinking "is there a way to know sooner when an earthquake is coming?" while in his first year of high school at Fudan High School in Taoyuan. He and Guo Chenyu did not start from science fair topics or teacher assignments, but by buying materials, assembling accelerometers themselves, and transmitting the recorded vibrations back to a server. This box, smaller than a power bank, cost no more than NT$800 per unit. Later, the two expanded their stations, forming a 15-person ExpTech team, and created the DPIP Disaster Weather and Earthquake Early Warning App.[^15] [^16]

When reported by Central News Agency in 2024, the team had installed over 150 self-made seismometers in schools and homes. DPIP transmits station signals back to a server and estimates the arrival time and shaking magnitude of seismic waves based on the user's location. It does not process "predicting" earthquakes, but rather transforms the time difference that national regional warnings have not yet calculated into a civilian observation layer that can notify users first.[^14]

📝 Curator's Note: Lin Rui's story is not about "high school students defeating the weather agency," but about breaking down disaster prevention technology into a problem a student can start: first making a sensor to get data back, and then thinking if it can provide an entry point for judgment before the closest person receives the official warning.

DPIP later did not just handle earthquake early warnings. The ExpTech open-source code describes it as a Disaster Prevention Information Integration Platform, integrating strong earthquake early warnings, real-time intensity, seismic reports, weather, and various disaster alerts. Its TREM-Net observation network is composed of two subsystems: strong motion observation and micro-seismic observation. This transformation is significant: an app that started in high school is ultimately not just a sound on a phone, but places observation, data service, and user interface within the same public disaster problem.[^17]

DPIP also made "whether civilian apps can replace official warnings" a flawed question. Lin Rui and his team still need data, reporting capabilities, and cooperation interfaces from the Weather Administration. Civilian stations supplement density, local signals, and end-user speed. Only when both parties know their limitations does cooperation not package those few seconds as "guaranteed safety."[^14] [^15] [^17]

The application scenarios listed by the Central Weather Administration are very specific: high-speed transportation can slow down, gas pipelines or life support systems can be shut off, factory production lines can stop, and computer hard drives can pause reading/writing. These functions do not require people to first understand the entire seismology; they connect the warning to pre-designed automatic controls.[^5]

Schools are another application. When promoting strong earthquake early warnings in the early days, the system initially used national primary and secondary schools as test and promotion subjects because schools have clear emergency responsibilities and opportunities to practice "what to do when the alarm sounds" during peacetime.[^5]

A review by National Tsing Hua University mentioned that Taiwan has gradually developed local, regional, and composite warning systems, extending messages to applications like high-speed rail, conventional rail, elevators, gas, electricity, and escape guidance. The question shifted from "is there a warning?" to "which equipment is connected, and which people know the next step."[^3]

More importantly, warning data can help prioritize when shaking has not completely stopped. An intensity map does not directly tell rescue workers which building will collapse, but it places station observations, ground conditions, and rupture direction in the same space, marking areas that need verification first. This makes P-Alert's value not just in the seconds before the warning, but also in the first information map after the warning.[^4] [^9]

For the general public, the most important function of the alert is often smaller: stopping running, staying away from glass, taking cover in place, and moving after the main shaking passes. If all expectations are placed on how far in advance the alert comes, it may ignore that building reinforcement, furniture securing, and peacetime drills are longer-term protections.[^5]

# Not Guaranteeing an Alarm is What the System Must Clarify

Strong earthquake early warnings have structural limitations. Areas near the epicenter might fall into a warning blind spot. If two earthquakes occur in quick succession, the system may struggle to distinguish the waveforms. Background noise from stations, communication quality, or human interference can also cause false alarms. The Central Weather Administration clearly writes these limitations into its technical descriptions, rather than packaging the alert as an infallible guarantee.[^5]

Research on the 2024 Hualien earthquake sheds light on another limitation: the system must make decisions when data is still incomplete, while simultaneously facing uncertainties in earthquake rupture direction, station spatial distribution, and magnitude estimation. More stable parameters, more comprehensive station configuration, and follow-up reports are all areas of continuous research improvement.[^7]

Therefore, "not receiving an alert" cannot directly equate to "no seismic risk," nor does "receiving an alert" mean "knowing where the disaster will happen." The warning is an entry point for risk communication; subsequent steps require collaboration from building design, community organization, traffic control, medical care, and rescue.

📝 Curator's Note: Mature disaster prevention technology is not promising that it won't make mistakes, but rather marking where mistakes might occur so people know what the next layer of protection is.

# What Taiwan Buys Is Not the Future, But a Choice

From the low-cost P-wave sensors developed by the Wu Yi-min team, to P-Alert operating since 2013, and to the regional warnings from the Central Weather Administration, Taiwan has accumulated not an isolated invention, but an experience of connecting seismic science to public systems.[^2] [^4] [^5] [^9]

This experience also carries Taiwan's unique geographical conditions: a small island, frequent earthquakes, high concentration of population and industry. Both station density and warning speed must be demanded simultaneously. For a society that cannot know when an earthquake will happen in advance, the most pragmatic progress is not finding a magical prophecy, but making every alert closer to reality, allowing every building to withstand shaking better, and letting everyone know what they can do with those few seconds.

The next time an alarm sounds, the system might only gain a few seconds for certain areas. But if those seconds are connected to the decisions of trains, factories, schools, and households, they are no longer just counting down. They are Taiwan's way of preparing in advance under conditions where earthquakes cannot be predicted.
