> **30-Second Overview:** Junyi Academy launched in October 2012, initially localizing content from Khan Academy through the Chengzhi Education Foundation. It has since grown into a free K–12 digital learning platform. During the nationwide school closures in 2021, the platform saw a peak of approximately 500,000 daily users; by 2024, official data listed cumulative registrations at 5.289 million and monthly active users at 271,000. [^1] [^2] [^3]
>
> What makes Junyi most interesting is its dual belief: that technology can break learning down into personalized paces, while also acknowledging that technology must enter classrooms, teacher training, and family support systems to avoid remaining merely a polished website. [^4] [^5]

These three numbers look like a smooth growth curve, but each corresponds to a different on-site reality: the first was translating foreign open educational resources into content usable by Taiwanese children; the second was the urgent search for alternatives by teachers and families after schools suddenly closed; the third is the distance the platform must confront between "having registered" and "actually continuing to learn." Junyi’s story is therefore not just about educational technology, but also about how a non-profit organization breaks down its vision into tools used by people every day.

## A Localized Video Finds Its Entrance in Taitung

Junyi’s origin is not a commercial curriculum designed from scratch. In 2012, the Chengzhi Education Foundation obtained authorization from Khan Academy, and together with foundation members and volunteers, localized a large number of English learning videos and interactive exercises. The platform began providing services in October of that year. [^4]

The core early problem was concrete: if a child’s location lacked after-school resources, could they repeatedly watch, pause, and rewind online, then use questions to confirm their understanding? This concept rewrote "the teacher can only care for the whole class at once" into "students advance from their own starting points first, and then the teacher sees who needs assistance." The foundation later organized this direction as providing free learning resources from elementary to high school, gradually incorporating the 108 Curriculum Standards, competency-based content, and collaborative courses. [^1] [^6]

The first important on-site was not an office, but Taoyuan Elementary School. A report from _The World_ CSR noted that Principal Zheng Han-wen brought the school, primarily composed of Bunun indigenous students, to try personalized learning as early as 2012. Taoyuan Elementary School subsequently became one of the early schools integrating technology into differentiated instruction, assisted by the Chengzhi Education Foundation. [^4]

## From Fang Hsin-chou to Lu Kuan-wei, the Platform Becomes a Foundation

Junyi was originally founded by Fang Hsin-chou and managed by the Chengzhi Education Foundation. In 2018, Lu Kuan-wei succeeded as chairman. The Junyi Platform Education Foundation was registered on November 21, 2017, and subsequently operated the platform full-time. [^2] [^6] [^7]

This handover is significant because it transformed Junyi from a digital project within an educational foundation into a non-profit platform with an independent organizational identity. Data from the Ministry of Economic Affairs’ Social Innovation Platform lists Junyi as a foundation-based non-profit organization, with Lu Kuan-wei as the responsible person. The data also states that the platform is free to use, and the organization relies on public and corporate donations for operations. [^7]

In an interview with _Manager Magazine_, Lu Kuan-wei said: "I really don’t want to sell the pity of children." [^2] This statement addresses Junyi’s fundraising dilemma directly: educational philanthropy cannot rely solely on depicting rural hardships to gain sympathy; it must provide content, services, and learning outcomes so that supporters know how resources translate into impact.

Therefore, Junyi began to distinguish between "how many people registered" and "how many teachers use it deeply." A _Manager Magazine_ report noted that Junyi once defined deep usage as teachers bringing at least half of their students to use the platform per semester, with a certain proportion of courses and learning time. In the 2022 data cited in that report, there were approximately 100,000 registered teachers, of whom about 2,000 met this definition of deep usage. [^2]

This distinction is worth preserving. Registration numbers can show that the platform is seen, but cannot alone prove what was learned. Deep usage is closer to whether teachers have integrated the platform into their teaching process. If Junyi only pursued account numbers, education could easily become traffic. If it only pursued short-term scores, it might ignore whether children are slowly learning to arrange their own learning.

## 2021: The Website Becomes a Temporary Classroom

In May 2021, Taiwan entered a phase of "schools closed, learning continues." Junyi organized materials from elementary to high school, across grades and subjects, within 48 hours, and held online teaching seminars. A _Manager Magazine_ report noted the platform’s daily peak reached 500,000 users, approximately 20% of the total number of elementary to high school students in Taiwan at the time. [^2]

The Junyi official page recorded that during the school closures, nearly 20 online live seminars and Q&A sessions were held, accumulating 104,592 views. During the summer vacation, there were 7 additional live seminars, with 12,500 teacher participants. [^1]

Behind these numbers is an often-overlooked action: teachers were not just finding videos for students to watch; they had to redesign tasks, confirm if students logged in, judge wrong answers, and handle family equipment and network issues. In a 2017 news release from the Taichung City Government regarding the opening of the Fenggu Elementary School Smart Classroom, Junyi seminars were placed in the same context as tablets, wireless networks, and differentiated instruction. [^8]

In other words, digital learning is not moving the blackboard to a screen. It requires schools to simultaneously have equipment, connectivity, teacher training, class management, and people who can interpret learning data. A document from the National Development Council on digital learning also pointed out that early teaching sites faced multiple thresholds including insufficient content, platform functions not meeting needs, bandwidth, and device management. [^9]

> **📝 Curator’s Note:** The pandemic made Junyi visible to more people, but it also brought a fact to the surface: the platform can handle large traffic in a day, but whether schools can long-term turn it into teaching remains another question.

## Videos, Questions, and Data: How Do They Return to Teachers?

The currently public Junyi website lists content including elementary to high school subjects and competency resources corresponding to the 108 Curriculum Standards. The homepage states there are over 41,000 videos and 108,000 exercises, providing different entry points for students, teachers, and parents. [^1]

Students can watch videos, complete exercises, and participate in activities at their own pace. Teachers can create classes, assign tasks, view error collections, and analyze reports. Parents can understand what content their children have recently encountered through tasks and learning data. The value of these functions lies not in turning teachers into data operators, but in changing "I feel he doesn't understand" into "he is stuck on this concept." [^1] [^7]

### How a Lesson Integrates the Platform into Teaching

If the platform is treated as a teaching tool rather than extra homework, teachers can start from course objectives, select corresponding videos or exercises, and then assign different tasks based on student starting points. After students complete activities, teachers can use errors and learning histories to identify common misconceptions, then arrange group discussions, individual supplements, or repeated practice in class. The core of this process is not letting everyone complete the same page simultaneously, but letting data assist teachers in deciding the next most worthwhile teaching action. [^1] [^7]

For students, the platform is better viewed as a "practice space where you can restart," rather than another scoring leaderboard. Videos can be paused and rewatched; exercises reveal concepts that are not yet solid; teacher feedback reconnects the records on the platform to real learning dialogues. This mode of use also echoes Junyi’s emphasis on self-directed learning and differentiated instruction, but its effectiveness still depends on school equipment, teacher design, and whether students receive appropriate companionship. [^4] [^8] [^9]

Research also reminds us that Junyi’s effects cannot be summarized by a single "using it will lead to improvement." Liu Yu-hung’s 2018 action research focused on 32 eighth-grade students at Zhongshan Junior High School in New Taipei City, finding that after introducing Junyi and differentiated instruction, the standard deviation of exam scores gradually narrowed, and students' learning processes showed a trend from awkwardness to proficiency. However, this is an action research on a specific class and period; it cannot be directly inferred to all schools. [^10]

Another master’s thesis completed in 2020 conducted a three-week, 12-class integer arithmetic teaching intervention for seven fifth-grade students receiving learning assistance. The study found that students had positive views of the platform and were willing to continue using it, but pre- and post-test scores did not show statistically significant improvements. [^11] This result is worth preserving: it separates "liking the tool" from "learning outcomes," and reminds us that the platform must be evaluated together with teaching design, time duration, and teacher judgment. The Junyi official website records feedback from a student at Keelung Jiande Junior High School: "Without using Junyi, I found learning difficult; after using it, I felt Junyi helped me learn, and I also recommended it to classmates, not just for myself but for others too." [^1] This sentence illustrates the help users felt, but cannot alone replace effectiveness research.

## "Free" Solves the Entrance, But Does Not Eliminate All Gaps

### A Set of Numbers Must Be Split into Four Questions

The 5.289 million cumulative registrants, 271,000 monthly active users, and 4,828 deeply using teachers in Junyi’s public data cannot be interpreted under the same denominator. Cumulative registration answers "how many people have ever created an account"; monthly active users are closer to "how many people are still using it recently"; deeply using teachers point to "whether the platform is brought into the course process." These three describe different levels of reach and usage; they cannot be directly added, nor can registration numbers be treated as learning outcomes. [^2] [^3]

| Indicator                 | What It Can Show                                                            | What It Cannot Alone Prove                                     |
| ------------------------- | --------------------------------------------------------------------------- | -------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Cumulative Registrations  | How many people have created an entry point for the platform                | Whether each account continues to learn or completes courses   |
| Monthly Active Users      | The scale of usage behavior in a specific period                            | Whether users truly understand the learning content            |
| Deeply Using Teachers     | The degree to which teachers integrate the platform into teaching processes | Whether all students thereby obtain the same learning outcomes |
| Learning Research Results | Observations under specific samples, periods, and teaching designs          | Whether they can be directly inferred to all schools in Taiwan |

This breakdown makes "impact" more honest. Philanthropic platforms need to demonstrate scale to show that resources are being used. They must also preserve research methods and limitations, avoiding mixing reach, activity, teacher adoption, and score changes into a single number that looks precise but is actually unexplainable.

Junyi’s free usage lowers the threshold for families to purchase textbooks and tutoring resources. Data from the Ministry of Economic Affairs’ Social Innovation Platform shows that Junyi’s main operational method is free platform, teacher training, and data analysis to assist in teaching students according to their aptitude. Public results for 2024 listed cumulative registrations of 5.289 million, 271,000 monthly active users, and 4,828 deeply using teachers. [^3] [^7]

But "having an account" is not equal to "having the conditions to use it." Children still need stable internet, usable devices, quiet time, and an adult who can provide assistance when stuck. Even within the same city, the after-school time and equipment families can provide differ. Rural schools may simultaneously bear limitations in teacher quality, equipment, network, and transportation conditions. A _The World_ CSR report placed this question in the 2021 pandemic context, noting that online resources can support school closures, but educational inequality may also be amplified by the pandemic. [^4]

Another challenge for Junyi is attribution of results. Student progress may come from platform content, or it may come from teachers changing methods, parents increasing companionship, schools improving equipment, or students themselves being more willing to practice during that time. This is why academic research must state samples, periods, and methods, and why the platform’s own annual reports should clearly distinguish between registration, activity, deep usage, and actual learning outcomes.

There is no simple conclusion here. Junyi indeed provides reusable free resources and shows improvements in learning processes in some research and on-site cases. But it cannot alone replace teachers, schools, and families, nor can it abbreviate structural educational gaps into a problem solved by an App.

## From "Watching Videos" to "Learning How to Learn"

### The Platform’s Daily Life Is Not Just a Row of Videos

Junyi’s interface breaks learning into videos, exercises, tasks, badges, and progress data. This design has a practical benefit: students can leave traceable records in one learning session, and teachers can also identify concepts needing supplements from class data. The official homepage divides students, teachers, and parents into different entry points, listing task assignment, error collections, analysis reports, and class data as main services. [^1]

But this design also brings new responsibilities. The finer the learning records, the more teachers need to understand the boundaries of the data. An incomplete task may represent unfamiliar concepts, or it may simply be that equipment, time, or family environment had issues. Data can hint at problems, but cannot alone explain why a child did not complete it. This is the most easily overlooked aspect of digital learning: visible records increase, but the real reasons still require people to ask.

### From Math/Science to AI and Competency Content

In 2024, Junyi platform public data indicated that content extends from elementary to high school, expands from math/science to English, and launches three AI tools. The official homepage currently also lists competency animations, financial literacy, semiconductors, and sustainable food era as collaborative or self-produced content. [^1] [^3]

This content expansion reflects that expectations for the platform in Taiwanese schools have changed. The early question was "is there a video to supplement math"; now it includes whether students can understand science, finance, technology, and sustainability issues in life. If Junyi is to maintain its original intent of educational equity, it must maintain the balance between content quality, curriculum standards correspondence, and teachers’ actual usability when expanding topics.

This expansion means Junyi is no longer just "online supplementary materials," but attempts to become a learning infrastructure connecting students, teachers, parents, and partners. For students, increased content means more entry points. For teachers, it means needing clearer course maps, task layering, and methods for interpreting data. The larger the platform, the more it cannot leave responsibility to students to explore alone, nor can it assume every teacher already possesses the same digital teaching conditions. However, the success of infrastructure is not only seen in how many people it can accommodate, but also in whether it gives on-site people more bandwidth: can teachers find students needing help faster, can children start from their own starting points without being labeled, can parents understand the data rather than being drowned by more notifications?

Junyi’s earliest question was "can a child obtain good materials"; today’s question is closer to "after obtaining materials, who accompanies them through the learning." This is the watershed Junyi must face: content can be massively copied, but understanding and companionship still require people. This is where the platform must continue to answer in the future: how to connect free content, personalized data, and real interpersonal support, so that digital tools return time to education rather than adding another system that needs maintenance.

> **✦** "Free" is Junyi’s entrance, not the endpoint of educational equity.

In 2012, Junyi started with a teaching video translated into Chinese. In 2021, it became a temporary scaffold for many families and classrooms during school closures. Today, the platform faces not whether content can be put online, but whether children can find a path willing to continue walking between content, teachers, and life.

If one day, Junyi no longer needs to use "rural areas" to prove its necessity for existence, perhaps that is not because rural problems disappeared, but because every child has already been treated as a learner with their own pace. The website remains just a website. What is truly returned to children is the right to start, restart, and continue learning.

This is the final standard worth leaving: Junyi is not used to prove that technology can replace teachers, but to test whether technology can return teachers’ time to understanding students, designing courses, and accompanying learning. When the platform’s data, classroom judgment, and children’s initiative connect with each other, digital tools may transform from content warehouses into part of educational relationships.
