> **30-second overview:** Junyi Academy went live in October 2012, initially Chinese-localizing Khan Academy content through the Chunghua Community Service Foundation, later growing into a free K–12 digital learning platform. During the 2021 nationwide school suspension, the platform saw a peak of about 500,000 daily visits; by 2024, official data showed a cumulative 5.289 million registered users and an average of 271,000 monthly active users.[^1] [^2] [^3]
>
> What's most interesting about Junyi is that it simultaneously believes technology can break learning into more personalized paces, while also recognizing that technology must enter classrooms, teacher training, and family support—otherwise it remains just a beautiful website.[^4] [^5]

These three numbers look like a smooth growth curve, but each corresponds to a different reality: the first was about translating foreign open educational resources into content usable by Taiwanese children; the second was about schools suddenly closing, with teachers and families urgently seeking alternatives; the third was about the platform facing the gap between "registered users" and "those who truly continue learning." Junyi's story is therefore not just about educational technology, but also about how a non-profit organization turns its vision into tools used daily.

## A Chinese-localized video first found its entry point in Taitung

Junyi's starting point was not a commercially designed curriculum from scratch. In 2012, the Chunghua Community Service Foundation obtained authorization from Khan Academy and, together with foundation members and volunteers, Chinese-localized a large number of English learning videos and interactive exercises. The platform began providing services in October of the same year.[^4]

The early core question was very concrete: if the place where a child lives lacks after-school resources, can they repeatedly watch, pause, and rewind online videos, then use questions to confirm whether they understand? This idea rewrote "teachers can only take care of the whole class at once" into "students move forward from their own starting points first, and then teachers see who needs help." The platform was later organized into free learning resources from elementary school to high school, gradually incorporating the 108 curriculum guidelines, literacy content, and collaborative courses.[^1] [^6]

The first important scene was not an office, but Pyuan-Yuan Elementary School. A report by _CommonWealth Magazine_ indicated that Principal Zheng Han-wen brought the school, which primarily serves Bunun students, to try personalized learning as early as 2012. Pyuan-Yuan Elementary School later became one of the early schools to integrate technology into differentiated instruction with assistance from the Chunghua Community Service Foundation.[^4]

> **📝 Curator's note:** What Junyi truly changed was not "putting the curriculum online," but rather breaking the learning pace from the average of one class back down to each individual child. The platform is the entry point; how teachers use data determines whether that entry point leads to new teaching relationships.

## From Fang Xinzhou to Lyu Kuan-wei, the platform transitions into a foundation

Junyi was originally founded by Fang Xinzhou and managed by the Chunghua Community Service Foundation. In 2018, Lyu Kuan-wei took over as chairman, and the Junyi Academy Education Foundation was officially registered on November 21, 2017, thereafter operating the platform full-time.[^2] [^6] [^7]

This transition was significant because it allowed Junyi to evolve from a digital project within an educational foundation into an independent non-profit platform. The Ministry of Economic Affairs' Social Innovation Platform lists Junyi as a charitable non-profit organization, noting Lyu Kuan-wei as the responsible person. The data also states that the platform is free to use, supported by public and corporate donations for operations.[^7]

Lyu Kuan-wei said in an interview with _CommonWealth Magazine_: "I just don't want to sell children's misfortunes."[^2] This statement directly addresses Junyi's fundraising challenges: educational philanthropy cannot rely solely on depicting rural hardships to gain sympathy; it must present content, services, and learning outcomes so supporters know how resources translate into impact.

Therefore, Junyi initially distinguished between "how many people registered" and "how many teachers deeply use it." _CommonWealth Magazine_ reported that Junyi once defined deep usage as teachers bringing at least half of their students to use the platform each semester, with a certain proportion of curriculum and learning time. According to data cited in that report from 2022, about 100,000 teachers had registered, with approximately 2,000 meeting this deep usage definition.[^2]

This distinction is worth preserving. Registration numbers can show how visible the platform is, but cannot alone prove what was learned. Deep usage is closer to whether teachers actually integrate the platform into their teaching process. If Junyi only pursues account numbers, it risks turning education into traffic. If it only pursues short-term scores, it may overlook whether children gradually learn to arrange their own learning.

## In 2021, the website became a temporary classroom

In May 2021, Taiwan entered a phase of nationwide school suspension with continued learning. Junyi organized materials from elementary school to high school, across all grades and subjects, within 48 hours, and hosted online teaching workshops. _CommonWealth Magazine_ reported that the platform's daily peak reached 500,000 visits, roughly equivalent to about 20% of the total number of elementary and high school students nationwide at the time.[^2]

Junyi's official page also recorded that during the suspension period, nearly 20 online live-streamed workshops and Q&A sessions were held, accumulating 104,592 views. During the summer vacation, another 7 live-streamed workshops were held, with 12,500 teacher participation counts.[^1]

Behind these numbers is an often-overlooked action: teachers not only found videos for students to watch, but also had to redesign assignments, confirm whether students logged in, analyze wrong answers, and handle family equipment and internet issues. A 2017 news report from the Taichung City Government about the smart classroom launch at Fenggu Valley Elementary School placed Junyi workshops in the same context as tablets, wireless networks, and differentiated instruction.[^8]

In other words, digital learning is not just moving the blackboard to the screen. It requires schools to simultaneously have equipment, connectivity, teacher training, class management, and people who can read learning data. A Ministry of National Development report on digital learning also pointed out that early teaching sites faced multiple barriers such as insufficient content, platform functions not meeting needs, and bandwidth and device management.[^9]

> **📝 Curator's note:** The pandemic made Junyi visible to more people, but also revealed a fact: the platform can handle massive traffic in a single day, but whether schools can turn it into long-term teaching is still another question.

## How videos, questions, and data return to teachers' hands

Currently, the content listed on Junyi's public website includes resources from elementary school to high school, corresponding to the 108 curriculum guidelines. The homepage indicates over 41,000 videos and 108,000 practice questions, providing different access points for students, teachers, and parents.[^1]

Students can watch videos, complete exercises, and participate in activities at their own pace. Teachers can create classes, assign tasks, view error collections, and analyze reports. Parents can understand what content their children have recently accessed through assignments and learning data. The value of these functions is not to turn teachers into data operators, but to change "I think he doesn't understand" into "he's stuck on this concept."[^1] [^7]

### How a lesson integrates the platform into teaching

If the platform is treated as a teaching tool rather than extra homework, teachers can start from curriculum goals, select corresponding videos or exercises, and then assign different tasks based on students' starting points. After students complete activities, teachers can use error patterns and learning processes to identify common misconceptions, then arrange group discussions, individual supplements, or re-practice in class. The core of this process is not to make everyone complete the same page at the same time, but to let data help teachers decide the next most worthwhile teaching action.[^1] [^7]

For students, the platform is more suitable to be seen as "a space for repeatable practice" rather than another scoring leaderboard. Videos can be paused and rewatched, exercises can reveal unstable concepts, and teacher feedback brings the records on the platform back into real learning conversations. This usage also echoes Junyi's emphasis on self-directed learning and differentiated instruction, but whether it's effective still depends on school equipment, teacher design, and whether students receive appropriate accompaniment.[^4] [^8] [^9]

Research also reminds us that Junyi's effects cannot be summarized in a single sentence like "using it will improve." Liu Yu-hong's 2018 action research, targeting 32 eighth-grade students at Zhongshan Elementary School in New Taipei City, found that after introducing Junyi with differentiated instruction, the standard deviation of test scores gradually decreased, and students' learning processes showed a trend from rough to refined. However, this was an action research conducted in a specific class over a specific period, and cannot be directly generalized to all schools.[^10]

Another master's thesis completed in 2020, involving 7 fifth-grade students receiving learning support, conducted three weeks and 12 lessons of integer arithmetic instruction. The study found that students had positive attitudes toward the platform and were willing to continue using it, but pre- and post-test scores did not show statistically significant improvement.[^11] This result is worth preserving: it separates "liking the tool" from "learning effectiveness," and reminds us that the platform must be evaluated together with teaching design, time length, and teacher judgment. Junyi's official website collects feedback from a student at Keelung Jian-de Junior High School: "Without using Junyi, my learning was more difficult. After using it, I feel Junyi helps me learn, and I also recommend it to my classmates—not just for myself, but also for others."[^1] This statement can illustrate the help felt by users, but cannot alone replace effectiveness research.

## "Free" solved the access problem, but did not eliminate all gaps

### A set of numbers should be broken down into at least four questions

The 5.289 million cumulative registered users, 271,000 monthly active users, and 4,828 deep-use teachers listed in Junyi's public data cannot be interpreted under the same denominator. Cumulative registration answers "how many people have created accounts," monthly active users are closer to "how many people are still using recently," and deep-use teachers point to "the extent to which the platform is integrated into the curriculum process." These three describe different levels of reach and use, and cannot be simply added together, nor can registration numbers be treated as learning outcomes.[^2] [^3]

| Indicator                   | Can explain                                                                     | Cannot alone prove                                           |
| --------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------ |
| Cumulative registered users | How many people have created a usage entry                                      | Whether each account continues learning or completes courses |
| Monthly active users        | The scale of those still using during a specific period                         | Whether users truly understand the learning content          |
| Deep-use teachers           | The extent to which teachers integrate the platform into the curriculum process | Whether all students gain the same learning effectiveness    |
| Learning research results   | Observations under specific samples, periods, and teaching designs              | Can be directly generalized to all schools nationwide        |

This breakdown also makes "impact" more honest. Public welfare platforms need to show scale to explain how resources are used. At the same time, they must also preserve research methods and limitations, avoiding mixing reach, activity, teacher adoption, and score changes into a seemingly precise but actually inexplicable total.

Junyi's free use lowered the threshold for families to purchase learning materials and tutoring resources. The Ministry of Economic Affairs' Social Innovation Platform data shows that Junyi operates mainly through free platforms, teacher training, and data analysis to provide personalized instruction. The 2024 public results listed a cumulative 5.289 million registered users, 271,000 monthly active users, and 4,828 deep-use teachers.[^3] [^7]

But "having an account" does not equal "having the conditions to use it." Children still need stable internet, available devices, quiet time, and an adult who can help when stuck. Even within the same city, families provide different amounts of after-school time and equipment. Rural schools may simultaneously face limitations in staffing, equipment, internet, and transportation. A report by _CommonWealth Magazine_ placed this issue in the context of the 2021 pandemic, pointing out that online resources can support school suspension, but educational inequality may also be amplified by the pandemic.[^4]

Another challenge for Junyi is outcome attribution. Student progress may come from platform content, or from teachers changing their teaching methods, parents increasing companionship, schools improving equipment, or the students themselves being more willing to practice during that period. This is also why academic research must explain samples, periods, and methods, while the platform's own annual report should clearly distinguish registration, activity, deep use, and actual learning results.

There is no simple conclusion here. Junyi does provide reusable free resources, and some research and on-site cases show improvements in learning processes. But it cannot alone replace teachers, schools, and families, nor can it reduce structural educational gaps to a problem solvable by a single app.

## From "watching videos" to "learning how to learn"

### The platform's daily life is not just a row of videos

Junyi's interface breaks learning into videos, exercises, assignments, badges, and progress data. This design has a practical benefit: students can leave behind reviewable traces in a single learning session, and teachers can identify concepts that need reinforcement from class data. The official homepage separates students, teachers, and parents into different access points, listing assignment tasks, error collections, analysis reports, and class data as main services.[^1]

But this design also brings new responsibilities. The more detailed the learning records, the more teachers need to understand the boundaries of the data. A single incomplete assignment may represent unfamiliar concepts, or may simply be due to equipment, time, or family environment issues. Data can hint at problems, but cannot alone explain why a child did not complete them. This is the most easily overlooked aspect of digital learning: while visible records increase, the real reasons still require people to ask questions.

### Extending from mathematics to AI and literacy content

In 2024, Junyi's public data indicated that content extends from elementary school to high school, from mathematics to English, and launched three AI tools. The official homepage also lists collaborative or self-produced content such as literacy animations, financial literacy, semiconductors, and sustainable food and clothing.[^1] [^3]

This content expansion reflects that Taiwan's schools' expectations of the platform have changed. The early question was "is there a video that can supplement mathematics," but now it includes whether students can understand science, finance, technology, and sustainability issues in daily life. If Junyi wants to maintain its original intention of educational equity, it must balance content quality, curriculum alignment, and whether teachers can actually use it while expanding topics.

This expansion makes Junyi no longer just "online supplementary materials," but an attempt to become a learning infrastructure connecting students, teachers, parents, and partners. For students, more content means more access points. For teachers, it means needing clearer curriculum maps, task hierarchies, and methods for interpreting data. The larger the platform, the more it cannot just leave the responsibility to students to figure out on their own, nor can it assume every teacher already has the same digital teaching conditions. But the success of infrastructure is not only about how many people it can accommodate, but also whether it gives people on the ground more breathing room: whether teachers can quickly find students who need help, whether children can start from their own pace without being labeled, and whether parents can understand the data instead of being overwhelmed by more notifications.

Junyi's earliest question was "can a child get good learning materials," and today's question is closer to "after getting the materials, who accompanies them through the learning journey." This is the watershed that the platform must face: content can be mass-reproduced, but understanding and accompaniment still require people. This is also where the platform must continue to answer in the future: how to connect free content, personalized data, and real human support, so that digital tools give time back to education, rather than adding another system that needs maintenance.

> **✦** "Free" is Junyi's entry point, not the endpoint of educational equity.

In 2012, Junyi started from a Chinese-localized teaching video. In 2021, it became a temporary support for many families and classrooms during school suspension. Today, the platform faces not whether it can put content online, but whether children can find a path worth continuing between content, teachers, and life.

If one day Junyi no longer needs to use "rural areas" to prove its own necessity, perhaps it is not because rural problems have disappeared, but because every child has already been treated as a learner with their own pace. The website is still just a website. What is truly returned to children is the right to begin, retry, and continue learning.

This is also the ultimate criterion worth preserving: Junyi is not used to prove that technology can replace teachers, but to test whether technology can return teachers' time to understanding students, designing curriculum, and accompanying learning. When the platform's data, the classroom's judgment, and the student's initiative connect with each other, digital tools may transform from a content repository into a part of educational relationships.
