> **30-Second Overview:** After 1951, Taiwan's newspapers were long limited to about 31. On January 1, 1988, the press ban was lifted, changing from a licensing system to a registration system; newspaper counts, printing locations, and new paper applications were no longer constrained by the same regulations. By the end of 1990, registered newspapers once reached 313, but free competition also brought new problems such as promotional wars, ownership concentration, and journalistic responsibility. This article truly asks: who took over the public sphere after political control left?

On January 1, 1988, Taiwan lifted the press ban. On that day, newspapers were no longer restricted by count, and new publishing houses did not need to first obtain a rare license.[^1]

What is more compelling than writing this history as "freedom from then on" is the scene of a market—once having only 31 legal papers—suddenly facing hundreds who wanted to enter.[^2]

## A Newspaper First Needed Several Permits

Taiwan in the early post-war period did not start without newspapers. After 1945, both private and party/military-affiliated papers appeared. _Min Bao_ (民報) and _Renmin Daobao_ (人民導報) also criticized the government, becoming channels for the public to obtain information and express dissatisfaction. This brief openness was quickly interrupted by the 228 Incident and subsequent purges.[^3]

In 1949, the government formulated the "Regulations on News, Magazines, and Books during the Martial Law Period in Taiwan Province." These regulations implemented the martial law system into the daily management of newspapers, magazines, and books, becoming a concrete legal basis for publishing control. National archives records show that it was indeed used to manage news and publications.[^4]

From the 1950s onward, media control gradually formed a set of routine rules. The licensing restrictions made it difficult for new papers to establish; page limits restricted how many could be printed per issue; printing restrictions tied newspapers to registered printing locations; and paper quotas constrained supply and print volume. Whether a newspaper could be published depended not only on the editorial desk but also on whether it held those administrative permits.[^5]

This is where the "press ban" is most easily overlooked. It did not always require someone to cut out an article daily. As long as new papers were prevented from entering, existing papers dared not cross lines, and paper, pages, and printing plants became administrative issues, the news market would shrink by itself.

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The most effective control under authoritarianism is not always deleting a sentence, but ensuring that only a few people are allowed to speak.

## 31 Was Not a Natural Number

From 1951 to 1988, the Taiwanese media industry was long maintained at around 31 papers. This number appears to be a market preference, but it is actually the result of systemic screening. Researcher Lin Li-yun pointed out that the structure of the media before the press ban lift was jointly influenced by long-term authoritarian rule, protection by regulators, and the resource accumulation of existing newspapers.[^6]

In this market, _United Daily News_ (聯合報) and _China Times_ (中國時報) became major private papers not just because their content was loved by readers, but also because they possessed capital, distribution networks, and operational experience during the press ban era. The system that restricted newcomers turned the advantages of early players into decades of compound interest.

The predecessor of _United Daily News_ was a joint edition published in 1951 by _Quanmin Daily_, _Minzu Bao_, and _Economic Times_. After officially renaming in 1957, it expanded its readership with social news and commentary, exceeding 75,000 copies distributed in 1958.[^3]

The predecessor of _China Times_ was _Zhenxin News_, founded in 1950. It started with economic news and commodity prices before transitioning into a comprehensive paper, building its own readership through supplements, culture pages, and current affairs commentary.[^3]

The history of these two papers shows that the press ban was not just about "fewer newspapers." It also reallocated who could accumulate readers, who could retain journalists, who could buy paper, and who had the capacity to expand on the first day of the lifting of the ban.

## Lifting the Ban Was Not Flipping a Switch

After martial law was lifted on July 15, 1987, lifting the press ban became the next political and administrative challenge. Before the press ban was opened in 1988, the News Bureau invited newspapers, scholars, and representatives of public opinion to discuss issues such as font size, paper, price, and printing locations. The policy choices at that time were to first dismantle the licensing system and then let the media compete in the market, with many details left to subsequent administrative and market adjustments.[^7]

The 2008 symposium on twenty years of lifting the press ban preserved memories from the former Director of the News Bureau and media workers. Speakers mentioned that after liberalization, the shift from "one paper, one license, one printing plant, three categories, twelve pages" to a registration system saw the gradual removal of restrictions on newspaper count, number of printing plants, and selling price.[^7]

This turning point was like suddenly opening a long-closed station. People first saw the crowds, not the new transportation rules.

This timeline shows that lifting the press ban was not a "liberalization switch" flipped instantaneously. Administrative restrictions were eased first, leading to a rapid increase in registered newspapers, followed by the redistribution of printing, distribution, advertising, and talent markets. A historical review of the English _Taipei Times_ noted that 95 new papers were established in 1988, increasing the newspaper count from 31 to 126 by the end of the year. This increase was not merely an addition of titles; it also drove the expansion of pages and advertising. The article cited research showing that _China Times_' advertising revenue increased by about 63% in 1988, indicating that post-ban competition quickly transformed into industrial competition to capture readers, ads, and distribution channels.[^11]

By the end of 1990, Taiwan had 313 registered newspapers, but only about 50 to 60 were actually published daily. These two figures are not contradictory; the former is the registered scale, while the latter is the market where papers truly reached readers every day. Lifting the press ban did indeed increase voices, but it also forced every paper to fight for survival within limited readership, advertising, and distribution channels.[^3]

The first few new newspapers that appeared were often established by existing media organizations or people familiar with the industry. The first batch of papers after the lifting of the ban included _Zili Morning News_ (自立早報), _United Evening News_ (聯合晚報), and _China Times Evening News_ (中時晚報), reflecting that even though the new system allowed entry, newspapers remained a capital-intensive industry requiring printing equipment, distribution networks, and advertising clients.[^11]

> **📝 Curator's Note:** The first side effect of freedom is often not chaos, but everyone realizing they must compete.

## From Political Control to Market Control

Before the newspapers were opened, public discourse in Taiwan did not completely disappear; it merely shifted outside of newspapers. After the 1970s, opposition magazines took on the role of breaking news taboos. They discussed democratic politics, Taiwanese history, local society, and human rights issues, forming an underground stream of thought that conveyed views without relying on official newspaper registration.[^12]

This history is important because it shows that there was already a demand for discourse in society. After 1988, these demands finally obtained relatively legitimate, stable, and sustainable publishing channels. The experience of opposition magazines also offers another reminder: media freedom depends not only on whether regulations allow founding a paper but also on whether editors, journalists, and readers can withstand censorship, economic pressure, and political retaliation.[^12]

Only after lifting the press ban did the question move from "can we publish?" to "who can sustain publishing long-term?"

The first obvious change brought by lifting the press ban was that new papers could enter the market at a faster pace. _United Daily News_ and _China Times_ both launched evening editions in 1988, which began using more vibrant layouts and horizontal formats, with other newspapers following suit.[^3]

Next came promotional wars. Newspapers gave away gold, cars, and houses; readers were not just choosing which news to read, but also comparing which paper could bring home a bigger gift. _Liberty Times_ (自由時報) entered the metropolitan market in the 1990s with free distribution and low-price strategies, showing that newcomers did not have to rely on traditional media industry paths to expand.[^3]

This competition made newspapers thicker, faster, and more eye-catching, while also exposing another problem. The design of the system for lifting the press ban mainly dealt with "who could publish," but it failed to address ownership concentration, political interference by operators, journalistic labor rights, and media responsibility simultaneously. Lin Li-yun's research therefore described the lifting of the press ban as a "historical experiment": the old structure did not disappear; it was merely placed in an environment of free competition to continue transforming.[^6]

After the abolition of the _Publishing Act_ in 1999, the old publishing control framework further exited. The space for press freedom expanded, but journalistic professionalism, responsibility, and market norms did not automatically grow. Records from the 2008 symposium repeatedly mentioned that media no longer only faced official control but also faced control from owners, advertisers, and the market.[^7]

## What Freedom Left Behind, What the Market Took Away

In its 1999 review of Taiwanese press freedom, _Guanghua_ (光華) regarded the lifting of the press ban as an important milestone toward media freedom, while also pointing out that publications in the early post-martial law period were still constrained by political ideology and publishing regulations. This reminds us that 1988 was only a starting point; laws, media, and society all needed to rewrite the rules.[^8]

A press release from the Presidential Office in 2008 recorded this history from another angle: after January 1, 1988, newspapers were no longer restricted by page count, and Taiwanese papers quickly appeared with dozens or even hundreds of pages. This "thickening" was both a visible result of liberalization and a pressure for the industry to maintain competition with more content and advertising.[^9]

The English version of _Guanghua_'s review twenty years later placed this history within the international context of press freedom that readers could understand. Its value is not in drawing conclusions for Taiwan, but in reminding readers: the lifting of the press ban was a systemic transformation event, not just an industry news story.[^10]

Today, print newspapers are no longer the only entry point for most people to get news. The disappearance of newspapers, the rise of online platforms, and the concentration of media ownership have pushed the issues to the next stage. The diverse voices sought after when the press ban was lifted in 1988 now require asking not just "can it be published," but also "who has the resources to be seen," "who can conduct long-term verification," and "who is willing to take responsibility for mistakes."

Therefore, 1988 did not directly lead Taiwan into enlightenment. It handed over a public sphere that was originally allocated by the government to more people, and it also handed it over to the market. Only after handing it over did everyone begin to face the real difficulty: how can freedom be prevented from being monopolized again by new powers?
