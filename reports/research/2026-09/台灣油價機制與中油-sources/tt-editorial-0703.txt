 CPC was smart to freeze prices
 By Wei Chi-Hung 魏季宏
 Add TT as Preferred Source
 This week, Taiwan welcomed domestic fuel price cuts with the cost of gasoline dropping by NT$1 (US$0.03) per liter. Prior to this, state-run CPC Corp, Taiwan had frozen prices for 11 weeks as part of its efforts to absorb the costs of high global fuel prices since the US-Israel war against Iran broke out in late February. 
In a world beleaguered by fraught geopolitics and inflation, Taiwan has had considerable success in maintaining stable prices and social order. This is no accident. The government has demonstrated genuinely smart decisionmaking in energy policy and support for peoples’ livelihoods. 
Many see the price freeze as just another subsidy, or have otherwise written it off as a cheap bid for votes, but if we zoom out and look at things from a macroeconomic perspective, the NT$17.7 billion that CPC has absorbed to date has helped to shield Taiwan’s entire economy. 
CPC sits upstream of virtually all other companies. Once oil prices rise, the impact is felt across the supply chain from transport costs and raw materials to prices at street-side snack stalls. 
The government’s strategy has been to position the firewall for inflation right at the gate. 
 If oil prices had been allowed to soar with the international market, imagine the level of inflation that would have ensued. 
It is likely that the amount the government would have had to invest in poverty relief and economic stimulus to address the fallout would have been several times greater than CPC’s bill. This is not to mention the task of assuaging public panic over rising prices. 
Instead, the government’s dual-price stabilization mechanism for gasoline and diesel has successfully kept fuel prices among the lowest in neighboring Asian markets, and checked inflation at safe levels. This has helped to protect ordinary people’s livelihoods, and given businesses across sector lines a stable environment in which to develop. 
This week, with global oil prices making their way back down, CPC has responded to the market shift with its own price drops. It shows that the policy mechanism, far from being rigid as some had feared, can flex just as any mature piece of governance policy addressing real-world challenges should. 
After the 11-week-long price freeze, it is worth appreciating this conscious decision by policymakers for what it is. Over the past few months, Taiwan’s economic performance and price stability has been an object of envy thanks to targeted measures that have put the defense of people’s livelihoods first. For this, the government should be commended. 
Wei Chi-hung is president of the Taiwan International Cultural and Educational Public Interest Association.
